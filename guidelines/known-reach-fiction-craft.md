# Fiction craft for Tales of The Known Reach

Use this guide when planning, drafting, continuing or reviewing Known Reach fiction. It adapts techniques from [wgwtest/novel-writing](https://github.com/wgwtest/novel-writing/tree/838729695148008d42c01bd7c3c0c1a5c15de830) for our military science fiction, grimdark, space horror and related stories. The upstream project's copyright and MIT license are retained in [its attribution and license](../third-party/novel-writing/LICENSE). The [source and adaptation record](../sources/novel-writing-craft-adaptation-2026-11-05.json) identifies the materials consulted.

## Start with the story and the owner's direction

- Read [README](../README.md), [the novel index](../novel/INDEX.md), the actual relevant scenes, and the applicable dossiers in [the reference index](../reference/INDEX.md). For an existing scene, read its full text and enough neighboring material to understand the transition. Search names and aliases; do not assume a dossier includes everything.
- Honor any spoiler cutoff. Track what the reader may know separately from what each character knows at that moment. Label facts, reasonable inferences, unanswered questions and proposed additions distinctly. Do not turn a side story's proposed placement into established main-novel canon.
- The owner's current request and [standing novel-writing guidelines](novel-writing-guidelines.md) control. Keep the existing one-scene-at-a-time format, approximately 1,000 words of prose and exactly four combinable continuation options for new main-novel scenes, unless the owner requests otherwise. For a new standalone side story, use at most 6,000 words of story prose, no internal scene captions/headings/number labels, and no continuation options, unless the owner gives a different instruction for that story. A title is allowed. Do not impose main-novel formatting on a review or an unrelated task.
- When the owner asks for review, give an assessment and specific suggestions first; do not silently revise source prose. Preserve accepted story versions, and sync user-added or accepted stories under [the GitHub-sync guideline](novel-github-sync-v01.md), subject to private, draft-only and hold instructions.

## Make the scene move through choices

Privately identify what each scene needs to change. Give the people involved a concrete immediate problem, a reason they act now and options constrained by what they know, possess and can reach. A useful check is: what is noticed, what is believed, who can decide, what is attempted, what follows, and what remains unresolved? It is a diagnostic, not a mandatory order or a formula for every paragraph. Mystery, silence, misjudgment and an intentionally unresolved scene can all work.

Separate the viewpoint character from the person with authority or expertise. A wounded marine may notice a casualty, the medic may decide what treatment is possible, and the captain may decide whether the ship can hold position. Let knowledgeable supporting characters make consequential choices; the central hero need not solve every problem.

Show enough geography and timing for the reader to follow action: where the ship or people are, relevant exits, line of sight, reach, equipment, communications, travel time and hazards. Tie a new problem to visible evidence or a plausible report. In established fictional technology, respect known limits; where a mechanism is unspecified, do not invent a definitive rule merely to solve the immediate scene.

## Dialogue, knowledge and distinct voices

Characters speak to particular listeners for a reason. Their rank, loyalties, trust, training and goals shape what they disclose, withhold or misunderstand. A commander can receive an uncertain sensor report without instantly knowing its cause; distinguish observation, testimony, inference and confirmed knowledge. An enemy's private motives are not automatically available to the heroes or reader.

Give important arrivals enough grounding for the current scene: who the person is, their relationship to the situation and a memorable impression. Anchor the reader without pausing the story for a dossier.

Let exchanges affect the next choice, pressure or relationship when that matters. A hand moved to seal an airlock, a withheld access code or a delayed order can change a conversation. Do not add an interchangeable gesture to every line: fast dialogue and meaningful silence can stand alone. Let professionals use shared knowledge naturally rather than explaining their own jobs to one another for the reader.

Retain the House's established voices and relationships, including disciplined rivalry, professional competence and the Charter's protections. Other factions, crews and civilians need their own interests and agency. The standing voice descriptions are starting points, not catchphrases to repeat in every exchange.

## Grimdark, horror and space

Choose the intensity that fits the owner's brief and the scene. Darkness can come from scarcity, institutional decisions, fear, loss or compromised choices; it does not require constant cruelty, hopelessness or graphic detail. Courage, loyalty, humor, compassion and hard-earned success remain available when the story supports them. Do not make the House or another established faction act against its values solely to make a scene darker.

For horror, connect the unknown to a specific character's immediate problem: a missing reply, a compromised seal, a familiar voice where no one should be. Control what can be observed and when; let uncertainty change behavior before explaining the threat. Use sensory details suited to the environment and viewpoint. Vary suspense, discovery, danger and aftermath instead of keeping every paragraph at maximum intensity. Graphic violence is a creative choice, not a requirement.

In spacecraft and stations, show how a problem travels through physical systems and people: compartments and pressure boundaries, oxygen and power, access, sensors, communication delays, repair capacity, navigation and the threat to crew or civilians. Use established setting rules and mark new technology as proposed. Plausibility serves readable drama; avoid unsupported real-world claims and unnecessary technical lectures.

For battles, make objectives, orders, opposing responses and consequences understandable through the viewpoint. Track which ship acts, who commands it, what can be seen or verified, what is damaged and how the change affects later choices. Do not turn suffering into a scoreboard or erase losses after a dramatic victory.

## Protect the author's voice during revision

Keep purposeful short paragraphs, repetition, dialogue, dark humor, quiet emotional beats and concrete sensory details where they carry rhythm or character. Vary sentence and paragraph length where the scene benefits; do not eliminate repetition mechanically. Preserve plot, outcomes, emotional intent and deliberate ambiguity. Correct clear confusion with the smallest useful change; propose larger changes instead of silently making them. Do not turn vivid action or dialogue into a summary just because the summary is shorter.

When reviewing, prioritize consequential problems before copyediting. For each meaningful finding give the location, what happens, why it matters and a practical suggestion. Cite the actual chapter/scene/file or the supplied passage. Show a short illustrative revision only if useful and clearly label it as a proposal. If something is a taste choice rather than an error, say so. If evidence is missing or a real-world claim is uncertain, say what needs checking.

## Quick private checks

1. What does this scene change, and who has the authority and ability to act?
2. What can this viewpoint actually perceive, infer and know here?
3. Can a reader follow where people and ships are and why the next action is possible?
4. Does dialogue carry individual goals and make use of meaningful responses or silence?
5. Do atmosphere, danger and consequences fit the requested tone without overwriting established character or canon?
6. Have I preserved the owner's expressive choices and separated suggestions from accepted prose?

These are tools for judgment. They are not an outline to insert into the finished story, and they do not require every scene to contain a battle, horror reveal or moral lesson.
