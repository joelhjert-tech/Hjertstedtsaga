# Dock Fees

The warship asked for the first available berth, priority repairs and the immediate removal of every civilian vessel from its approach. Tamsin Korr, superintendent of Tern Station, looked through the observation glass at the three civilian vessels already bolted to her docks. One was unloading medical filters. One had a child aboard who needed those filters. The third belonged to Old Pell, and its engines had been in pieces for eleven days. She pressed the transmit key. “Your first available berth is berth four. Your priority is behind a medical delivery. And if you want the other ships moved, you may explain it to their owners yourself.”

The captain on her screen wore a dark uniform with the insignia of the Directorate of Ash. His expression suggested that he had expected her to recognize it. “Superintendent, my ship has suffered damage. I require your yard.”

“Then we have something in common,” Tamsin said. “My yard has suffered your ship arriving without notice. Shut down your weapons, transmit your damage report, and I will tell you what we can do.” She ended the call before he could tell her how important he was. Outside, the vessel hung beyond the station's approach lights, its long hull scarred near the stern. It was too far away for her to make out more than the dark plating and one pale line along its underside. The captain had not fired, and his ship was holding position. For now, that was enough.

“You could have been gentler,” said Ivo, her chief mechanic, from the hatch.

“I was gentle. I gave him a berth.” Tamsin glanced at the manifest on her desk. Tern was an independent repair station on a busy freight route, with four berths, three tugs and a crew small enough that everybody knew who had failed to put a tool back. They serviced haulers and passenger ships, not Directorate warships. “Get me a look at that stern before anyone attaches a hose.”

The examination came from Sella, a tug pilot who had once threatened to charge Tamsin for breathing her air. Her tug circled the warship at a careful distance while its crew watched from their gun positions. “I see a ruptured pipe,” she reported. “It's venting a fine mist. Looks like the port-side radiator return.” She sent the image across. Specks glittered against the black, drifting toward Tern on the ship's slow approach. “That's coming our way.”

Ivo studied the image and called up the berth's air-handling diagram. “If those particles get into our intake, they could foul the filters. Maybe worse, depending on what's in that coolant.” The medical filters in berth one had not yet reached the station's clinic, and the clinic's current supply was already close to exhaustion. “We need to know what it is before they get any closer.”

Tamsin called the warship back. This time a woman in an engineer's coveralls appeared beside the captain. “Your stern is leaking,” Tamsin said. “We need the coolant specification and a way to stop the flow. Until then, stay outside the approach boundary.”

“Our systems are classified,” the captain said.

The engineer looked past him, toward a display Tamsin could not see. “Captain, if the return line has opened to vacuum, the reserve tank will feed it until the isolation valve closes. We tried the valve from the bridge.”

“Did it close?” Tamsin asked.

“The indicator says yes. The pressure says no.” The engineer introduced herself as Tessa Rell. “I can give your mechanic the coolant's hazard sheet. I need your word that the rest of the system stays off your records.”

“I need the hazard sheet and enough of the layout to keep my people alive,” Tamsin replied. “You can stand beside Ivo while he reads it.” The captain objected, but Rell was already transmitting. The coolant was toxic and left a sticky residue when exposed to vacuum. The station's filters could catch some of it; they could not be trusted to catch all of it. Tamsin ordered the exposed intake shut and switched the clinic to its reserve supply. A timer appeared above her desk: forty-six minutes before the reserve would need replacing.

Sella's tug returned with more images. The damaged pipe ran beneath an armored panel, and the panel had folded over the manual valve. “I can hold the ship steady while somebody cuts through,” she said. “But I'm not putting my tug in that cloud without a clean route out.”

“Nor am I sending a mechanic into it,” Tamsin said. She asked Rell what was upstream of the leak. Rell hesitated, then marked a coupling on the schematic. It was accessible from a service recess near the stern, well clear of the cloud, but removing it would dump the contents of the line. “We catch that in a sealed bag,” Ivo said. “Then fit a blank over the open end. It won't repair your radiator, but it should stop the tank feeding the leak.”

“Should?” the captain asked.

“If your drawing is right and the upstream valve holds,” Ivo said. “We test the pressure before anyone uncouples it.” Rell asked to join the work. The captain refused at first, pointing out that she was his chief engineer. Rell pointed out that she knew which of the ship's valves were prone to lie. He gave permission, then insisted on sending two armed guards with her. Tamsin allowed them to remain at the berth entrance. Their weapons would stay secured.

Sella brought the tug alongside the service recess, using its handling arm to brace against a marked point on the hull. Ivo and Rell crossed on safety lines, carrying a sealed collection bag and the blanking plate between them. From the control room, Tamsin watched their suit cameras and the station's intake readings. The captain watched on a separate channel. The pressure test showed that the upstream valve held. Ivo fitted the bag around the coupling, and Rell loosened the first fastener. A warning flashed across her display. The pressure behind the valve was rising.

“Bridge,” Rell said, “who restarted the coolant pump?” There was a pause before someone answered. A junior officer had seen the temperature climbing and followed the standard emergency procedure. Rell told him to stop the pump and lock out its controls. She did not shout. When the pressure fell, she waited a full minute and checked it again before touching the coupling. The crew on Tern's control deck stayed silent with her. Tamsin watched the reserve timer pass twenty-one minutes.

The fasteners came free. Coolant surged into the bag, which swelled against its retaining straps, then settled. Rell held the open end steady while Ivo fitted the plate. He tightened the last bolt, checked the seal and backed away. The mist at the stern thinned, but it did not stop. Something was still escaping from the broken section. Sella rotated her tug to bring its lights across the underside, and Rell found a second fracture farther aft. The loose coolant in that section was draining into space; the tank pressure, however, remained steady.

“How long?” Tamsin asked.

“I can't promise,” Rell said. “Not until we measure how much is left in the line.” She ran the calculation with Ivo, while Sella kept the tug between the drifting cloud and the service recess. They estimated another ten minutes of leakage. The station could not wait for it to clear on its own: the clinic reserve had fourteen minutes left, and a replacement cylinder would take longer than that to install.

Sella had a proposal. Her tug carried a broad capture screen for handling loose cargo, a flexible sheet normally used to keep damaged packages from spilling into a berth. She could put it between the fracture and the station while another tug pulled the leaking ship farther out. She would need a clear channel and a precise course; the screen was not a sail she wanted to wrap around her own engines. Rell checked the warship's remaining pressure and agreed. The captain ordered his crew to prepare for towing, then stopped himself. “Superintendent,” he said, “I request a tow.”

“Granted,” Tamsin replied. “It's on the invoice.”

The second tug took the warship under tow. Sella paid out the screen and held it open with her handling arm, keeping its edge away from the damaged hull. The particles struck the sheet and collected in dull patches. Not all of them; the station's sensors still showed traces drifting past. But the concentration at the intake fell, and the distance increased with every careful meter. Tamsin kept the intake closed until the reading had remained below Ivo's stated limit for three consecutive checks. Then she ordered fresh air through the reserve filters. The clinic called a minute later. The child was stable, and the new filters were on their way down the corridor.

It took another hour to secure the leak and bring the warship into berth four. Rell supervised her crew as they isolated the damaged line, and Ivo checked the work from Tern's side of the connection. The captain signed the repair agreement after reading every line. He paused at the charge for the capture screen, which Sella had declared unfit for further cargo work. “That is expensive,” he said.

“It was a good screen,” Sella replied. She stood beside Tamsin with a streak of sealant on one sleeve and the satisfied expression of someone presenting a long-overdue bill. “You can have it if you like. We packed it in the same sealed container as your coolant.”

The captain signed. His people paid for the screen, the tow, the replacement filters and the hours Tern's crew had spent keeping his ship away from their intake. They paid the docking fee as well. Before returning to her vessel, Rell stopped at the clinic door and asked whether the child would recover. The medic told her the treatment was working. Rell nodded, thanked her and went back to work. Tamsin watched her leave, then called down to the repair bay to ask whether anyone had remembered to feed Ivo. The answer was no. She added two meals to the station's account and sent them down with the next parts trolley.
