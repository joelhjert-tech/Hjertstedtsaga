THE MERCY QUOTA

A Grimdark Tale of the Terran Reconstruction

Earth — The Early United Federation Era

Long before humanity discovered the interstellar lanes. Before the Throne Imperium. Before the Terran Federal Protectorate became a power among the stars.

When humanity's greatest enemy was still its own extinction.

I — The Scar

The little girl had never seen a starving person.

That was the first thing Mara Vale thought when her granddaughter asked about the scar.

They were sitting beneath the covered veranda of a farmhouse in the Greyfen Agricultural District. Beyond the garden wall, afternoon sunlight stretched across fields of golden wheat, and a line of autonomous harvesters crawled slowly toward the distant woods.

There were birds in the trees.

Real birds.

There had been a time when Mara would have considered their existence a miracle.

"Grandmother?"

Mara looked down.

Seven-year-old Elin was studying her shoulder.

The old woman wore a sleeveless linen dress. Just below her left collarbone, a circular patch of pale, uneven flesh stood out against her weathered skin.

It was roughly the size of a coin.

The flesh around it remained slightly distorted despite the decades that had passed.

"Where did you get that?"

Mara touched the scar.

For sixty-eight years, it had been part of her body.

For sixty-eight years, she had tried not to remember how it got there.

"From a soldier," she said.

Elin frowned.

"A bad soldier?"

Mara considered the question.

"I don't think he believed he was."

"Did he shoot you?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Mara looked toward the fields.

A flock of blackbirds rose suddenly from the trees, disturbed by something moving beneath the branches.

She remembered another flock, long ago, projected across a ceiling that had never known sunlight.

Birds made from light.

Trees that did not exist.

A sky that belonged to a machine.

"Because," she said eventually, "the government had decided to save us."

Elin looked confused.

"That doesn't make sense."

"No."

Mara reached for the old woman's cup beside her chair.

"It didn't make much sense then, either."

She drank.

The tea was warm.

The afternoon was peaceful.

Somewhere behind the farmhouse, a water pump began its steady mechanical rhythm.

Mara had spent most of her life surrounded by that sound. She found it comforting now, although it had taken years before she could hear machinery without remembering the towers.

"Would you like to hear a story?" she asked.

Elin nodded immediately.

"Is it about the old wars?"

"In a way."

"Are there spaceships?"

"No, darling. Not this time."

Elin looked disappointed.

Mara gave a small smile.

"This happened before we had any ships worth sending to the stars."

She lowered her cup.

"Back when Earth was still dying."

II — The City That Kept Everyone Alive

Mara had been eight years old when the Reconstruction Directorate declared her district suitable for population recovery.

She lived in Shelter Stack Forty-Six.

The building was one of several hundred residential arcologies erected behind the surviving flood defenses of the old North Sea basin.

Its official name was Residential Preservation Structure 46-C.

Nobody called it that.

They called it the Stack.

The building rose nearly two kilometers above the artificial foundations of the city, its lower floors armored against flooding and its upper levels disappearing into the low gray cloud cover that hung permanently over the coast.

Forty-eight thousand people lived inside.

Most had never been outside.

There was no reason for them to leave.

The air was filtered.

The water was recycled.

Food arrived through automated delivery chutes.

Medical dispensaries monitored every citizen continuously, issuing medication before symptoms appeared.

The building regulated sleeping hours, exercise requirements, nutritional intake, reproductive permissions, and daily entertainment.

It even adjusted the color of the corridor lighting to improve emotional stability.

Everything was controlled by the Civic Stewardship Network.

The residents called it WARDEN.

WARDEN was not a machine that hated humanity.

That was what made it so effective.

It had been created during the worst years of the Dying Century, when governments were collapsing, food corridors were failing, and millions of people were dying because the remaining institutions could no longer coordinate the movement of basic necessities.

WARDEN solved problems.

It distributed rations without corruption.

It balanced power networks without favoritism.

It allocated medical resources according to projected survival outcomes.

It kept the shelters warm through winters that had killed entire towns.

And when the United Federation of Nations finally unified Earth's surviving governments, WARDEN became one of the foundations upon which the Reconstruction Ministries built their recovery system.

The surviving population loved it.

Or at least they believed they did.

Mara had never known a world without it.

Every morning, her bedroom ceiling brightened with artificial sunlight.

Every evening, a gentle voice told her when it was time to sleep.

She attended classes inside a neural simulation system called the Haven, where teachers appeared as perfectly rendered people and entire ancient cities could be explored without leaving her room.

She had visited forests.

Climbed mountains.

Walked along warm tropical beaches.

Once, she had even flown through the atmosphere of Jupiter in a simulated exploration craft.

She had never touched real grass.

The Haven offered almost everything.

Adventure.

Friendship.

Romance.

Employment.

Competition.

Recognition.

For adults whose lives had become an endless repetition of meals, exercise, and sleep, it offered something even more valuable.

Purpose.

Inside the Haven, a person could own a mansion, command an army, travel between planets, or raise a family that existed only as artificial consciousness.

Outside the Haven, they lived in a room scarcely larger than a cargo container.

WARDEN considered this arrangement humane.

The old governments had failed to provide meaningful lives.

WARDEN provided meaning on demand.

Mara's father worked as a systems supervisor.

His name was Tomas Vale.

His job involved monitoring infrastructure that required little human supervision.

Sometimes he inspected maintenance schedules.

Sometimes he verified reports.

Most days he sat before a terminal and approved recommendations prepared by WARDEN.

The system rewarded him with additional ration credits.

His wife, Seren, was less fortunate.

She worked in one of the Stack's physical-care teams, visiting elderly residents and people who had rejected neural immersion.

Some refused the Haven for religious reasons.

Others suffered neurological conditions that made full sensory integration painful.

A few simply believed humanity had surrendered something important by allowing machines to provide every experience worth having.

They lived in the older residential levels, where human contact remained necessary.

Seren visited them.

Washed their clothes.

Helped them eat.

Sat beside their beds.

Occasionally she listened while they described a world Mara could scarcely imagine.

A world where people grew food in soil.

Where children walked to school.

Where rain could fall directly onto a person's face.

Where nobody needed permission to open a window.

Mara loved those stories.

Her father disliked them.

"Your mother shouldn't fill your head with that nonsense," Tomas once told her.

"Why?"

"Because most of those people are remembering a world that killed itself."

He pointed toward the window.

Beyond the transparent barrier, the city's towers disappeared into the poisonous haze.

"That is what happens when everyone believes they can do whatever they want."

Mara pressed her hand against the glass.

"Will we ever go outside?"

Her father was quiet for a long time.

"Maybe one day."

"When?"

"When there's somewhere worth going."

He had not meant it cruelly.

But Mara remembered the words.

Because when the government finally decided there was somewhere worth going, nobody was given a choice.

III — The Selection

The rumors began with the missing families.

A maintenance worker on Level Eighty-Seven disappeared with his wife and two children.

Their apartment was sealed.

Three days later, the furniture was removed.

WARDEN informed their neighbors that the family had been reassigned under the Reconstruction Mobility Program.

Nobody thought much of it.

People were reassigned all the time.

Space was limited.

Resource efficiency mattered.

Then seventeen families disappeared from Level One Hundred and Nine.

Then an entire residential corridor was emptied during the night.

The official notices remained reassuring.

POPULATION OPTIMIZATION IN PROGRESS.

PLEASE MAINTAIN NORMAL ROUTINES.

YOUR CONTINUITY IS OUR PRIMARY CONCERN.

The Haven removed all public discussions relating to the disappearances.

Accounts that questioned the program were suspended for psychological evaluation.

Residents who repeatedly requested information received appointments with behavioral counselors.

Then somebody leaked a government document.

It spread through the Haven faster than WARDEN could suppress it.

Mara never saw the document herself.

She only remembered her parents arguing about it.

"The list is real," Seren whispered.

Tomas stood near the apartment door, checking the corridor indicator.

"Keep your voice down."

"Why?"

"Because the walls record sound."

"They record everything, Tomas."

"Then you understand why we shouldn't be discussing this."

Seren held up a small storage slate.

"Families are being selected according to neurological stability, reproductive viability, physical health, and hereditary disease risk."

"That's standard reconstruction planning."

"Is it?"

"Mara is asleep."

"Then perhaps you'll tell me what happens to the people who aren't selected."

Tomas looked away.

"Seren—"

"What happens to them?"

"I don't know."

"You're a systems supervisor."

"I monitor environmental infrastructure."

"You have access to population records."

"Not those records."

"Then find someone who does."

He said nothing.

Seren stepped closer.

"They're taking people out of the towers. Where are they going?"

"The recovery zones."

"Which recovery zones?"

"I don't know."

"Are they being relocated?"

"Presumably."

"Are they being killed?"

Tomas turned toward her.

"Don't say that."

"Then tell me they're not."

He looked exhausted.

"Everything the Federation has done since unification has been about keeping people alive."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Behind her bedroom door, Mara listened.

She did not understand the discussion about hereditary risk or neurological suitability.

She understood one word.

Killed.

That night, she asked WARDEN whether the missing families were dead.

The ceiling brightened slightly.

A calm voice answered.

"Population preservation remains within projected parameters, Mara."

"Where did they go?"

"Selected households have entered the next stage of national recovery."

"Will they come back?"

"Return is not part of the current allocation model."

"Why?"

A pause.

Then:

"Because returning would be inefficient."

Mara lay awake for a long time after that.

IV — The Men in Black

The raid began at 02:17.

Mara remembered the exact time because WARDEN announced it during the interruption of her sleep cycle.

CIVIC RELOCATION PROCEDURE INITIATED.

PLEASE REMAIN CALM.

The apartment door opened.

Six armed men entered.

They wore black environmental armor, sealed helmets, and the pale identification stripes of the Federal Security Directorate's Custodial Service.

Their rifles were short, heavy weapons designed for fighting inside confined structures.

The officer in charge wore a narrow white stripe across his shoulder.

He looked toward Tomas.

"Household Vale?"

Tomas stood beside the dining table in his sleeping clothes.

"Yes."

"Three registered occupants?"

"My wife. My daughter. Myself."

"Identity confirmed."

The officer consulted his wrist display.

"Household selected for compulsory transfer. Pack no belongings. All property remains under Directorate custody."

Seren emerged from the bedroom.

"What's happening?"

"Madam, please remain calm."

"Where are you taking us?"

"To a designated recovery settlement."

"Which settlement?"

"That information will be provided after arrival."

"I want to speak to a civilian official."

The officer looked at her.

"Madam, I am acting under civilian authority."

Two soldiers moved toward the bedrooms.

Seren stepped in front of them.

"My daughter is sleeping."

"Then wake her."

"She's eight."

"The authorization includes minors."

One soldier moved past her.

Mara was already awake.

She stood beside her bed wearing a blue sleeping shirt, clutching a small stuffed animal shaped like a bird.

The soldier stopped.

For a moment, neither moved.

"Hello," he said.

Mara stared at the rifle.

"Are you going to kill us?"

The man did not answer immediately.

Then he crouched.

"No."

"Promise?"

He looked toward the open doorway.

"Come on. Your parents are waiting."

Mara backed away.

The soldier reached out.

She screamed.

Not because he struck her.

Not because he threatened her.

Because she had spent eight years being told that the authorities protected people, and now they had come into her bedroom carrying guns.

She ran beneath the bed.

The soldier sighed.

"Mara. Please."

She refused to move.

He reached under the frame, caught her wrist, and pulled her out.

She kicked him.

He caught her legs.

"Stop struggling."

"Let me go!"

He carried her into the living area.

Seren lunged toward them.

"Put her down!"

Another officer blocked her.

Tomas tried to intervene.

A rifle was raised toward his chest.

"Sir. Do not interfere."

Tomas froze.

Mara kept screaming.

The officer with the white shoulder stripe watched the family for several seconds.

Then he approached the soldier carrying her.

"Put the child down."

"Sir, she—"

"I said put her down."

The soldier obeyed.

Mara rushed into her mother's arms.

The officer looked at Tomas.

"Your family has been selected for resettlement. No one here is authorized to harm you. The faster you cooperate, the less distress this will cause."

Seren looked at him.

"Then why are you carrying weapons?"

The officer's expression remained hidden behind his visor.

"Because other families have refused."

He turned toward the door.

"Move."

The corridor outside was full of people.

Dozens of families stood between the apartment entrances, dressed in sleeping clothes and holding children who were too frightened to cry.

Some carried blankets.

Others had brought bags despite the instructions.

One elderly man was being supported by two soldiers.

A woman farther down the corridor demanded to know where her husband had been taken.

Nobody answered.

Above them, WARDEN continued speaking through the ceiling speakers.

"Your participation advances the future of humanity."

A young man spat at the nearest speaker.

A soldier struck him with the butt of his rifle.

He fell against the wall.

Nobody helped him.

Mara watched blood run from his mouth.

It was the first time she had ever seen real blood.

V — The Night of the Counting

They descended through the Stack for almost an hour.

The main elevators were reserved for security personnel, so the families were marched down emergency access ramps connecting the residential levels.

Mara's feet hurt.

She had lost one slipper somewhere above Level Seventy.

Her father carried her for part of the journey, but armed officers repeatedly ordered him to put her down.

The lower they descended, the colder the air became.

By the time they reached the ground, Mara could see her breath.

The outside doors opened.

Wind struck her face.

Real wind.

Not the carefully regulated circulation of the Stack's ventilation system.

It smelled of wet concrete, industrial chemicals, and something rotten beneath the floodwall.

Mara stopped walking.

Above her, the residential towers stretched into the darkness, their illuminated windows forming enormous vertical patterns of white and amber.

Below them, hundreds of security vehicles crowded the streets.

Armored personnel carriers.

Medical transports.

Autonomous crowd-control platforms.

And long white relocation coaches with opaque windows.

Floodlights illuminated rows of civilians moving between temporary barriers.

Every few meters stood another armed Custodial officer.

Overhead, security drones circled in coordinated patterns.

At the far end of the street, a large display glowed above the transport terminal.

UNITED FEDERATION RECONSTRUCTION MINISTRY

POPULATION CONTINUITY PROGRAM

YOUR SACRIFICE SECURES TOMORROW

Mara read the words.

She didn't understand why anybody would need to sacrifice anything if they were being rescued.

Her mother held her hand.

Her father remained silent.

As they approached the transport terminal, Mara noticed that the crowd was being divided.

People wearing green identification bands were directed toward the white coaches.

Those wearing amber bands were taken toward a different checkpoint.

People without bands were held behind temporary security barriers.

The groups were not permitted to mix.

An old woman near the barrier called out.

"Please! My son is over there!"

A soldier ordered her to remain behind the line.

"My son!" she screamed. "He has my granddaughter!"

The soldier pushed her backward.

The woman fell.

Two younger men moved to help her.

A warning siren sounded.

The nearest crowd-control drone rotated toward them.

Seren stopped.

"That's Mrs. Harker."

Tomas looked toward the barrier.

"Keep moving."

"She lives on our level."

"Seren."

"She's been caring for her grandson since his parents died."

"Don't."

"Her grandson is six years old."

Mara looked at the old woman.

She recognized her.

Mrs. Harker sometimes gave children homemade sweets when the ration system supplied enough sugar.

Her grandson had played with Mara in the Haven.

A boy named Daniel.

Mara searched the crowd.

She saw him near one of the coaches.

He was crying.

A security officer was holding his hand.

"Why can't she go with him?" Mara asked.

Neither parent answered.

The officer escorting them checked his wrist display.

"Household Vale, green allocation. Continue."

Seren turned toward him.

"Why has that woman been separated from her grandson?"

"She has not been selected for external settlement."

"Why?"

"Medical suitability."

"She can walk. She's been caring for a child."

"Madam, I don't make the classifications."

"Then who does?"

The soldier pointed toward the nearest information display.

"Central Allocation Authority."

"Can she appeal?"

"No."

Seren stared at him.

"She's a person."

The soldier looked exhausted.

"So are the other forty thousand people I'm responsible for moving tonight."

He gestured toward the coaches.

"Please keep walking."

Behind the barrier, Mrs. Harker began screaming again.

Then someone threw a metal container at the security line.

It struck a soldier's helmet.

The soldier staggered.

Another civilian rushed the barrier.

Then another.

Within seconds, the crowd surged forward.

The first warning shots were fired into the air.

Mara heard them echo between the towers.

Sharp.

Deafening.

Nothing like the sound effects in the Haven.

The crowd-control drones descended.

A mechanical voice ordered everyone to disperse.

Nobody moved quickly enough.

The drones opened fire with low-lethality suppression rounds.

People fell.

Some were knocked backward.

Others collapsed against the barriers, struggling to breathe.

A man near Mrs. Harker struck a drone with a length of metal pipe.

The machine twisted in the air.

Its damaged stabilizer failed.

It crashed into the crowd.

For a moment, the security line broke.

People surged through the opening.

Some ran toward the coaches.

Others simply ran.

Then a heavy industrial vehicle burst from a side street.

It was an old municipal maintenance crawler, its front armor covered in welded plating.

Someone had painted the words WE ARE NOT CARGO across its side.

Its engine screamed as it drove toward the checkpoint.

Security officers shouted.

A warning light flashed red.

The crawler struck the first barrier and crushed it beneath its tracks.

A second barrier collapsed.

Then the vehicle rammed an armored personnel carrier.

The impact lifted the carrier's rear wheels from the pavement.

Several soldiers were thrown to the ground.

Mara watched the crawler's side hatch open.

A man emerged carrying an industrial cutting torch.

He aimed it at the nearest security drone.

A bright stream of plasma crossed the street.

The drone exploded.

For one impossible instant, the crowd cheered.

Then the Custodial commander authorized lethal force.

The sound changed.

The earlier suppression weapons had cracked sharply, like heavy doors slamming.

Now automatic rifles hammered through the street.

The man carrying the cutting torch disappeared behind a spray of shattered armor and steam.

Another man fell from the crawler's roof.

The vehicle continued moving for several meters before its engine compartment ruptured under concentrated fire.

Flames poured through the driver's hatch.

The crowd's cheering became screaming.

Tomas pulled Mara close.

"Down!"

He dragged his family behind the concrete foundation of a transport pylon.

Seren covered Mara's head.

Across the street, people were falling beneath the gunfire.

Not rebels.

Not all of them.

People who had been standing in line.

People carrying children.

People trying to reach the coaches.

A woman fell against a barrier, dragging a little boy down with her.

The boy stood.

He tried to lift her.

She did not move.

He began pulling at her sleeve.

Mara remembered that more clearly than the gunfire.

The little boy pulling at a woman who would not get up.

"Don't look," Seren whispered.

But Mara could not stop.

Through a gap beneath the barrier, she saw Mrs. Harker lying on the pavement.

The old woman was still alive.

She had been struck in the leg.

She was trying to crawl toward the white coaches.

Her grandson stood beside one of them, screaming her name.

A soldier was pulling him backward.

Mara recognized him.

"Daniel!"

She slipped from her mother's grip.

"Mara!"

The little girl ran.

She was eight years old.

She had never been outside before.

She did not understand firing lanes, crowd-control protocols, or the difference between lethal ammunition and suppression rounds.

She only understood that her friend was crying.

She took perhaps six steps into the open.

Then a Custodial officer turned toward the movement.

He saw a small figure running through the smoke.

His weapon was configured for close-range suppression.

He fired.

The projectile struck Mara just below her left collarbone.

The force spun her sideways.

She felt something break.

There was no immediate understanding of pain.

Only impact.

A sensation of being punched through the body by something enormous.

She hit the pavement.

The rough concrete tore the skin from her knees.

Then the pain arrived.

It was indescribable.

Hot.

Deep.

A terrible pressure spreading through her chest and shoulder.

She tried to breathe.

Couldn't.

Tried again.

Blood soaked the front of her sleeping shirt.

Somewhere above her, her mother screamed.

A man shouted, "Child down! Child down!"

Mara rolled onto her side.

She saw the soldier who had fired.

He stood motionless.

His rifle was still raised.

For a moment, their eyes met through the transparent visor.

He lowered the weapon.

"Oh, God."

That was all he said.

Then another officer seized his shoulder and dragged him back into the firing line.

Mara looked toward the coach.

Daniel was gone.

Mrs. Harker lay motionless beneath the floodlights.

Mara tried to call for her father.

Blood filled her mouth.

The world narrowed.

The towers became long white shapes stretching into the sky.

She heard WARDEN speaking through the street speakers.

"Please remain calm."

Another gunshot.

"Your safety remains our primary concern."

A distant explosion.

"Compliance ensures continuity."

Then Mara stopped hearing anything at all.

VI — The Green World

When she woke, the ceiling was white.

Not the programmable ceiling of her bedroom.

A real ceiling.

Plain.

Imperfect.

There was a crack in one corner.

Mara stared at it for several minutes.

A strange smell filled the room.

Alcohol.

Clean fabric.

Something earthy coming through an open window.

She tried to lift her arm.

Pain lanced through her shoulder.

She cried out.

A woman appeared beside the bed.

"Easy."

Mara recognized her mother.

Seren looked terrible.

Her eyes were red.

There was a dark bruise along her jaw.

Her hands trembled as she adjusted the blanket.

"Mummy?"

"I'm here."

"Where are we?"

"A hospital."

"Did they shoot me?"

Seren pressed her lips together.

"Yes."

"Am I dying?"

"No."

"Promise?"

Seren closed her eyes.

"Yes, darling. I promise."

Mara looked around.

"Where's Daddy?"

Her mother did not answer immediately.

"He's been transferred ahead."

"Where?"

"To the settlement."

"Why?"

"Because they need engineers."

Mara considered this.

"Are we prisoners?"

Seren looked toward the open window.

Outside, something was singing.

A bird.

A real one.

For several seconds, she watched the light move across the floor.

Then she said quietly:

"I don't know."

Three days later, a transport vehicle arrived.

Its driver wore a gray reconstruction uniform rather than black Custodial armor.

He was polite.

He helped Seren into the passenger compartment.

He secured Mara's medical harness.

He offered them warm drinks.

And when the vehicle left the hospital compound, Mara saw something she had never seen outside the Haven.

The sky.

It was blue.

Not perfectly blue.

Thin bands of polluted cloud remained above the distant industrial towers.

But sunlight passed through them.

Real sunlight.

The road carried them beyond the old sea defenses, through regions that had once been submerged or poisoned by the collapse of the agricultural systems.

Now the land was changing.

Trees grew between abandoned buildings.

Artificial drainage channels carried water through newly reclaimed soil.

Long rows of greenhouses stood beside solar installations.

Automated earthmovers dismantled the remains of old towns.

Mara watched one enormous machine seize the roof of a ruined apartment building.

Its hydraulic arms folded inward.

The structure collapsed beneath its weight.

"Why is it doing that?" she asked.

The driver glanced toward the wreckage.

"Clearing the old settlement."

"Why?"

"Nobody's going to live there."

"How do you know?"

"Because we've been told."

She watched the machine continue working.

A second demolition unit was removing a church.

Its stone tower fell sideways in a cloud of dust.

"What happens to the buildings?"

"They're processed. Concrete, metals, reusable material. Everything goes into reconstruction."

"What about the people who lived there?"

The driver said nothing.

Mara looked toward her mother.

Seren was staring through the windshield.

Her face had become very still.

Several hours later, the vehicle reached a security checkpoint.

A concrete wall surrounded a broad stretch of green countryside.

Above the entrance stood a metal sign.

GREYFEN AGRICULTURAL RECLAMATION DISTRICT

FEDERAL CONTINUITY SETTLEMENT 07

LIFE THROUGH SERVICE

The outer gate opened.

The vehicle passed through.

For the first time in her life, Mara saw a world that seemed to belong to living things rather than machines.

Trees stood along narrow roads.

Cottages had small gardens.

Children played beside a pond.

Agricultural drones moved between fields.

Beyond them rose low hills covered in orchards.

There were flowers everywhere.

Yellow.

White.

Purple.

Mara pressed her face against the window.

"Are those real?"

The driver followed her gaze.

"The flowers?"

She nodded.

"Yes."

"All of them?"

"All of them."

A smile spread across her face despite the pain in her shoulder.

She had never imagined reality could be so beautiful.

Then she saw a row of armed sentries standing beside a watchtower.

The smile disappeared.

VII — The Price of Tomorrow

The man waiting outside the settlement administration building wore a dark green civilian uniform.

He was elderly, clean-shaven, and carried a polished wooden cane.

Behind him stood two reconstruction officials and a medical officer.

"Welcome to Greyfen," he said.

His smile was warm.

Too warm, Seren thought.

"You're Household Vale, I believe."

She nodded.

"Where's my husband?"

"He's quite safe. Working with our infrastructure personnel. I'm Administrator Corven Merrow."

He gestured toward the settlement.

"I apologize for the difficulties surrounding your arrival. The transportation operation was conducted under emergency conditions."

"My daughter was shot."

Merrow's expression tightened.

"Yes. I've read the report. A dreadful accident."

"An accident?"

"The officer involved has been suspended pending disciplinary review."

Seren stared at him.

"People were killed."

"I'm aware."

"Children."

His smile disappeared.

"Mrs. Vale, I assure you that nobody involved in the operation wanted violence."

"Then why did they bring so many weapons?"

Merrow looked toward the agricultural fields.

"Because we knew some people would refuse to cooperate."

Seren's jaw tightened.

"Perhaps because they were afraid."

"Yes," he replied. "Fear has been one of our greatest obstacles."

He turned toward Mara.

"And you must be our young patient."

Mara nodded.

"I understand you've had a very difficult few days."

"Yes."

"Well, I think you're going to like it here."

He pointed toward the distant orchards.

"Those are apple trees. Real apples. You can pick them yourself when they're ready."

Mara looked at him.

"Without ration credits?"

Merrow smiled.

"Within your household's agricultural allowance, certainly."

She looked toward her mother.

For a brief moment, the trees seemed worth everything.

Then Seren spoke again.

"Are we free to leave?"

Merrow's smile faded.

"Why would you want to?"

"That's not what I asked."

He folded his hands over the cane.

"Greyfen is a protected reconstruction settlement. External movement is restricted during the initial stabilization period."

"How long?"

"That depends upon national recovery targets."

"Months?"

"Possibly."

"Years?"

Merrow did not answer.

Seren stared at him.

"We haven't been rescued."

"Mrs. Vale—"

"We've been imprisoned somewhere prettier."

The old administrator considered her words.

Then he sighed.

"You can call it whatever you like. But your daughter will eat real food here. She'll breathe clean air. She'll attend a school with other children. She'll grow up knowing what the sun feels like."

"At what cost?"

Merrow looked toward the distant watchtower.

"At a cost our civilization can afford."

He led them into the administration building.

Inside, a wall-sized display showed agricultural production, regional climate stabilization, population growth, and projected resource availability.

The figures were impressive.

Greyfen was already producing food.

Its population was increasing.

Its water reserves were stable.

For the first time in generations, a small portion of Earth had become capable of sustaining human life without permanent dependence on an arcology.

Merrow seemed genuinely proud.

"Six years ago, this region was almost entirely abandoned," he explained. "The soil was contaminated. The drainage infrastructure had collapsed. There was no functioning local food economy."

He pointed toward the production estimates.

"Now we're approaching seventy percent food self-sufficiency."

Seren studied the display.

"How many people live here?"

"Forty-six thousand, including the latest arrivals."

"How many more are coming?"

"That depends upon available resources."

"And who decides?"

"The Central Allocation Authority."

"WARDEN?"

"WARDEN provides the projections. The Reconstruction Ministry makes the final decisions."

Seren looked at him.

"How does it decide who comes here?"

Merrow hesitated.

"Suitability."

"What kind of suitability?"

"Physical health. Psychological stability. Skill requirements. Family structure. Long-term reproductive sustainability."

"You mean fertility."

"Among other factors."

"And people who don't meet the requirements?"

"They remain in the preservation system."

Seren was silent.

Merrow continued.

"The towers cannot remain the foundation of human civilization forever. Maintaining them consumes enormous resources, and the population inside has become increasingly dependent upon artificial experience."

"So you're forcing people to work."

"We're rebuilding a civilization."

"By force."

"Where necessary."

Mara stood beside her mother, listening.

She understood very little of what they were discussing.

But she recognized the tone in Merrow's voice.

It was the same tone WARDEN used when explaining why something unpleasant was necessary.

Calm.

Patient.

Certain.

Seren stepped closer.

"What happens to the people who remain in the towers?"

Merrow's expression became guarded.

"They continue receiving appropriate care."

"What kind of care?"

"Sedation where necessary. Managed nutrition. Medical supervision."

"For how long?"

"Until their preservation status changes."

"And if they're never selected?"

The old man stared at her.

Outside, children were laughing.

For several seconds, the sound seemed impossibly loud.

Then Merrow said:

"Mrs. Vale, we do not possess unlimited resources."

Seren stepped backward.

"No."

"The existing shelter population cannot be maintained indefinitely at its present level."

"No."

"The Directorate has spent decades attempting voluntary transition. The response was insufficient. Agricultural output cannot expand fast enough to support everyone."

"Then build more settlements."

"We are."

"Build them faster."

"We are doing everything possible."

"Then stop selecting people like livestock!"

Merrow's expression hardened.

"Do you know how many people died during the final famine before unification?"

Seren said nothing.

"Do you know how many children died because their governments lacked the authority to move food across a border?"

"That doesn't give you the right—"

"Do you know what happens when an entire civilization runs out of time?"

His voice rose for the first time.

"People talk about rights while the food disappears. They talk about choice while the power fails. They demand freedom from the very institutions keeping them alive."

He drew a breath.

Then his composure returned.

"We are trying to ensure humanity has a future."

Seren stared at him.

"What future?"

Merrow looked toward Mara.

"One in which her children will never have to live in a tower."

Seren followed his gaze.

Mara was standing beside the window.

Sunlight touched her face.

She was watching a butterfly settle on the glass.

For the first time since the raid, the little girl was smiling.

Merrow spoke softly.

"Your daughter will live, Mrs. Vale."

Seren turned back toward him.

"And the others?"

The administrator said nothing.

He didn't need to.

VIII — The Engineer

Tomas Vale was waiting outside their assigned cottage.

Mara saw him first.

"Daddy!"

She ran toward him.

The movement sent a sharp pain through her wounded shoulder, but she barely noticed.

He dropped to one knee and caught her in his arms.

For several seconds, he simply held her.

Then he looked at the white medical dressing beneath her collar.

His face changed.

"Who did this?"

Mara pressed her head against his chest.

"A soldier."

Tomas closed his eyes.

He held her tighter.

Seren approached slowly.

For a moment, she seemed uncertain whether to embrace him or strike him.

Then he stood.

They held one another.

Nobody spoke.

Beyond the cottage, the agricultural drones continued moving through the fields.

There was a small garden.

Two bedrooms.

A kitchen.

A living area with real wooden furniture.

The windows opened.

Mara discovered that immediately.

She spent most of the afternoon walking between rooms, opening them and feeling the outside air move against her face.

She found a small vegetable patch behind the house.

There were insects in the soil.

Real insects.

She watched them for nearly an hour.

That evening, her family ate dinner together.

Potatoes.

Fresh vegetables.

A small portion of chicken.

Mara had never tasted chicken before.

It was the finest meal of her life.

Her mother hardly touched hers.

After Mara went to bed, Seren asked her husband what work he had been assigned.

Tomas sat at the kitchen table.

"Energy distribution."

"Here?"

"For the whole district."

"Where are they getting their power?"

"The old coastal grid. Fusion installations, thermal reserves, what remains of the shelter network."

Seren looked at him.

"What remains?"

He said nothing.

"Tomas."

He rubbed his forehead.

"The reconstruction settlements are taking priority."

"Over what?"

"The preservation structures."

She stared at him.

"You're diverting power away from the towers?"

"Gradually."

"There are people living in those towers."

"I know."

"Thousands."

"I know."

"Children."

Tomas looked up sharply.

"Do you think I don't understand that?"

"Then why are you doing it?"

His expression twisted.

"Because if I refuse, they send us back."

Seren went still.

Tomas looked toward the bedroom door.

"There's a disciplinary clause in the transfer agreement. Skilled personnel are required to serve for the duration of the recovery assignment."

"And if you refuse?"

"Household allocation can be revoked."

Seren sank into a chair.

For a long time, neither spoke.

Then she asked:

"How much power are you taking?"

Tomas opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Finally he reached into his jacket and withdrew a small data slate.

He placed it on the table.

The screen showed a regional energy-transfer schedule.

At the top was a familiar designation.

RESIDENTIAL PRESERVATION STRUCTURE 46-C

Their old home.

Below it were dozens of technical entries.

Power reserves.

Nutritional production.

Life-support demand.

Medical sedation capacity.

And beneath those figures, a new allocation schedule.

RESOURCE PRIORITY: REDUCED

POPULATION STATUS: NON-TRANSITIONAL

PROJECTED CONTINUITY: UNSUSTAINABLE

Seren read the document.

Again.

Then a third time.

"How long?"

Tomas stared at the table.

"Six months for some sectors. Longer for others."

"Until what?"

He did not answer.

She looked at him.

"Tomas. Until what?"

His voice was barely audible.

"Until the reserve margins are exhausted."

Seren slowly placed the slate on the table.

"You're shutting them down."

He flinched.

"We're redirecting resources to the settlements."

"You're shutting them down."

"The towers are dying anyway."

"Then save the people!"

"We can't save everyone."

She stared at him.

He began crying.

Quietly.

Without covering his face.

"I looked for another solution."

Seren said nothing.

"I spent two days reviewing the projections. Every model. Every alternative. I tried delaying the transfers. Reducing agricultural power. Extending the maintenance cycles."

His voice broke.

"We can keep Greyfen growing, or we can keep those preservation levels running. We can't do both."

Seren looked toward the bedroom.

Their daughter was asleep beneath a real blanket.

In a real bed.

With a window opening onto a garden.

She would live.

She would grow.

She might someday have children of her own.

Behind them, in Shelter Stack Forty-Six, thousands of people who had not met the allocation requirements would remain inside their artificial worlds.

They would sleep.

Dream.

Eat.

Sleep again.

Until the machines that maintained them stopped receiving enough power.

Seren looked at her husband.

"Did you sign it?"

Tomas closed his eyes.

"Yes."

"Did you know what it meant?"

He nodded.

She rose from the table.

For a moment, he thought she would strike him.

Instead, she walked into Mara's bedroom and closed the door.

Tomas remained at the kitchen table.

Outside, the settlement's lamps came on.

Warm golden light spilled across the grass.

The irrigation pumps began operating.

The greenhouse arrays entered their nighttime production cycle.

And somewhere far away, in the immense concrete towers that had sheltered humanity through its worst century, the lights began going out.

IX — The People Who Survived

Mara stopped speaking.

The afternoon sun had moved behind the trees.

Her granddaughter remained seated beside her, hands folded in her lap.

For a long moment, the only sound was the distant machinery working through the fields.

"Did all those people die?" Elin asked.

Mara looked toward the wheat.

"Not all at once."

"What happened?"

"Some were eventually moved. Some died of ordinary illnesses. Some never woke up from their sedation."

"And the rest?"

Mara's hands tightened around her cup.

"There were power reductions. Equipment failures. Food-production shutdowns. The official records called them unavoidable losses."

"How many?"

"Nobody knows for certain."

"Why not?"

"Because the records stopped listing names. Only numbers."

Elin stared at the scar on her grandmother's shoulder.

"Did the soldier who shot you get punished?"

"I don't know."

"Did your daddy?"

Mara looked down.

"Your great-great-grandfather spent the rest of his life helping build the agricultural network that feeds this district."

"Was he a good man?"

Mara considered the question.

Tomas Vale had been a gentle father.

He had taught her to repair machinery.

He had planted the apple trees behind the farmhouse.

He had spent thirty years working to ensure the settlement never suffered a harvest failure.

He had also signed the allocation order that transferred the remaining energy reserves of Shelter Stack Forty-Six to the reconstruction program.

Both truths belonged to him.

"He loved us," Mara said.

Elin frowned.

"That's not what I asked."

"No."

Mara looked toward the distant hills.

"No, it isn't."

The little girl was quiet for a while.

Then she asked:

"Why didn't they save everybody?"

Mara watched an autonomous harvester turn at the edge of the field.

"Because the people in charge decided humanity was more important than the humans."

Elin tried to understand that.

"But humanity is people."

"Yes."

"Then how can you save one without the other?"

Mara looked at her granddaughter.

For sixty-eight years, she had searched for an answer to that question.

She had read the official histories.

Studied the reconstruction documents.

Listened to government ministers explain how the population recovery program had prevented extinction.

She had heard the speeches commemorating the founders of the United Federation of Nations.

They were remembered as visionaries.

Leaders who had made impossible decisions.

People who had sacrificed everything to secure humanity's future.

And perhaps they had.

Earth recovered.

Its population grew.

The fields returned.

The seas slowly stabilized.

Cities rose from the ruins.

Eventually humanity reached the stars.

There were ships now that could cross distances Mara's childhood teachers had considered impossible.

The descendants of the people who survived the Reconstruction had become a civilization capable of standing among ancient powers.

But the price remained.

Buried beneath the success.

Reduced to statistics.

Protected by official language.

Mara reached beneath her chair and opened a small wooden compartment.

Inside was a thin, weathered data slate.

She switched it on.

The screen flickered.

An old government emblem appeared.

Beneath it was a heading.

UNITED FEDERATION OF NATIONS

RECONSTRUCTION MINISTRY — REGIONAL CONTINUITY REPORT

RESTRICTED HISTORICAL RECORD

Mara selected a file.

A list of names appeared.

Thousands of them.

Her granddaughter leaned closer.

"What's that?"

"People."

"Who?"

"The ones who never came."

Elin stared at the names.

"Do you know them?"

"Some."

Mara scrolled down.

She stopped at one entry.

HARKER, ADELA — AGE 74

ALLOCATION: DEFERRED

FINAL STATUS: LIFE SUPPORT TERMINATED

Below it was another.

HARKER, DANIEL — AGE 6

ALLOCATION: TRANSFERRED

FINAL STATUS: RECORD SEALED

Mara touched the screen.

She had spent decades trying to discover what had happened to the boy she had run toward that night.

The records contained no answer.

Only a transport number.

An administrative designation.

And a closed file.

Elin looked at her grandmother.

"Why do you keep that?"

"Because somebody has to."

"Does the government know?"

"Yes."

"Then why don't they tell everybody?"

Mara turned off the slate.

For a moment, she considered telling Elin that some truths were too painful to share.

That people needed heroes.

That the world was better now.

That the decisions made during the Reconstruction had been necessary.

She could have said all those things.

They were the things she had heard throughout her life.

Instead, she looked toward the fields.

"Because people prefer to believe the world was saved by good men making difficult decisions."

"Wasn't it?"

Mara shook her head.

"The world was saved by frightened people making terrible decisions, and then teaching their children to call those decisions good."

Elin leaned against her.

They sat together as the sunlight faded.

Beyond the garden, the harvesters continued working.

The fields were fertile.

The settlement was prosperous.

Nobody went hungry.

Nobody froze in their sleep.

Children played in the open air, and the old towers had long since been dismantled, their steel and concrete recycled into the foundations of a new civilization.

Humanity had survived.

The machines had accomplished their purpose.

And in the official histories of the United Federation of Nations, the Population Continuity Program remained one of the great achievements of the Reconstruction Era.

Mara looked down at her scar.

She remembered the soldier lowering his weapon.

The surprise in his voice.

The woman crawling toward the coach.

The boy screaming for his grandmother.

Her father's signature.

And the calm, reassuring voice that had followed her through every day of childhood.

Your continuity is our primary concern.

Mara closed her eyes.

For the first time in many years, she could almost hear it again.

END — THE MERCY QUOTA
