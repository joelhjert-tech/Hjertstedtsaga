# THE LAST PASSENGER OF VEYR'S CROSSING

A Tale from the Known Reach

The first thing Commander Elias Vorn noticed was that the ship had no business being there.

The second was that someone aboard it was still paying for electricity.

He stood behind the tactical station on the bridge of the House von Hjertstedt reconnaissance frigate HVS Skymning, watching the sensor returns resolve into something that looked increasingly like an old passenger transport. Beyond the forward observation screens, the Veyr's Crossing relay hung against a field of distant stars, its ancient Varanthi structure broken in three places and surrounded by the debris of centuries. The relay had been abandoned long before Elias was born. Its authentication systems were considered unreliable, its surrounding lanes were absent from modern navigation libraries, and the nearest inhabited settlement lay more than six days away through a chain of poorly maintained approaches. Nobody came here unless they were lost, desperate, or searching for something the rest of the Known Reach had forgotten. Elias had spent nineteen years in House service learning that all three possibilities usually ended badly.

"Contact bearing zero-eight-four, elevation minus twelve," Lieutenant Sera Kald reported from the sensor station. "Range eighty-three thousand kilometers. Passive thermal return confirmed. I'm getting reactor emissions, Commander."

Elias leaned closer to the display. "From the relay?"

"Negative. Separate contact. Large civilian hull, approximately nine hundred meters. Minimal maneuvering emissions. No active communications." Sera adjusted her filters, and a rough silhouette appeared on the central tactical display. The vessel had a long, narrow passenger spine surrounded by cylindrical habitation sections, with four engine nacelles arranged around its stern. Much of its exterior plating was dark, but several windows showed faint internal illumination. "She's old," Sera continued. "Very old. I'm not finding a current registry match."

Captain Mara Teld, commanding officer of the Skymning, rose from her chair and joined them. She was a compact woman in her late forties, with close-cropped dark hair and a mechanical left hand that clicked softly whenever she flexed its fingers. The prosthetic had been installed after a boarding action in the Pale Corridor, and she had refused every offer to replace it with something more sophisticated. She liked machinery that made noise when it worked. "Run the historical registries," she ordered. "Civilian passenger traffic, old imperial routes, pre-collapse salvage records. Everything we have." Sera nodded and began the search. Elias studied the contact's thermal profile, noting that the vessel's reactor output was remarkably stable for something apparently abandoned. "Captain, there's something else. She's holding atmosphere throughout most of the habitation spine." Mara glanced toward him. "Life support?" Elias nodded. "Active. Environmental control, gravity systems, internal power distribution. Either someone's aboard, or this ship has been maintaining itself for a very long time."

The Skymning had not come to Veyr's Crossing looking for survivors. Its assignment was a routine reconnaissance sweep of several neglected relay approaches after House intelligence detected unauthorized authentication attempts in the region. Three merchant vessels had disappeared during the previous month, although none had been confirmed destroyed. The House suspected independent raiders using obsolete navigation keys to move through abandoned corridors, a practice that had become increasingly common as frontier security deteriorated. Captain Teld had been ordered to investigate the relay, document its condition, and return without provoking unnecessary trouble. Her frigate carried eighty-six personnel, including a small boarding detachment, a Justicar, and enough technical specialists to examine the relay's damaged systems. It was a capable vessel, but it was not equipped to evacuate thousands of passengers from a crippled transport. Elias was considering that problem when Sera's search returned a match.

"Historical identification confirmed," she announced, her voice losing some of its earlier confidence. "Passenger transport Mourning Star. Registered to the Veyr Transit Cooperative. Last authenticated departure seventy-three years ago."

The bridge became quiet.

Mara looked toward the display. "Seventy-three?"

"Yes, Captain. She departed the commercial anchorage at Tarsen Hollow carrying twelve thousand, four hundred and eighteen registered passengers and two hundred and ninety-three crew. Destination was the old Veyr's Crossing transfer station. The vessel failed to arrive, and the investigation was closed after eleven years without confirmed wreckage." Sera hesitated before continuing. "The cooperative went bankrupt. Passenger compensation was never paid. Most of the families were declared legally extinct or transferred to debt settlement under the old transit regulations."

Elias stared at the vessel's silhouette. Twelve thousand people. Seventy-three years. He had seen abandoned transports before, and the discovery of an old missing ship was not impossible in a region filled with forgotten lanes and unreliable navigation records. What troubled him was the power signature. The Mourning Star was not drifting cold through the wreckage of an old relay. Her reactors were running, her environmental systems were maintaining pressure, and the faint thermal patterns along her habitation sections suggested that internal machinery was still operating. A ship could survive decades in the dark if its systems were sufficiently automated and its fuel reserves remained intact, but maintaining a passenger vessel for seventy-three years without resupply was another matter entirely. He turned toward Mara. "Captain, I recommend we hold at Whisper Range until we've established a complete sensor picture. If someone has been using that ship as a concealed base, I don't want to discover it after we're alongside."

Mara nodded. "Agreed. Deploy witness drones. Passive observation only. Weapons remain cold unless we identify a threat." She returned to her command chair and opened a channel to the engineering compartment. "Chief, I want a full assessment of that transport's reactor profile. Tell me whether the numbers make sense." A tired voice answered almost immediately. "Understood, Captain. Although if you're asking whether a seventy-year-old passenger ship should still be running, the answer is probably no." Mara allowed herself a faint smile. "Then find out why this one is."

The Skymning reduced thrust and began deploying its reconnaissance drones. Twelve small machines emerged from recessed launch bays along the frigate's hull, spreading into a loose observation pattern around the distant transport. Their passive sensors began collecting thermal, electromagnetic, and structural information while the ship's tactical systems compared the returns against historical records. The process took time. Old civilian vessels often carried modifications that never appeared in official registries, and the Mourning Star had clearly undergone substantial maintenance during her operational life. Several sections of the hull had been replaced with mismatched armor panels, while the passenger spine showed evidence of repairs performed using industrial materials rather than standard commercial components. Elias watched the growing model and noticed that some of the replacement sections appeared newer than the original structure. He enlarged the image, examining a broad band of plating along the port habitation cylinder. The metal was scarred and discolored, but its manufacturing pattern was different from the surrounding hull.

"Captain, someone repaired her."

Mara turned toward him. "When?"

"I can't tell yet. The plating doesn't match the original construction, and the weld patterns are unusual. It could be old salvage work, but I'd like a closer look." He highlighted the section on the display. "There are at least six major repairs along the passenger spine. Some of them look like complete compartment replacements."

Mara studied the image. "Could be survivors. They might have kept her running."

"Possibly."

"Or?"

Elias hesitated. "Or someone found her and decided she was worth keeping."

The distinction mattered. A passenger transport with surviving crew was a rescue operation. A vessel being maintained by unknown personnel in an abandoned relay system could be a pirate anchorage, an illegal research facility, or something considerably worse. The Known Reach contained enough forgotten infrastructure that entire communities could disappear into the gaps between authenticated routes. Some survived through legitimate salvage and trade. Others endured by preying on whatever unfortunate travelers crossed their paths. House patrols had discovered stations where civilians were sold as labor, medical facilities where prisoners were used for experimentation, and abandoned colonies whose inhabitants had been reduced to eating their own dead after supply routes failed. Elias had learned not to assume that an illuminated window meant safety.

The first drone reached the transport's outer observation range twelve minutes later. Its cameras transmitted a slow sweep across the starboard habitation cylinder, revealing rows of dark passenger windows, several illuminated maintenance bays, and a series of docking collars that appeared to have been sealed from the outside. The vessel's name remained visible beneath layers of grime and old impact scars. MOURNING STAR. The lettering had once been white against a blue hull, but decades of radiation exposure and accumulated dust had reduced it to a pale, uneven stain. A decorative emblem depicting a silver bird crossing a field of stars was still visible near the forward passenger section. Elias recognized the symbol from the historical registry. The Veyr Transit Cooperative had marketed itself as a reliable carrier for families traveling between the older frontier settlements. Its advertisements promised safe passage, comfortable accommodations, and a new beginning for those willing to leave their homeworlds behind. Most of the people aboard the Mourning Star had been colonists, migrant workers, and families whose savings had purchased passage to a better life.

"External damage is limited," Sera reported. "No obvious weapon impacts. No evidence of catastrophic decompression. I'm seeing several sealed maintenance hatches and a number of inactive docking systems." She paused, adjusting the drone's camera. "Commander, take a look at the forward observation gallery."

Elias enlarged the image.

Behind one of the transport's broad viewing windows, faint light illuminated what appeared to be a passenger lounge. Rows of chairs stood beneath decorative ceiling panels, and several tables remained arranged beside the windows. The drone's camera struggled to resolve the interior through the thick transparent armor, but Elias could make out shapes seated near the observation wall. They were motionless, their silhouettes dark against the weak lighting. He leaned forward, narrowing the image filters. There were at least a dozen figures in the gallery, some sitting upright, others leaning toward the tables as though engaged in conversation. None moved when the drone passed outside the window.

"Are those people?" Mara asked.

Elias studied the image for several seconds. "Possibly. Could be mannequins or abandoned equipment. We need better resolution."

Sera adjusted the drone's approach, bringing it closer to the window. The figures became clearer. They were people, or at least something shaped like people, dressed in old civilian clothing and seated around the tables. One appeared to be holding a drinking glass. Another sat with its head tilted toward the observation window, its face obscured by shadow. Elias felt a cold pressure settle behind his ribs as he examined the scene. There was no visible movement, no reaction to the drone, and no indication that anyone inside the lounge was aware of the vessel approaching them. The Mourning Star continued drifting through the darkness, its internal lights burning steadily over passengers who had apparently been sitting in the same positions for a very long time.

Mara studied the image before speaking. "Medical assessment?"

Elias shook his head. "Not from this range. We can't confirm whether they're alive."

"Thermal signatures?"

"Environmental temperature is stable. Individual body heat is difficult to separate through the window armor."

The captain considered the information, then opened a channel to the ship's Justicar. "Justicar Haldane, report to the bridge. We may have a civilian recovery situation." She turned toward Elias. "Prepare a boarding assessment. Small team, full environmental protection, independent communications, and enough equipment to withdraw without relying on the transport's systems. Nobody enters until we've established a secure approach." Elias nodded and began assembling the requirements. The House's White Warrant doctrine governed hostile boarding operations, but civilian rescue followed many of the same practical principles: establish reliable information, preserve life support, secure access, and avoid committing personnel into compartments whose condition remained unknown. The Mourning Star had not demonstrated hostile intent, and there was no justification for treating her as an enemy vessel. Nevertheless, Elias intended to approach her with the same caution he would use against an armed cruiser.

The next twenty minutes passed in a steady exchange of sensor reports and technical assessments. Engineering confirmed that the transport's reactors were operating within normal parameters, although the fuel consumption figures suggested either unusually efficient power management or a system operating far below its original design capacity. The navigation archive remained inaccessible, but the vessel's external transponder continued broadcasting an identification code that had expired decades earlier. Several maintenance drones were visible along the hull, moving slowly between service ports and performing what appeared to be routine inspections. Their movements were mechanical and repetitive, following patterns that suggested automated maintenance rather than active human supervision. One drone spent nearly four minutes examining a section of hull plating before returning to its storage bay without making any visible repairs. Another emerged from a maintenance hatch carrying a replacement panel, installed it over an already intact section of armor, and returned inside.

Elias watched the second drone with growing unease. "Why replace undamaged plating?"

Chief Engineer Tovan Rell answered from the engineering channel. "Could be scheduled maintenance. Some automated systems replace components based on elapsed service time rather than actual condition."

"After seventy-three years?"

"Commander, after seventy-three years, I'd expect the maintenance schedule to have collapsed entirely. But the machines are still following something."

Mara leaned forward. "Could the ship be operating under an old emergency preservation protocol?"

"Possibly," Rell replied. "If the navigation system failed and the crew couldn't restore it, the automation might have entered long-term survival mode. But there's a problem."

"What problem?"

"The reactor's operating history doesn't match the amount of fuel she should have carried. Unless someone refueled her, or the original registry was wrong, she should have exhausted her reserves decades ago."

The bridge fell quiet again.

Elias looked toward the distant transport, its faint lights visible against the darkness. He had encountered enough strange machinery during House service to know that impossible readings usually had ordinary explanations hidden beneath layers of missing information. A ship might have received fuel from an unregistered salvage vessel. Its reactor could have been modified. Its records might be incomplete or deliberately falsified. None of those possibilities required supernatural explanations, and Elias had little patience for officers who allowed fear to replace investigation. Yet the Mourning Star was beginning to accumulate inconsistencies faster than his team could resolve them. The passengers remained motionless, the maintenance systems continued operating, and the ship's power reserves appeared to defy its historical specifications. He was considering whether to recommend a second observation period when Sera suddenly straightened at her station.

"Commander, I'm receiving a transmission."

Mara turned toward her. "From the transport?"

"Yes, Captain. Low-power civilian frequency. Very weak. It appears to be an automated docking announcement."

"Put it through."

A faint hiss filled the bridge speakers, followed by a woman's voice speaking in the calm, practiced tone of a commercial passenger-service officer. The recording was old, its audio degraded by interference and repeated compression, but the words were clear enough.

"Welcome to the Mourning Star, operated by the Veyr Transit Cooperative. We are pleased to confirm that all scheduled passengers have completed boarding. Departure preparations are underway, and our estimated arrival at Veyr's Crossing remains unchanged. Please ensure that all personal belongings are secured and that children remain within designated passenger areas during the initial transit sequence."

The message ended with a brief musical tone.

Elias stared at the communications display. "That's a departure announcement."

Sera nodded slowly. "Yes, Commander."

"From seventy-three years ago?"

"According to the historical records."

Mara studied the transmission data. "Could it be a corrupted automated loop?"

"Possibly. But the signal wasn't broadcasting when we arrived. It began after our drones approached the passenger spine."

Elias looked toward the central display, where the Mourning Star continued drifting silently beside the ruined relay. The vessel had been missing for seventy-three years, yet its automated systems were announcing a departure that should have taken place before most of the people aboard the Skymning had been born. He felt the familiar tightening in his stomach that came whenever an investigation crossed the boundary between unusual and dangerous. It was not fear exactly. It was the recognition that his team had entered a situation whose rules they did not yet understand.

"Captain," he said quietly, "I recommend we treat the transmission as evidence of active control. Someone or something aboard that ship responded to our approach."

Mara nodded. "Agreed. Maintain distance. No active scans until we've completed the passive survey."

The bridge returned to its work, but the atmosphere had changed. Conversations became quieter, reports more precise, and every new sensor return received closer examination. Elias watched the transport's internal lighting patterns, searching for evidence of movement behind the windows. The observation gallery remained unchanged, its seated passengers motionless beneath the dim illumination. A maintenance drone crossed the hull near the forward docking collar, paused beside a sealed access panel, and disappeared into a service hatch. Beyond the transport, the broken Varanthi relay continued to rotate slowly through the darkness, its ancient structure reflecting faint light from the distant system star.

Then Sera spoke again.

"Commander, there's another transmission."

Elias turned toward her. "Same frequency?"

"No. Different source. Narrow-band signal, directed specifically toward us."

Mara leaned forward. "Origin?"

Sera examined the data, her expression tightening. "The Mourning Star. Forward passenger section."

"Automated?"

"I don't think so."

The speakers crackled softly, and for several seconds there was nothing but interference. Then a woman's voice emerged, quieter than the earlier announcement and carrying a tremor that suggested she was struggling to remain composed.

"Unidentified vessel, this is passenger compartment seventeen aboard the Mourning Star. We can see your lights outside. Please respond."

Elias looked toward Mara, who immediately signaled for him to answer.

"This is Commander Elias Vorn of the House von Hjertstedt reconnaissance frigate Skymning. We have identified your vessel and are assessing its condition. Are you in immediate danger?"

The response came after a long pause.

"I don't know."

Elias exchanged a glance with the captain. "Can you tell us your name?"

Another pause.

"Lyra."

"Lyra, are you alone?"

The woman hesitated. Elias could hear faint movement behind her voice, followed by what sounded like a door closing somewhere nearby.

"Yes," she whispered. "I think so."

Mara leaned closer to the communications station. "Lyra, this is Captain Mara Teld. We need you to remain calm and tell us what happened aboard your ship. Do you know why the Mourning Star is here?"

For several seconds, there was no answer.

Then the woman spoke again.

"Captain, I don't understand. We only departed yesterday."

Elias felt the blood drain from his face.

Mara remained perfectly still.

On the central display, the Mourning Star drifted through the darkness, its ancient hull illuminated by the faint glow of the broken relay. Behind its observation windows, the motionless passengers continued sitting in their chairs, waiting for an arrival that had never come.

And somewhere deep inside the ship, a young woman was asking why nobody had told her they were lost.
