# The Forty Dead

TALES FROM THE KNOWN REACH — A standalone side story

The warning reached Commodore Helena Varek as the third tow cable snapped. On the tactical display, the condemned cruiser rolled slowly away from its tug and toward a crowded maintenance dock above Jupiter. Hundreds of workers were still inside the dock. The cruiser had no crew, no functioning reactor, and no engines to arrest its movement. Its surviving cable dragged behind it, whipping across the path of a smaller ship trying to escape. Helena leaned over the command console. “Clear the dock. Tug crews, stay out of that cable's reach.”

“Evacuation is underway,” her operations officer said. “The cruiser will reach the outer gantry before the last shift gets clear.”

Helena studied the plot. A fourth tug was approaching from above the cruiser, but the dock lay directly beyond it. If the tug pushed from that angle and missed, the wreck would strike the gantry even harder. “Bring that tug around the cruiser's bow,” she ordered. “Push it away from the dock. No one goes between the hull and the gantry.” The tug captain acknowledged, his voice sharp with effort as his ship burned across the cruiser's path. Its engines flared against the ancient armor. For a moment the drifting hull continued on course, too massive to notice the smaller vessel pressing against it.

A yard engineer appeared on the local channel. Her face was pale behind the scratched visor of a work suit. “This is Lena Ortiz on the gantry. We have two people trapped in the outer control booth. The access bridge is buckled.” Behind her, sparks flashed in the dark as another worker tried to cut the door free. Helena checked their position against the moving hull. The tug was slowing the cruiser, but not enough. “Ortiz, get your team back to the inner lock. The gantry is about to be hit.”

“The booth door opens toward the ship,” Lena said. “If it strikes, they won't get out.” She turned her helmet toward the workers beside her. “Cut the lower hinge. I'll take the top.” Helena heard the hiss of a cutting torch before she could answer. On the display, the tug's drive burn increased. The cruiser's projected path shifted by a fraction, then began to move away from the dock's central spine. It was still going to strike the gantry.

“Gantry team, you have ten seconds,” Helena warned. “Ortiz, that is an order.” The door came free. Two figures scrambled out, one pulling the other by a suit harness. Lena shoved the slower worker toward the inner lock as the derelict filled the camera view. The tug pushed again, its hull shaking under the load. The cruiser clipped the far end of the gantry instead of crushing the control booth. Metal folded outward, carrying a cloud of tools and broken plating into space. The inner lock closed behind Lena's team before the pressure warning reached its final tone.

For several seconds, Helena watched the tumbling debris and waited for a distress signal. The tug captain reported his crew safe. The dock's inner compartments had held. Lena came back on the channel, breathing hard. “We have everyone, Commodore. One broken arm, possibly two.” Helena let the breath leave her lungs. “Get them to medical. Then somebody tell me why that ship was allowed anywhere near an occupied dock.”

The ship was one of forty. They had entered Sol through the Pluto Gate under Imperial escort, passed Terran inspection, and been cleared for the slow tow inward. The Throne Imperium called them a donation to the Terran Federal Protectorate of Sol. Their weapons were unusable, their authentication systems stripped, and their reactors removed or disabled. Some trailed frozen coolant and fragments of armor. Several had sealed sections that no one from the Imperial decommissioning authority had been willing to enter. The broken tow cable had failed beneath a layer of corrosion hidden inside an apparently sound coupling. Helena looked at the remaining thirty-nine hulls and ordered every tow assembly inspected before the formation moved again. Then she called her chief engineer. “They haven't given us ships,” she said. “They've given us a disposal problem.”

Two days later, Legate Severian Kord stood beneath the flags of the Federal Assembly and the Imperial Protectorate Authority on Luna. Behind him, a vast display showed the donated vessels holding position around Jupiter. The Imperial Ministry of Frontier Stability, he explained, was proud to support Terra's developing defenses. The ships would provide valuable experience to Terran engineers and help reduce the burden on Imperial patrols. He did not call them combat-ready, and he made no promise of replacement components. His legal officer remained at his side while a recording team captured the ceremony.

Helena stood among the Terran officers, still tired from the investigation at Jupiter. She had read the transfer agreement twice during the journey to Luna. Terra had accepted ownership of the hulls and responsibility for every repair, accident, and disposal expense. The estimated cost of dismantling them safely would consume money already assigned to several escort squadrons. When the applause ended, Kord approached her with the composed expression of a man who knew exactly what had happened at the dock. “Commodore, I hope your people are recovering from the difficulty with the tow.”

“They are,” Helena said. “The gantry will take longer.”

“Unfortunate. Old equipment can be demanding.” Kord glanced toward the display. “Still, I understand you will oversee the restoration program. I trust the educational value will be considerable.” Helena looked at him. “It already has been, Legate. We learned to inspect Imperial tow fittings before we trust them.” His smile tightened. “You may find that the engineering requirements exceed your present industrial capabilities.”

“Perhaps. But I've spent most of my career repairing things their original owners considered beyond recovery.” Helena left him with his recording team and called Jupiter before she reached her transport. She wanted full structural scans, radiation surveys, and every original component preserved until her engineers had examined it. Her deputy, listening beside her, looked at the projected costs. “You're considering restoring all forty?”

“I'm considering finding out what we actually have.”

What they had was worse than the manifest suggested. On one vessel, radioactive dust had spread through the ventilation system. Another had decks warped by an old ammunition fire. Beneath replacement armor on a third, engineers found a pressure spine damaged badly enough to split under load. Lena led the team that opened an abandoned medical compartment on a fourth ship. Inside were twenty-seven sealed casualty containers, left in the cold when the life-support systems failed. She read the first name through the frost on a container window, then called Helena. “They're still here, Commodore. All of them.”

Helena ordered the remains recovered and identified where possible. An Imperial liaison objected that the transfer agreement contained no provision for the expense. Helena heard him out while Lena's team carried the first container through the airlock. “The Federation will cover it,” she said. “We don't leave people inside machinery because removing them is inconvenient.” The liaison made a note in his report. Lena waited until he had gone before she asked whether the names could be sent to the dead sailors' families. Helena told her they would find the families if they could.

Lena was back at work the next day, crawling through a narrow access shaft inside Hull Seventeen with chief structural engineer Tomas Rell. The cruiser's outer armor was a ruin, but his scans showed something promising beneath it. Lena braced her boots against a crossbeam while Tomas aimed a light along the frame. The metal had been scarred and patched, yet the main structural members ran intact from the forward compartments toward the drive section. “This part was built to take a beating,” he said. “The machinery around it is finished, but the spine might carry a new ship.”

Lena tapped the beam with a gloved hand. “Then we stop trying to save the walls.” Tomas took another scan, then another from a different angle. The numbers held. “We'll need to strip it almost to the frame. New compartments, new power, new everything.” Lena shone her light into a cramped service space. “Good. We might finally be able to reach the things that break.” When they brought the survey to Helena, Tomas projected the cruiser beside a modern Terran destroyer. “The original machinery is obsolete,” he said, “but we could rebuild around the surviving spine.”

“How much of the original ship would remain?” Helena asked.

“Enough for the Imperial registry to recognize it. Not enough for the original crew.” Lena watched the old frame rotate above the table. She had spent that morning showing Helena the maintenance records her team had recovered from another hull. The records were incomplete, but they showed where an earlier crew had strengthened a damaged junction rather than abandon the ship. “They kept fixing her because they had to,” Lena said. “We can do the same. We just have better tools now.”

The accident happened before the first month was over. A team was removing a suspended machinery assembly from a different hull when one of its supports failed. The warning came over the yard channel as a burst of noise and a worker shouting for the lift to stop. Tomas reached the engine room with the rescue crew. The assembly had fallen across the access platform, crushing the railing and pinning Lena beneath one edge. Two other workers had escaped through the hatch. A third was trapped on the far side of the machinery, conscious but unable to reach her.

The rescue chief ordered everyone clear of the unstable load. Lena answered when Tomas called her name, but her voice was weak and her suit alarms were sounding. A lifting frame could take the weight, yet the nearest safe attachment point was behind the fallen assembly. Tomas and a rigger worked their way through a lower maintenance passage while the medical team held position by the hatch. The passage ended under the damaged platform. Tomas could see Lena's hand through a gap in the metal. She moved her fingers when he called again.

They secured the lifting frame and raised the assembly a few centimeters. The rigger reached Lena first. The trapped worker on the far side helped pull her clear, and the medical team carried her out while Tomas stayed behind to stabilize the load. At the inner lock, Helena stepped aside to let the stretcher pass. Lena's visor had been opened for treatment. She looked once toward the shipyard lights beyond the hatch, then the medic drew the curtain around her. Lena died before they reached the hospital.

Helena attended her funeral beside the yard crews. Tomas stood near the back, holding a copy of Lena's scan of Hull Seventeen. He had printed it because she disliked examining structural plans on a screen without something she could mark by hand. After the service he left the copy with her family, including the notes she had made in the margins. Helena returned to Jupiter and stopped every suspended-load operation until the equipment and procedures had been inspected. The delay angered officials who wanted progress photographs. It also exposed two more unsafe lifting points before anyone else worked beneath them.

The industrial ministries resisted Tomas's proposal. Mars was expanding its factories, the Jovian yards already had ships waiting for repairs, and civilian reconstruction could not be abandoned to build a fleet. Helena brought the costs to the Executive Directorate herself. Restoring old Imperial equipment would leave the Federation dependent on parts it could not manufacture. Rebuilding around Terran systems would cost more at first and might fail. It would also give Sol the knowledge to build, repair, and arm larger ships without asking the Imperium for help. The Directorate approved six hulls for reconstruction. Helena requested four more for disassembly and research, then brought skeptical Assembly members to inspect Hull Nine's compromised reactor shielding. They returned to Luna less interested in keeping all forty vessels intact.

The program expanded. Across Jupiter's military yards and contracted industrial docks, crews stripped the best hulls while teams on Mars built reactor modules and standardized components. Existing naval maintenance was delayed to free equipment and experienced workers; civilian reconstruction kept its protected allocation. Six hulls were ultimately dismantled because their frames could not be made safe. Their usable parts and the lessons from taking them apart supported the others. Tomas kept Lena's annotated copy of the Hull Seventeen survey beside his console as the ship was cut open around him.

Kord followed the work through Imperial observers and a steady stream of reports. He could not stop Terran crews from repairing vessels the Imperium had transferred to them, but he could ask questions that consumed weeks. Why did a patrol cruiser require a larger power plant? Why were domestic weapons being fitted to an obsolete frame? Was a ship still being repaired if most of its interior had been replaced? The Protectorate charter required notification of new military hull construction and restricted access to Imperial military technology. Kord's legal officers examined every report for evidence that Terra had crossed either boundary. Helena supplied the records and kept her engineers working. She did not intend to give him a genuine violation to use.

The first reactor trial nearly ended the program. Helena watched from the protected control room while Tomas stood with the test team behind a transparent shield. Inside the rebuilt compartment, a modular fusion plant ran against a bank of industrial loads. Its power climbed steadily. For seventeen minutes, the readings held. Then a coolant manifold split. Pressure dropped across the display, and a cloud of vapor burst from the housing. The automatic shutdown engaged, but a valve in the secondary loop failed to close. Hot coolant continued to feed the damaged line.

“Isolate the reserve tank,” Tomas told the operator. The command failed. The valve controller had lost power when the manifold ruptured. A technician pointed to the manual shutoff on the compartment plan. It was on the other side of the damaged line. Helena saw Tomas reach for a protective suit. “Your team leader decides who goes,” she said. He stopped. The test engineer checked the temperatures, selected two suited specialists, and sent them through the outer access route while the rest of the team prepared to evacuate. Helena watched their suit cameras as they moved behind the shielding, found the manual wheel, and forced it closed together. The flow stopped. No one had been inside the reactor compartment when the manifold failed, and both specialists returned without injury.

The shutdown worked, but the failed valve had nearly made it useless. Tomas's team redesigned the control and moved the manual access to a protected corridor. The second trial ran for six hours. The third held full output for a day. When the test engineer finally allowed the crew into the control room, Tomas placed Lena's annotated survey beside the console. No one made a speech. They stood together long enough to watch the steady green readings, then began preparing the next plant.

Fourteen months after the donation, Hull Seventeen left the Jovian reconstruction yard under its own power. Its new registry read TFP Resolute. Much of the old silhouette remained, but the ship carried dark layered armor, reinforced mounts, Terran propulsion, and a reactor built on Mars. Commander Elias Dorn watched the maneuvering trial beside Helena on the command deck. He had served aboard Terran escorts for most of his career; this cruiser was larger than anything he had commanded. As Resolute accelerated, the deck gave a low, unfamiliar shudder. An engineer reported stress building at a junction near the drives. Dorn reduced power immediately and ordered the trial halted. Helena watched the numbers settle. “That's why we test,” she said.

The yard reinforced the junction. Later, a point-defense synchronization fault appeared during high reactor output, and a heavy-weapon trial transmitted enough recoil through its mounting to damage forward armor. Dorn wanted a ship he could trust with a crew, not one that looked impressive in a report. Every defect went back to the engineers, even when Imperial observers recorded it. Across the other yards, teams incorporated the fixes into hulls already under construction. By the time Resolute completed acceptance trials, many of her sisters were months into their own rebuilds.

The distress call came during a propulsion endurance trial beyond Neptune. A Terran industrial convoy had been intercepted by three armed frigates and several converted transports. Its ordinary escort was too small to hold them. Dorn's tactical officer brought the contact plot onto the bridge display. Industrial ships crowded around the convoy's lead freighter, their identification beacons flickering among the raiders. One freighter had already stopped responding. Dorn opened a channel to Helena at Jupiter. “Request permission to intervene.”

“Protect the convoy,” she answered. “Keep your identification clear before you fire. Outer patrols are coming, but you're closest.” Dorn acknowledged and turned to his crew. “Bring us in on the convoy's outer flank. Give their ships room to withdraw toward Neptune. No main-battery shot until we have a confirmed target.” Resolute accelerated. The old frame, reinforced by Terran hands, carried the strain. On the display, a raider frigate crossed behind a cargo vessel. The weapons officer held fire. A second later the cargo ship's beacon stabilized and the hostile moved clear.

“Target its engines,” Dorn ordered. The forward accelerator fired. The shot missed, passing close enough to force the frigate into a violent turn. Before its captain could recover, the second round struck the propulsion assembly. Armor and engine fragments scattered behind the raider, and the ship drifted out of formation. A cheer started on the bridge, then died as the missile warning sounded. The other two frigates had launched together.

“Point defense, engage. Turn the forward belt toward them.” Dorn gripped the edge of his console while the cruiser swung. Defensive guns flashed across the display, cutting through the incoming weapons. Several got through. One destroyed a sensor cluster; another struck the forward armor hard enough to throw two people from their stations. A third impact drove a shock through the command deck and filled the bridge with alarms. The damaged sensor picture broke apart. For a moment Dorn could see weapon returns, debris, and civilian beacons overlapping without knowing which was which.

“Convoy master, sound your beacons in sequence,” his tactical officer transmitted. A nervous voice answered from the lead freighter, and the beacons began to pulse one after another. The crew rebuilt the plot around them. Meanwhile, a hostile frigate drove toward a cargo ship falling behind the convoy. Dorn could pursue the damaged raider or turn to cover the freighter. He ordered the turn. “Missiles on the second frigate. Engines first.” Resolute's salvo crossed the widening gap and crippled the raider's maneuvering systems before it reached the cargo ship.

A report came from forward damage control. A sealed compartment behind the defensive belt had been struck by fragments, and a team was trying to reach the people inside. Dorn asked whether the bulkhead would hold. “For now,” the damage-control officer replied. “We need the ship steady to open the inner access.” Dorn gave the order. The third frigate fired once more, saw the convoy pulling behind the cruiser, and withdrew with the converted transports. A weapons officer asked permission to pursue. Dorn looked at the unprotected freighters and the two disabled raiders. “Negative. Stay with the convoy. Prepare rescue teams for both sides.”

The fighting lasted less than twenty minutes. Forty-seven people aboard the convoy had died before Resolute arrived. Three of her sailors died in the damaged forward compartment, and eleven were injured. Dorn stood outside the medical bay as the last of the wounded were brought through. A young damage-control specialist, her suit still streaked with sealant, asked whether the freighter they had turned to protect had escaped. He told her it had. She nodded once, then went back to help the medics. Resolute returned to Jupiter with blackened armor, broken defensive mounts, and damage the yard crews would study for weeks.

Helena came aboard before repairs began. She listened to Dorn's account, walked the impact sites with the engineers, and spent an hour with the medical staff. The forward belt had prevented penetrations that might have destroyed the original cruiser, but fragments had still reached the compartment where the three sailors died. She ordered the findings sent to every yard, even though the changes would delay launches. An Imperial observer, examining the damaged armor, suggested that the engagement demonstrated the danger of operating reconstructed warships. Helena handed him the casualty report. “Three of our people died. The raiders killed forty-seven aboard the convoy before we arrived. If you have a better way to protect them, I'll listen.” The observer offered no alternative.

By the eighteenth month, twenty-six of the original hulls were operational and eight more were in final trials. Six had been dismantled. The surviving ships were not identical. Some became heavy cruisers; others served as missile-support ships, command vessels, or convoy defenders. Their reactors, weapons, and replacement parts came from Sol's industrial network. By the time Legate Kord returned two months later, all thirty-four were in service. Three had already seen combat protecting civilian traffic.

Kord had received the observers' reports, but he expected a handful of patched patrol vessels. As his transport approached Jupiter under Terran escort, its sensors found one reconstructed cruiser after another. Their hulls carried Terran markings. Their reactors were active, and their weapons no longer matched the old Imperial designs. Kord ordered his aide to retrieve the original transfer manifest. “How many?” he asked when the formation filled the display. His naval adviser checked the registry. “Thirty-four operational, Excellency. Six dismantled.”

“They destroyed six Imperial vessels?”

“They were condemned for scrapping before the transfer.” Kord looked back at the formation and said nothing until the transport docked with Resolute.

Helena received him on the command deck beside Dorn and several engineering officers. Kord studied the Terran consoles, the repaired armor reports, and the reactor specifications. “Your engineers have exceeded the original class's certified operating limits.” Helena glanced at Dorn. “The original class no longer exists, Legate. These ships were rebuilt and certified under Terran standards.” Kord turned toward his legal officer. “The Imperium provided these vessels for technical development, not unauthorized military expansion.”

“Your ministry transferred ownership,” Helena said. “The agreement made us responsible for maintenance, repair, and disposal.” The legal officer confirmed the transfer wording, though he added that the charter still restricted Imperial military technology and required notification of new hull construction. Helena brought up the reports her office had submitted throughout the program. The equipment was Terran, and the work concerned hulls already owned by the Federation. Kord could challenge how extensively they had been rebuilt. He could recommend new restrictions. What he could not find was an undisclosed Imperial weapons system or a new hull hidden inside the paperwork.

“You have turned obsolete patrol ships into heavy warships,” he said.

“We turned abandoned machinery into something useful,” Helena answered. “The fact that it can fight is a consequence of what we needed it to survive.” She watched him study the damage report from the convoy engagement. He did not dismiss the casualties, but he did not set the report aside either. “I would like a demonstration,” he said. “Of the ships' current capabilities.”

Resolute and several reconstructed vessels moved to a range beyond the Jovian traffic corridors. Kord watched from the command deck as target hull sections took position at measured distances. Dorn ordered the firing sequence. Resolute's forward accelerator struck the first target through its central structure. Another ship launched missiles against a simulated propulsion assembly. A third engaged training drones with its point-defense network. Most were destroyed, but several penetrated close enough to expose a gap in coverage. The weapons officer reported the failure without waiting to be asked. Helena ordered the results recorded and sent to the engineers. Kord noticed that as well.

His naval adviser quietly reminded him that the reconstructed ships were still inferior to modern Imperial warships. Their sensors were less refined, their reactors produced more waste heat, and their propulsion systems were less efficient. Kord understood. He also understood that Terra could make the parts, repair the damage, and train the crews itself. The Federation had gained more than thirty-four ships. It had learned how to rebuild them.

After the demonstration, he met Helena privately in an observation chamber overlooking Jupiter. “The Imperium will take an interest in this development,” he said. Helena watched the fleet return to its anchorages. “I would be disappointed if it didn't.” Kord rested a hand on the back of an empty chair. “Some within the Ministry may interpret your actions as evidence of growing Terran independence.”

“We are responsible for defending our citizens, Legate. Every convoy we protect reduces the burden on Imperial patrols. Every ship we repair ourselves is one less vessel we must request from your government.” Kord looked at her. “You make it sound like a service to the Imperium.” Helena turned toward him. “You called the original donation a gesture of goodwill. We took you at your word.”

Kord departed Sol two days later with a more complicated report than his superiors had expected. The Federation had not violated the transfer agreement, and the fleet remained within the charter's broad military limits. Restricting what Terra had learned would require a new political confrontation. From Resolute's command deck, Helena watched his transport disappear toward the outer transit corridor. Several ships in the fleet were still undergoing maintenance. Their armor carried the marks of old Imperial battles and newer Terran repairs. She thought of Lena Ortiz, the sailors recovered from the condemned medical compartment, and the crew members killed protecting the convoy. None of them would see what the yards built next.

Dorn joined her with the latest repair schedules. “Do you think they'll try to stop us?” Helena considered the question. “Eventually. Not because of these ships. Because of what we've learned to build without them.” He looked toward the thirty-four reconstructed vessels taking their positions around Jupiter. “And what happens when they do?” Helena turned back to the engineering reports waiting on her terminal. “Then we'd better have something better than these ready.”

Beyond the observation windows, Jupiter's storms moved beneath the dark silhouettes of the reconstructed fleet. The ships were ugly, inefficient, and already becoming obsolete as Terran engineers applied what they had learned to new designs. But they belonged to Sol, and every component that failed could be replaced by people who understood how it worked.

The Imperium had given Terra forty dead ships. Thirty-four had returned to service. Six had been sacrificed to make that possible. And somewhere in the shipyards of Mars, the first entirely Terran-designed heavy warship was already taking shape. The men who had delivered the gift had intended to teach humanity its place. They had succeeded in teaching it something else.
