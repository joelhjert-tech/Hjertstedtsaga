# The Unfired Salvo

*A Saga Side Story — The Verath Incident*

> In-progress standalone story: Chapters One through Four, as continued after review in the source conversation. Proposed standalone continuity; placement in the main novel is unestablished.

## Chapter One — The Detained Convoy

Verath Station — Inner Reach

The first warning was not a weapons lock.

It was a change in the docking lights.

Captain Mara Venn watched the green approach corridor turn amber across the forward observation panels of the Aster Vale. Beyond the glass, Verath Station hung against a field of distant stars, its illuminated docking rings surrounding a dense central structure of old armor, habitation cylinders, cargo gantries, and relay equipment.

Hundreds of vessels moved through its surrounding traffic lanes. Merchant haulers waited for customs clearance. Fuel tenders drifted between enormous bulk carriers. Smaller courier craft slipped through carefully regulated approach corridors, their engine signatures flashing across the navigational display.

Verath was not beautiful.

It was crowded, overbuilt, and old enough that entire generations of engineers had forgotten why some of its internal passageways existed.

But it was alive.

And for the five civilian freighters approaching from the outer lanes, it was supposed to be nothing more than a scheduled resupply stop.

“Navigation,” Venn said, “why have we lost our approach clearance?”

Lieutenant Tomas Erlen leaned forward over his console.

“Checking, Captain.”

The bridge of the Aster Vale was narrow and practical. Cargo capacity had always taken priority over crew comfort. The captain's chair sat between two raised workstations, with tactical navigation to port and engineering to starboard.

There were no grand holographic tables, no ceremonial command rails, and certainly no space for the kind of military staff that occupied the bridges of capital ships.

The Aster Vale was a freighter.

A good one.

Venn had commanded her for eleven years.

She knew the sound of every coolant pump, the vibration of every maneuvering thruster, and exactly how much pressure the old vessel could tolerate before her forward cargo bulkheads began complaining.

She also knew that commercial traffic authorities did not normally cancel approach authorization without providing a reason.

“Verath Traffic has suspended our docking slot,” Erlen reported. “We're being instructed to enter holding vector seventeen.”

“Reason?”

“None provided.”

Venn frowned.

“That's not a reason.”

“No, Captain.”

“Ask them again.”

Erlen transmitted the request.

The answer arrived seven seconds later.

“Traffic Control confirms the order. All five freighters are to maintain formation and proceed to holding vector seventeen. Civilian docking clearance suspended pending inspection.”

“Inspection?”

Venn glanced toward the manifest display.

The convoy carried reconstruction equipment, agricultural machinery, medical systems, and several thousand containers of industrial components destined for Terran settlements.

Nothing unusual.

Nothing worth delaying an entire convoy over.

“Tell them we cleared customs at Merath and our transit documentation was authenticated before departure.”

Erlen began speaking into his microphone.

Then he stopped.

“Captain.”

Something in his voice made Venn turn.

“What?”

“Traffic Control is no longer issuing the instruction.”

“Who is?”

Erlen swallowed.

“Imperial Transit Authority.”

For a moment, the bridge was silent.

Then Venn reached for her command display.

“Put it on the main screen.”

A formal transmission appeared.

The symbol of the Throne Imperium rotated above several lines of densely formatted legal text. Beneath it was an authentication seal belonging to the Imperial Frontier Transit Office.

Venn read the first paragraph.

Then the second.

By the third, her expression had hardened.

“Article Seventy-One,” she murmured.

Erlen looked toward her.

“Captain?”

“They're invoking strategic cargo inspection authority.”

“Can they do that?”

Venn looked through the forward glass.

Far beyond the crowded merchant lanes, a military formation had begun separating itself from the station's outer security perimeter.

Six engine signatures burned against the darkness.

Two cruisers.

Four escort vessels.

Their identification codes appeared a moment later.

Imperial Navy.

Venn settled back into her chair.

“They apparently think they can.”

The first Imperial cruiser was already moving to intercept when Commander Elias Damar received the notification.

He stood aboard the bridge of the Terran frigate Peregrine, flagship of the small escort detachment assigned to protect the convoy.

The Peregrine was a practical ship, built for convoy security and rapid intervention rather than prolonged engagements against capital vessels.

Her forward weapons included precision missile batteries and a spinal gun intended to disable hostile propulsion systems. Her defensive arrays were designed to defeat raider attacks, not exchange sustained fire with Imperial cruisers.

Damar had two frigates under his command.

The Imperials had six warships.

He did not like the arithmetic.

“Confirm those identifications.”

“Confirmed, Commander,” Tactical replied. “Imperial cruiser Crown's Edict. Second cruiser Vigilant Ascension. Four supporting escorts.”

“Are they tracking us?”

“Passive tracking confirmed. No active fire-control illumination.”

Damar folded his arms behind his back.

“Good. Keep our weapons cold.”

The tactical officer hesitated.

“Sir, they're moving into the convoy's projected course.”

“I can see that.”

“Should we alter formation?”

“No. Not yet.”

Damar studied the movement vectors.

The Imperial vessels were not advancing in an assault pattern.

They were spreading into a controlled interception formation, positioning themselves so that any Terran attempt to continue toward the civilian docks would require passing dangerously close to one of their ships.

It was deliberate.

Professional.

And irritatingly effective.

“Communications. Open a channel to their senior officer.”

A moment later, the forward display changed.

An Imperial captain appeared, dressed in a charcoal uniform with gold rank markings and a dark crimson collar. He stood before a broad tactical projection, his expression carefully neutral.

“Commander Damar,” the man said. “Captain Lucien Varek, Imperial Navy. You have received our transit instruction?”

“I have received an inspection notice.”

“Then there should be no misunderstanding.”

“I am afraid there is. This convoy has valid commercial authorization. Its cargo was inspected before transit. We are operating under a registered Terran escort mandate.”

“All of which is acknowledged.”

“Then explain the detention.”

Varek glanced briefly toward something outside the image.

“Certain cargo manifests require additional verification.”

“Which manifests?”

“That information is restricted.”

“Restricted from the officers responsible for protecting the vessels?”

“Commander, this is an administrative matter.”

Damar glanced at the tactical display.

The Imperial cruisers were still closing.

“Captain, with respect, administrative matters do not usually require heavy cruisers.”

Varek's expression did not change.

“The presence of Imperial naval assets is a precaution.”

“Against what?”

“Unnecessary escalation.”

Damar almost smiled.

He restrained himself.

“Then perhaps you should stop moving your warships toward my civilian convoy.”

For the first time, something cold entered the Imperial captain's voice.

“Your government remains bound by the Protectorate Transit Charter, Commander. You would be wise to remember that.”

Damar felt the familiar pressure building behind his eyes.

It was always the same.

When Terran officers escorted Imperial commerce, they were praised for professionalism.

When Terran officers insisted on exercising independent judgment, someone inevitably reminded them of the protectorate.

“I remember our obligations very well, Captain,” he said. “Including our obligation to protect Terran citizens.”

“Then protect them by following instructions.”

The transmission ended.

Damar stared at the empty display.

“Charming fellow.”

A quiet laugh came from the navigation station.

He turned.

“Lieutenant?”

“Sorry, sir.”

“Don't apologize. Just keep us clear of those cruisers.”

He looked back at the tactical projection.

Six Imperial warships.

Five civilian freighters.

Two Terran escorts.

And somewhere inside those five freighters, apparently, something worth mobilizing a naval squadron over.

“Signal the convoy,” Damar ordered. “They are to reduce thrust and hold formation. Nobody approaches an Imperial vessel without my authorization.”

“Understood.”

“Launch two witness drones. Passive recording only. I want independent telemetry of everything happening here.”

The tactical officer acknowledged.

Damar lowered his voice.

“And send an encrypted incident report to Sol.”

Aboard the Aster Vale, Captain Venn was beginning to understand that the inspection had very little to do with ordinary customs regulations.

The first indication came when the Imperial authorities requested access to her cargo manifest.

She provided it.

The second came when they asked for a more detailed inventory of the sealed diplomatic containers stored in Hold Six.

Venn stared at the request.

Hold Six.

Thirty-two refrigerated containers.

Officially classified as specialist reconstruction material.

The cargo had been loaded under Federal Security Office authorization at a Terran-controlled transfer depot.

She had been given the correct transit seals, the correct insurance documentation, and a signed declaration that the material was harmless under standard shipping conditions.

The loading officer had also made one thing very clear.

The containers were not to be opened during transit.

Not by customs officers.

Not by maintenance personnel.

Not even by Venn herself.

She had disliked that arrangement.

Captains were supposed to know what their ships carried.

But government contracts paid well, and the cargo had been legally certified.

Now two Imperial cruisers were approaching her convoy.

And Imperial customs was asking about Hold Six.

“Captain?” Erlen asked.

Venn looked up.

“What?”

“They're requesting that we isolate the hold from the ship's internal network.”

“Absolutely not.”

“Ma'am?”

“Those containers depend on regulated temperature and environmental controls. Isolating the entire hold could compromise the cargo.”

“Should I tell them?”

“Tell them exactly that.”

Erlen transmitted the response.

A moment later, the Imperial reply appeared.

REQUEST DENIED. HOLD SIX WILL BE PLACED UNDER INSPECTION AUTHORITY.

Venn felt her jaw tighten.

“They denied a technical safety warning?”

“Yes, Captain.”

“Who signed the order?”

Erlen examined the authorization.

His expression changed.

“It's from Imperial Fleet Command.”

Venn looked toward the main screen.

“Fleet Command?”

“Yes.”

“Not customs?”

“No, Captain.”

The bridge grew very quiet.

Venn had spent enough years traveling Imperial-regulated lanes to recognize the distinction.

Customs could detain cargo.

Transit authorities could suspend docking privileges.

But fleet command did not normally involve itself in disputes over agricultural machinery.

Something larger was happening.

And nobody had bothered to inform the people carrying the cargo.

“Get me Commander Damar.”

Far from Verath, beneath the armored surface of Luna's principal diplomatic district, Minister Helena Sorn was reading a report she had hoped never to receive.

The conference room was small, windowless, and protected by multiple layers of communications security.

An illuminated projection of the Known Reach floated above the central table.

Several transit corridors glowed in pale blue.

One of them terminated at Verath Station.

A single amber marker identified the detained convoy.

Across from Sorn sat Deputy Director Adrian Pell of the Federal Security Office.

Pell was a thin man with closely cropped hair and the permanently tired expression of someone who spent too much of his life reading information he could not discuss.

Sorn placed the report on the table.

“Tell me this is a misunderstanding.”

Pell did not answer immediately.

That was answer enough.

“Adrian.”

“The Imperial authorities have identified a restricted shipment.”

“Which shipment?”

Pell hesitated.

“Hold Six aboard the Aster Vale.”

Sorn closed her eyes.

For several seconds, the room was silent except for the faint ventilation system.

Then she opened them again.

“You assured me that shipment would be transferred through commercial channels.”

“It was.”

“You assured me the documentation was legitimate.”

“It is.”

“You assured me the Imperium would have no reason to interfere.”

Pell shifted in his chair.

“We underestimated their intelligence capability.”

Sorn stared at him.

“That is a remarkably elegant way of saying somebody made a catastrophic mistake.”

Pell said nothing.

She rose and walked toward the tactical projection.

Verath.

A merchant station under nominal Imperial administration, governed in practice by commercial interests, shipping syndicates, and an extraordinary collection of competing intelligence services.

A place where almost anything could be purchased if one knew the correct intermediary.

Including information.

Especially information.

“What do they know?” she asked.

“We cannot determine that yet.”

“Do they know what the shipment contains?”

“They know it is connected to a Varanthi authentication archive.”

Sorn turned.

“How much of the archive?”

“We believe they only know about the fragment.”

“You believe?”

Pell's jaw tightened.

“Yes, Minister.”

Sorn looked back toward the map.

The Terran government had spent years quietly reducing its dependence upon Imperial transit infrastructure.

New navigational models.

Independent authentication research.

Alternative supply agreements.

Discreet exchanges with frontier powers.

None of it was formally prohibited.

At least, not individually.

But taken together, it represented a fundamental shift in the protectorate relationship.

The Imperium had helped rebuild Earth.

It had also ensured that much of Earth's interstellar economy depended upon systems the Imperium could regulate.

A navigation archive capable of identifying independent routes might change that balance.

Which was precisely why the Imperium would never willingly surrender one.

“How valuable is the fragment?” Sorn asked.

Pell considered the question.

“Potentially transformative.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It could provide enough authentication data to begin reconstructing an approach chain outside established Imperial corridors.”

“Could?”

“The archive is incomplete. We need to examine it before we can determine what it actually unlocks.”

Sorn returned to her chair.

“And we are risking a diplomatic crisis over something we haven't even examined.”

“Minister, if we surrender every strategic discovery because the Imperium objects, Terra will remain dependent forever.”

“I am aware of that, Adrian.”

“Then we cannot simply abandon it.”

Sorn studied him.

“You seem to believe that because an objective is important, every method used to obtain it becomes justified.”

Pell's expression hardened.

“And you seem to believe the Imperium will eventually grant us independence if we remain sufficiently polite.”

Neither spoke.

The tension remained between them.

Finally, Sorn touched her terminal.

“Connect me with Naval Operations.”

Pell leaned forward.

“Minister—”

“The convoy carries civilian reconstruction supplies. Whatever else is aboard those ships, we have an obligation to bring those supplies home.”

“Agreed.”

“And I intend to keep this from becoming a war.”

She looked directly at him.

“Which means the navy will receive the facts necessary to protect those ships. All of them.”

Pell lowered his eyes.

“As you wish.”

Sorn disliked the answer.

It sounded too much like compliance.

And not nearly enough like agreement.

At the Jovian naval anchorages, Commodore Anika Rell was reviewing a maintenance report when the priority summons arrived.

Her flagship, the Terran cruiser Meridian, had been undergoing scheduled refit after three months of convoy duty along the inner trade corridors.

The starboard defensive arrays had required replacement.

Two maneuvering thrusters were still awaiting final calibration.

And the engineering department had discovered a persistent fault in one of the main heat-transfer systems.

Nothing catastrophic.

Nothing that could not be repaired.

But enough to make Rell deeply unhappy about the prospect of immediate deployment.

She was standing over a maintenance console when her flag lieutenant entered.

“Commodore. Priority orders from Naval Operations.”

Rell did not look up.

“How urgent?”

“Executive Directorate authorization.”

That got her attention.

She straightened.

“Put it on my command display.”

The report unfolded across the screen.

VERATH STATION — CONVOY DETENTION INCIDENT

FIVE TERRAN COMMERCIAL VESSELS

TWO ESCORT FRIGATES

IMPERIAL INTERDICTION FORCE PRESENT

Rell read the preliminary assessment.

Then she read it again.

“An Imperial naval force is detaining a civilian convoy?”

“Yes, Commodore.”

“Under whose authority?”

“Imperial Transit Charter. Article Seventy-One.”

Rell frowned.

“That article concerns undeclared strategic cargo.”

“Correct.”

“Do we have undeclared strategic cargo aboard those freighters?”

Her lieutenant hesitated.

“That information is restricted.”

Rell slowly turned toward him.

“Lieutenant, I am being asked to take warships into a confrontation with the Imperial Navy.”

“Yes, Commodore.”

“And the government has decided that the reason for the confrontation is none of my business?”

The lieutenant looked uncomfortable.

“Those are the orders currently available.”

Rell exhaled through her nose.

“Of course they are.”

She examined the attached fleet disposition.

The convoy's current escort was badly outmatched by the Imperial vessels already present.

Additional Imperial forces were believed to be operating within neighboring relay sectors.

The Terran government was requesting a naval response sufficient to secure the convoy and ensure its safe passage.

Not an attack.

Not a retaliatory expedition.

A protective deployment.

The distinction mattered.

Especially when the opposing fleet belonged to the power under whose political framework Terra still formally operated.

“What's our readiness?”

The lieutenant consulted his slate.

“Meridian can depart within six hours, provided engineering accepts a reduced maneuvering margin. Two destroyers are immediately available. A third requires twelve hours. Four frigates can be assembled from local patrol assignments. Additional vessels would need to be recalled.”

“Support ships?”

“One fleet tender is available. The second is currently resupplying.”

Rell looked toward the tactical board.

The Imperium's naval establishment was enormous.

Her own ships were capable, but she had spent enough years studying Imperial formations to understand what their numerical advantage could mean in a sustained engagement.

She could assemble a task force.

She could reach Verath.

What happened afterward depended on decisions being made in rooms far from her bridge.

“Get Engineering to clear the heat-transfer fault,” she said. “I want the maneuvering thrusters certified before departure. No exceptions.”

“And the other vessels?”

“Immediate readiness. Recall the destroyer captains and alert the frigate commanders. I want a complete support assessment before we leave.”

“Understood.”

The lieutenant turned to go.

“Lieutenant.”

He stopped.

“Find out who authorized that cargo.”

“Yes, Commodore.”

“And why the Imperium thinks it's worth stopping five civilian ships.”

At Verath, Captain Venn watched an Imperial inspection cutter approach the Aster Vale.

The little vessel was painted in dark gray, with the Imperial crest displayed across its forward armor.

It carried no heavy weapons.

It did not need them.

The cruisers behind it were enough.

“Inspection cutter at twelve kilometers,” Erlen reported. “Requesting permission to dock.”

“Have we received an authenticated warrant?”

“No, Captain.”

“Then they don't have permission.”

“Imperial control says refusal will be considered obstruction.”

Venn rubbed her forehead.

She had spent eleven years keeping her ship out of trouble.

She had survived engine failures, dangerous gate arrivals, equipment shortages, and one particularly unpleasant encounter with raiders near the outer trade routes.

She had never imagined that the most dangerous decision of her career might involve refusing a customs inspection.

“Tell them we are prepared to cooperate with a lawful inspection,” she said. “But we require a verified warrant, identification of the disputed cargo, and confirmation from Verath's civilian port authority.”

Erlen transmitted her words.

A moment later, the response arrived.

“Captain, they're instructing us to surrender docking control.”

“No.”

“They say they're authorized to assume it.”

“Then they can produce the authorization.”

A new voice came across the bridge speakers.

“Captain Mara Venn.”

It was not the customs officer.

Not the inspection cutter commander.

The voice was older.

Measured.

Accustomed to being obeyed.

“This is Admiral Severin Caul of the Imperial Navy.”

Venn felt the blood drain slightly from her face.

An admiral.

Personally.

“Admiral,” she replied, keeping her voice steady. “I have been requesting clarification of your detention order.”

“I am aware.”

“Then perhaps you can provide it.”

“Your vessel carries material subject to Imperial strategic transit restrictions.”

“My vessel carries legally certified commercial cargo.”

“Captain, we are not interested in the agricultural equipment or reconstruction machinery aboard your ship.”

Venn glanced toward Erlen.

He was watching her intently.

Caul continued.

“We require access to one sealed consignment. If you cooperate, the remainder of your convoy will be released without unnecessary delay.”

“And if I refuse?”

“Then the inspection will proceed under naval authority.”

Venn looked toward the forward display.

The inspection cutter had stopped approximately five kilometers from her ship.

Beyond it, the enormous hull of the Crown's Edict drifted across the stars.

Its armor plates were dark.

Its engine exhausts glowed faintly blue-white.

Hundreds of weapon apertures were visible along the central hull.

A ship built to impose decisions.

“Admiral,” Venn said slowly, “I am a civilian captain. I don't know what you believe is aboard my ship. But I cannot surrender sealed government cargo without authorization from the government that placed it in my custody.”

A pause followed.

Then Caul spoke again.

“You are a citizen of a recognized Imperial protectorate.”

“I am a Terran citizen.”

“The distinction does not invalidate Imperial transit law.”

“Nor does Imperial transit law invalidate my responsibilities as a captain.”

For several seconds, the channel remained open.

Venn heard a faint exchange of voices in the background.

Then Caul answered.

“I respect your position, Captain.”

It was the sort of statement that usually preceded something unpleasant.

“But the matter is no longer subject to civilian discretion.”

The channel closed.

Almost immediately, a warning sounded from Tactical Navigation.

“Captain!”

Venn turned.

“Imperial cruiser changing position. She's moving across our departure vector.”

The great warship advanced slowly, its bulk blotting out the lights of the station beyond.

Another contact moved behind the convoy.

Then two more.

The Imperials were enclosing the freighters within a controlled volume of space.

Not attacking.

Not yet.

Simply making it impossible to leave without challenging the authority of the largest fleet in the region.

Venn gripped the armrests of her chair.

“Contact Peregrine. Tell Commander Damar what's happening.”

“We're already receiving his transmission.”

“Put him through.”

The Terran commander's face appeared.

“Captain Venn. Are your people safe?”

“For the moment.”

“Good. Maintain your position. We are documenting the Imperial deployment.”

“They want Hold Six.”

Damar's expression changed.

“Hold Six?”

“Yes.”

“Have they identified the contents?”

“No.”

“Have you?”

Venn hesitated.

“No, Commander.”

Damar considered that.

“Do not surrender the cargo without further authorization. But don't obstruct a boarding party with force. If they attach, secure your crew and notify me immediately.”

“Can you stop them?”

The question hung between them.

Damar glanced away briefly.

Venn could see the tactical display reflected in the bridge glass behind him.

Six Imperial warships.

Two Terran frigates.

He looked back.

“Captain, I can make sure they know we're watching.”

Venn understood what he was telling her.

He could protest.

He could record.

He could put his ships between the Imperials and her convoy.

But if the Imperial commander decided to use force, there was very little he could do to stop it.

“Understood,” she said.

“Stay calm. Help is being arranged.”

The transmission ended.

Venn remained motionless for a moment.

Then she looked toward Erlen.

“Any word from Verath's civilian authorities?”

He shook his head.

“They've suspended all traffic instructions in our sector. Imperial Fleet Command has assumed operational control.”

“And our other freighters?”

“Maintaining position. All reporting normal systems.”

Venn nodded.

“Keep them informed.”

She stood and crossed toward the forward observation panels.

The inspection cutter remained stationary.

The Imperial cruisers held their positions.

Beyond them, Verath Station continued operating.

Merchant vessels departed their docks.

Fuel barges transferred supplies.

Navigation beacons flashed.

Cargo was bought and sold.

Life continued.

But the small Terran convoy was now surrounded by warships.

Five civilian freighters carrying machinery, food-production systems, and medical equipment intended for settlements that could not afford to lose them.

And thirty-two sealed containers that might contain something capable of changing the political future of Sol.

Venn watched the distant cruiser.

She wondered how much Admiral Caul knew.

She wondered what the Terran government had placed aboard her ship.

And, for the first time since assuming command of the Aster Vale, she wondered whether her vessel would ever complete its journey.

Then the communications officer spoke again.

“Captain.”

“What now?”

“A private message. From the Imperial flagship.”

“Another demand?”

“No, ma'am.”

Erlen looked up.

“It's a personal communication from Admiral Caul.”

Venn hesitated.

“Read it.”

Erlen glanced at the display.

Then he slowly repeated the message.

“Captain Venn. I understand that you believe you are acting in the interests of your government. I would remind you that governments are not always honest with the people who serve them.”

The bridge fell silent.

Venn looked toward the sealed cargo manifest.

Hold Six.

Thirty-two containers.

No inspection rights.

No explanation.

No questions permitted.

Outside, the Imperial cruiser remained motionless against the stars.

“Save that message,” she said quietly.

Erlen nodded.

“Under what classification?”

Venn returned to her chair.

“Evidence.”

Far away, among the vast naval installations orbiting Jupiter, the cruiser Meridian began preparing for departure.

Engineering teams withdrew from open maintenance compartments.

Ammunition barges approached her loading docks.

Fuel lines connected to reinforced transfer ports.

Across the anchorage, destroyers and frigates began coming alive, their reactors rising from standby into operational readiness.

On the command deck, Commodore Anika Rell studied the most recent tactical information from Verath.

The Imperial interception force had expanded its security perimeter.

The detained convoy remained stationary.

No casualties had been reported.

No shots had been fired.

For now, it was still a diplomatic incident.

Her flag captain approached.

“Commodore. Naval Operations has approved our initial departure group.”

“How many?”

“Seven combatants, including Meridian. The third destroyer can follow when ready. Additional escorts are being recalled.”

“Not much against the Imperial Navy.”

“No, ma'am.”

Rell studied the deployment chart.

“Enough to escort five freighters, though.”

“If they let us reach them.”

She looked at him.

“That's the question, isn't it?”

The captain said nothing.

Rell enlarged the Verath tactical projection.

The Imperial vessels formed a narrow enclosing screen around the civilian convoy.

It was an elegant little maneuver.

No weapons fire.

No casualties.

No overt declaration of hostility.

Just sufficient military pressure to force obedience.

Rell recognized the doctrine immediately.

The Imperium had used variations of it for generations.

A disputed relay.

A customs violation.

A territorial claim.

First the lawyers arrived.

Then the warships.

By the time anyone admitted a confrontation existed, the stronger power had already established control.

But this time, the detained vessels belonged to Terra.

And Terra was beginning to grow tired of being told where its ships could travel.

“Navigation,” Rell said.

“Commodore?”

“Prepare the Verath route. Request transit authorization for the full task group.”

“Imperial authentication required for the central corridor.”

Rell looked at the chart.

There it was.

The same structural weakness that had shaped Terran foreign policy for decades.

Even to reach a convoy being detained by the Imperium, her ships might have to request permission to transit infrastructure administered under Imperial authority.

She considered the irony for a moment.

Then she straightened.

“Submit the request.”

“And if they refuse?”

Rell looked toward the enormous orbital yards outside her bridge.

Beyond them lay Jupiter, its storm bands turning slowly beneath the reflected lights of a thousand installations.

The shipyards.

The factories.

The military bases.

The trade routes that kept a recovering civilization alive.

All of it depended, to some degree, upon access to a wider interstellar network Terra did not fully control.

“If they refuse,” Rell said, “we'll have our answer about what kind of dispute this really is.”

Her captain nodded.

“Understood.”

Rell returned to the tactical display.

Somewhere beyond the inner sectors, five freighters were being held under the guns of an Imperial fleet.

She had been ordered to bring them home.

And she intended to do exactly that.

But as she studied the intercepted Imperial deployment, one question remained unanswered.

Why would an admiral of the Throne Imperium personally oversee the detention of a civilian cargo shipment?

Whatever was aboard those freighters, someone had decided it was worth risking a confrontation with Terra.

Rell suspected she was about to discover just how much.

## Chapter Two — A Question of Authority

Verath Station — Inner Reach

The Imperial inspection cutter had been holding position for forty-three minutes.

Commander Elias Damar had counted every one of them.

On the forward tactical display of the Terran frigate Peregrine, the cutter appeared as a small amber contact, stationary five kilometers from the civilian freighter Aster Vale. Beyond it, the Imperial cruiser Crown's Edict occupied a position directly across the convoy's authorized departure corridor.

The second cruiser, Vigilant Ascension, had taken station farther out, supported by four escorts.

It was an effective blockade.

Damar could see the purpose behind every Imperial movement. The cruisers controlled the broad approaches. The escorts covered the gaps between the freighters. The inspection cutter remained close enough to begin boarding within minutes.

His own two frigates occupied the only position from which they could protect the convoy without directly challenging the Imperial formation.

They were outnumbered three to one.

Outgunned by considerably more.

And increasingly short of options.

"Update on civilian traffic?" he asked.

Lieutenant Commander Nia Serrat, his tactical officer, examined the station telemetry.

"Verath Control has diverted all commercial arrivals away from holding vector seventeen. A twelve-thousand-kilometer exclusion volume has been established around the convoy."

"Under whose authority?"

"Imperial Fleet Command."

Damar glanced toward the larger tactical projection.

"Of course."

"Station Control has filed an objection."

"Any response?"

"None."

He leaned forward.

The exclusion volume bothered him.

Not because it was tactically unreasonable. Any competent commander conducting a sensitive inspection would want civilian traffic kept out of the immediate operating area.

But it had another effect.

It removed independent witnesses.

Verath was a merchant station. Thousands of commercial vessels passed through its approach corridors every day. Their navigation systems, thermal imagers, collision-avoidance arrays, and passive military-threat monitors recorded enormous amounts of information.

Ordinarily, any significant confrontation near the station would be observed by dozens of unrelated parties.

Now the Imperial Navy had cleared the surrounding space.

Only military sensors remained close enough to see what happened.

Damar looked toward Serrat.

"Status of our witness drones?"

"Both operational. Passive recording only. One positioned above the convoy plane, the second behind the outer escort screen."

"Keep them there."

"Imperials have challenged their presence twice."

"Have they issued a formal removal order?"

"No, sir."

"Then they can challenge them a third time."

Serrat almost smiled.

Almost.

The forward display changed.

A communications notification appeared.

IMPERIAL FLEET COMMAND — PRIORITY TRANSMISSION

Damar touched the acceptance control.

Captain Lucien Varek appeared.

"Commander Damar."

"Captain."

"I trust you have received the updated security instructions."

"I have."

"Then I must ask why your vessels remain inside the inspection perimeter."

Damar studied the Imperial officer's face.

Varek appeared tired.

Not frightened.

Not angry.

Simply tired.

It was a familiar expression among officers who had spent too many years enforcing decisions made by people who would never stand on a warship's bridge.

"My frigates are escorting five Terran-registered commercial vessels," Damar replied. "Until that responsibility is formally transferred or the convoy is released, we remain with them."

"Your vessels are not being detained."

"That's very generous."

Rell remained motionless.

Beyond the forward observation panels, the damaged Imperial flagship hung against the distant lights of Verath Station.

It was a vast machine.

Built by an empire that believed its authority was the foundation of civilization.

Rell had fought that machine.

And she had found that it could bleed.

But somewhere far beyond the battlefield, politicians were calculating the cost of what she had accomplished.

She wondered whether they would decide the victory was worth keeping.

A new transmission arrived.

EXECUTIVE DIRECTORATE — PRIORITY COMMAND CHANNEL

Rell looked toward Kade.

"Another message from Sol?"

"Yes, Commodore."

"Content?"

"Encrypted. Command authority level."

She stared at the notification.

For several seconds, she did not open it.

Then she reached toward the control.

"Put it through."

The display changed.

Minister Helena Sorn appeared.

Her expression was grave.

"Commodore Rell."

"Minister."

"I need to discuss the terms under which your mission may be concluded."

Rell felt the words settle over the bridge.

Not completed.

Concluded.

She looked toward the Imperial flagship.

The damaged cruisers.

The freighter.

The men and women who had died.

Then back toward the minister.

"Understood," she said.

And for the first time since arriving at Verath, Commodore Anika Rell feared that the greatest threat to her mission might no longer be the Imperial fleet.
