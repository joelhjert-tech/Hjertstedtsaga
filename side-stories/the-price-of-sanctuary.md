# THE PRICE OF SANCTUARY

A Tale from the Known Reach

We came to Sol because every other door had closed.

The official histories of the Veyran Remnant describe our arrival as a calculated withdrawal through three contested lane corridors, followed by a diplomatic request for emergency repairs. They record the preservation of seven military vessels, including a medical transport, and the successful evacuation of more than eleven thousand military personnel and civilians. They describe my command decisions as examples of disciplined retreat under overwhelming enemy pressure. There are monuments to those decisions now, and children learn about them in schools built on worlds I never expected to see again. I have attended three dedication ceremonies and stood quietly while politicians explained what a remarkable achievement it had been.

The truth is considerably uglier.

We had seven ships because the other forty-three were dead. We had eleven thousand survivors because nearly ninety thousand others had been killed, captured, or abandoned during the retreat. Four of our vessels were leaking atmosphere, two were operating on emergency reactor systems, and our last functioning medical transport had turned its cargo decks into a morgue three weeks earlier, while its remaining medical compartments struggled to care for the living. We had no proper ammunition reserves, no functioning repair tender, and barely enough fuel to reach the next authenticated transit point. The surviving crews had been eating reduced emergency rations for nineteen days. We had stopped recording individual deaths aboard the smaller escorts because their medical officers were already dead, and nobody had enough personnel left to maintain the lists.

My name is Captain Varek Thenn. I commanded the cruiser Enduring River, flagship of what remained of the Veyran Third Defensive Flotilla.

I am Veyran, one of the river-born peoples of the outer Known Reach. We are broad through the shoulders, heavy-boned, amphibious by ancestry, and considerably more comfortable in warm water than aboard a military vessel. Our skin ranges from dark green to the gray of river stone, and our eyes possess a second protective membrane that makes prolonged exposure to dry air uncomfortable. We built our civilization along the enormous freshwater basins of Veyra, where our oldest cities were constructed around canals, reservoirs, flood barriers, and floating agricultural platforms. Our engineers were famous for building structures intended to survive centuries of storms. Our merchants preferred predictable contracts, our politicians preferred long arguments, and our soldiers had traditionally been regarded as an unfortunate necessity.

We had never wanted an interstellar war.

The Sorynth Veil had not cared.

Eleven years earlier, their first ships had appeared beyond the outer approaches of the Kelrath Corridor. They were pale, narrow vessels with curved hulls and recessed engines, accompanied by clouds of small autonomous machines that spread through local space like drifting seeds. They did not issue conventional challenges or demands. We received signals, but none that our translators recognized as replies to our diplomatic channels. What happened at that first meeting was less clear than I believed at the time.

In the encounters that followed, their ships approached our navigation buoys, disabled transmitters, and interfered with civilian traffic. When our patrol frigates attempted to intercept them, the Sorynth destroyed their sensor arrays and propulsion systems with frightening precision.

We interpreted those actions as the beginning of an invasion.

Perhaps we were right.

Perhaps we were wrong.

By the time anyone thought seriously about the distinction, the first cities were already burning.

The war lasted eleven years. During the first three, we believed we could win. During the next four, we believed our allies would intervene. During the final four, we fought because surrender appeared to mean disappearance, and because every Veyran commander understood what happened to civilian populations when the fleet protecting them ceased to exist. The Sorynth rarely destroyed a warship outright if it could be disabled. They blinded it, stopped its engines, silenced its communications, and left it drifting until enormous recovery frames emerged from the dark to carry it away. Entire crews vanished without distress signals. Captured vessels were never returned. We found empty colonies, silent orbital habitats, and navigation stations whose computers had been stripped while their reactors continued running.

By the eleventh year, we had lost twenty-seven inhabited systems.

We had also lost the ability to tell our children why.

The final retreat began at the shattered relay of Damaris Reach. The Third Defensive Flotilla had been ordered to hold the approach while civilian evacuation convoys escaped toward the inner lanes. We held for fourteen hours. The Sorynth did not overwhelm us with a conventional battle line; they separated our formations, destroyed our communications relays, and dismantled the ships responsible for coordinating our defenses. Our battleship Stone of Veyra remained operational for nearly six hours after losing its main engines, firing its surviving batteries until a precision strike severed the command spine. I watched its forward sections break apart while the aft compartments continued transmitting casualty reports. The evacuation convoy escaped with approximately forty thousand civilians. Of the flotilla vessels assigned to protect it, seven eventually reached Sol.

I still do not know whether the convoy survived the next gate.

We entered the outer Sol approach through the Pluto Gate on the thirty-second day of our retreat. Our charts called it the Gate of Truce; the Terrans named it for the distant world inside its orbit.

The Enduring River emerged first, and for several seconds I thought the transit had killed us. The ship struck the arrival envelope badly, our damaged navigation systems failing to compensate for the gate's reception geometry. Emergency thrusters fired along the port side, throwing everyone on the bridge against their restraints. A structural warning screamed through the command deck, followed by the terrible metallic groan of a keel brace that had already endured more punishment than its designers intended. The main display flickered, recovered, and showed the distant light of Sol surrounded by navigation markers, patrol contacts, and an astonishing density of controlled traffic.

"Transit complete," First Officer Audren Vash reported, although his voice sounded as though he scarcely believed it. "We're inside the outer reception zone. Reactor stable at twenty-one percent. Port maneuvering cluster remains offline. Life support reserves approximately seventeen hours at current consumption."

"Other ships?"

"Two contacts emerging. Three. Four. The Quiet Stone is late."

I watched the tactical display as our surviving escorts appeared one by one. The destroyer Grave Current emerged with its starboard engine housing open to vacuum, followed by the frigates Reedwatch, Hollow Bank, and Last Measure. Our medical vessel Mercy of Rain appeared nearly a minute later, trailing fragments of insulation and frozen atmosphere. The final contact did not arrive until the gate had already begun cycling toward its next reception interval.

The Quiet Stone emerged sideways.

Its damaged engines fired at full power, sending the small escort into a slow, uncontrolled rotation. For one terrible moment I watched its projected trajectory intersect the Mercy of Rain, and I knew that we were about to lose two more ships before reaching safety.

"Collision warning," Audren said. "The Quiet Stone has no maneuver authority."

"Warn the medical ship. Emergency separation burn."

"Captain, their fuel reserves—"

"Burn anyway."

The Mercy of Rain fired its surviving thrusters and began moving away, but the damaged escort continued drifting toward it. Then three unfamiliar contacts accelerated from the outer security perimeter. They were compact Terran patrol craft, painted in subdued Federal colors, and they moved with the controlled precision of vessels accustomed to operating inside congested transit space. One positioned itself ahead of the escort, another beneath its damaged engine housing, and the third deployed a small cluster of maneuvering drones.

The drones attached to the Quiet Stone.

A moment later, their engines ignited in sequence, slowing the escort's rotation and nudging it clear of the medical vessel.

"Collision avoided," Audren whispered.

I stared at the display.

The Terrans had not asked permission.

They had simply seen two ships about to die and done something about it.

A transmission arrived almost immediately.

"Unidentified formation, this is Sol Outer Traffic Control, Pluto Gate Authority. You are inside a restricted reception envelope. Maintain current vectors, disable all nonessential emissions, and acknowledge this transmission."

The voice was human, female, and entirely professional.

Audren looked toward me. "They've identified our weapons."

"Of course they have."

"Multiple defense platforms are tracking us. At least six naval vessels are moving into interception positions."

I examined the tactical picture. The Terran formation was disciplined, its warships holding enough distance to avoid provoking an immediate confrontation while still controlling every practical escape route. Their point-defense systems were active, and several remote sensor platforms had begun taking independent readings of our damaged hulls. Farther away, larger contacts were adjusting their patrol patterns to prevent us from moving deeper into the system without authorization.

They were not welcoming us.

They were making certain we could not hurt them.

I respected that.

"Open communications," I ordered.

The channel cleared, and I addressed the unknown controller in the most formal trade language I could manage. "Sol Outer Traffic Control, this is Captain Varek Thenn of the Veyran Third Defensive Flotilla. We are six damaged warships and one medical transport requesting emergency sanctuary, medical assistance, fuel, and repairs. Our crews include wounded personnel and civilian evacuees. We are unable to continue transit."

There was a brief silence.

"Captain Thenn, acknowledge that you understand Sol is operating under restricted military-access protocols. You will not approach the inner system without Federal authorization."

"Understood."

"Are your weapons active?"

"No. Most are no longer functional."

"Are you carrying biological, radiological, or unknown technological hazards?"

I hesitated.

"Captain?"

"We have been fighting an enemy whose technology we do not fully understand. Several of our vessels have sustained direct strikes from their weapons. We cannot guarantee that our hulls are free of contamination."

Another silence followed.

Then the controller spoke again, and something in her tone changed.

"Thank you for telling us. Maintain your present course. Medical and quarantine teams are being dispatched. Do not attempt to dock with civilian installations. We are arranging a controlled anchorage."

Audren leaned toward me. "Captain, we should inform them about the Sorynth."

"I intend to."

Before I could continue, another voice entered the channel.

"Captain Thenn, this is Commodore Elias Rourke, Terran Federal Navy. I have operational authority over the outer reception defense group. Your vessels are currently being classified as distressed military traffic under emergency custody procedures. We will render immediate assistance, but your ships will remain under armed escort until their status has been verified."

His voice was calm, direct, and carried the unmistakable weight of someone accustomed to making decisions that other people would have to live with.

"Commodore," I said, "we have no intention of threatening your system."

"I believe you, Captain. My job is to verify it."

"I must also inform you that we cannot pay for repairs."

"That is not the immediate problem."

"We possess no meaningful cargo, no reserve funds, and no political authority capable of guaranteeing repayment."

"Captain, I have seven damaged ships in a restricted gate envelope, one of them is rotating because half its maneuvering system is missing, and your medical transport has been broadcasting a low-pressure alarm for the last six minutes. I am not going to discuss invoices while your people are dying."

I could not answer.

Rourke continued, his voice becoming slightly less formal. "We'll discuss your situation once everyone is somewhere safe. For now, I need your engineers to cooperate with our rescue teams. Can they do that?"

"Yes."

"Good. Then let's get your people out of the transit corridor."

I remember that conversation more clearly than almost anything that followed.

Not because the Terrans had promised us sanctuary.

They had not.

Not because they had offered to repair our ships without payment.

They had made no such promise either.

I remember it because Commodore Rourke had treated our survival as an immediate operational problem rather than a political negotiation.

After eleven years of watching governments debate whether our destruction justified intervention, I found that almost impossible to understand.

The first Terran rescue craft reached us twenty-three minutes later.

They were not beautiful vessels. Their hulls were blunt, heavily reinforced, and covered in maneuvering thrusters, grappling assemblies, docking collars, and emergency equipment. Their pilots operated with a confidence born from years of working around damaged ships and dangerous machinery. One rescue tug approached the Enduring River from beneath our ruined port engine housing, deployed four articulated arms, and attached itself to the cruiser with a series of impacts that reverberated through the bridge.

"External docking contact," Audren reported. "Terran vessel requesting access to engineering."

"Authorize."

A moment later, a voice appeared on our engineering channel.

"Veyran cruiser, this is Federal Rescue Tug Mule Nine. I'm Lieutenant Mara Keene. We've got a look at your port engine housing, and I have some bad news."

I closed my eyes.

"Go ahead, Lieutenant."

"Your main support frame is cracked through three structural members, your thrust vectoring assembly is missing, and somebody appears to have welded a coolant bypass directly across an access hatch."

"Our engineers have been making emergency repairs for eleven years."

"Yes, Captain. That explains the welding."

I heard someone laughing quietly in the background of her transmission.

Keene continued. "We're going to secure the engine frame before we move you. Please tell your people not to activate the port drive under any circumstances."

"It has not functioned for nineteen days."

"Excellent. Let's keep it that way."

Keene asked for the hatch to be opened so her team could reinforce the frame from inside. Our chief engineer, Pell, came onto the channel before I could answer. He explained that the welded bypass carried coolant for the one pump keeping the port reactor loop stable. Cutting it without another route would stop the pump.

Keene asked how long he needed to fit a temporary line. Pell requested her team's portable pump and two of his own engineers. I authorized access to the machinery compartment and sent Audren to coordinate the security checks. Keene's team held position while Pell installed the line; only after he confirmed the flow did he open the hatch for them.

The rescue operation lasted nearly nine hours.

During that time, Terran tugs stabilized our damaged ships, emergency medical shuttles evacuated the most seriously wounded, and Federal security personnel conducted rapid inspections for weapons, contamination, and unauthorized passengers. We were not permitted to move freely through the system. Every vessel received a restricted navigation corridor, and our remaining ammunition was inventoried under armed supervision. The Terrans were courteous, but their precautions were thorough. I later learned that this was ordinary Federal procedure, developed by a civilization that had survived its own near-extinction and possessed no intention of repeating the experience.

By the time the final escort was stabilized, the Mercy of Rain had transferred nearly eight hundred critical patients to Federal medical ships.

Thirty-seven died before reaching them.

I had grown accustomed to numbers like that.

What I had not grown accustomed to was watching strangers become angry about them.

A Terran medical officer contacted me after the last evacuation shuttle departed. She was a middle-aged woman with tired eyes, short gray hair, and a uniform stained with blood from patients she had been treating personally.

"Captain Thenn, I'm Commander Nessa Calder, Federal Emergency Medical Service. We have your wounded. Some of them are in very poor condition."

"I understand."

"Your medical records are incomplete."

"Our medical staff have been operating without proper equipment for several years."

She looked away briefly, and when she spoke again her voice was quieter. "We've identified children among the evacuees. Some are severely malnourished. Several have injuries that should have been treated months ago."

"Yes."

"How long have you been running?"

"Thirty-two days."

"How long have you been at war?"

"Eleven years."

Calder stared at me for several seconds.

Then she nodded.

"All right, Captain. We'll start with the ones we can still save."

The first death notification arrived less than an hour later.

Then another.

Then twelve more.

The Terrans continued working.

We were eventually ordered toward a secured industrial anchorage in the Martian region, escorted by Federal Navy vessels and accompanied by enough rescue tugs to move our entire flotilla without relying on its damaged propulsion systems. The journey took considerably longer than normal traffic would have required. Our ships could not sustain high acceleration, and the Terrans refused to risk additional structural damage merely to satisfy a schedule. We passed through controlled transit corridors, beneath the distant silhouettes of naval patrol formations, and eventually entered the enormous industrial region surrounding Mars.

I had expected a planet of factories.

I had not expected the scale.

Orbital construction yards stretched across the darkness in immense distributed clusters, their docking frames illuminated by welding arcs and industrial floodlights. Cargo vessels moved between fabrication stations, while enormous component transports carried armor sections, engine assemblies, reactor housings, and structural frames toward unfinished warships. Farther away, civilian habitats glowed beneath protective shields, and the red surface of Mars filled half the observation display. The planet itself was scarred by centuries of mining and industry, its orbital infrastructure carrying the weight of a civilization determined never again to depend entirely upon foreign shipyards.

Audren studied the industrial traffic with visible disbelief.

"They build all this themselves?"

"Apparently."

"There are hundreds of active construction slips."

"Yes."

"Captain, some of those ships are larger than anything we possessed before the war."

I looked toward the nearest military construction complex, where a half-completed Terran cruiser hung between enormous assembly gantries. Its armor was unfinished, exposing internal decks and structural supports, while robotic fabrication arms moved across the hull with relentless precision. Beyond it, another vessel was being stripped for parts, its engines removed and its weapons disconnected. Nothing appeared wasted. Even the damaged sections were being cataloged, sorted, and carried toward processing facilities.

"They are preparing for something," Audren said quietly.

I watched the construction yards for several seconds before answering.

"Perhaps they simply remember what happens when you are not prepared."

Our assigned destination was Martian Naval Repair Anchorage Seventeen, a heavily defended industrial complex located beyond the main civilian construction lanes. The anchorage consisted of several enormous repair frames, sealed fabrication halls, ammunition storage platforms, medical facilities, and a network of armored service stations connected by transport gantries. Unlike the bustling civilian yards we had passed, Anchorage Seventeen operated beneath strict security protocols. Automated defense turrets followed our approach, and several Federal patrol vessels remained nearby while rescue tugs guided our damaged ships into their assigned cradles.

The Enduring River entered the largest repair frame shortly after the beginning of the Martian night cycle.

I stood on the bridge as the enormous docking clamps closed around our ruined hull.

For eleven years, I had known the sound of metal breaking.

I had almost forgotten the sound of metal being secured.

The clamps engaged with a series of deep, deliberate impacts, and the cruiser settled into the cradle. External stabilization systems activated, taking the strain from structural members that had been holding our ship together through little more than stubbornness and emergency welding. Engineering reported that the forward keel was no longer carrying its own weight. For the first time since Damaris Reach, I allowed the main reactor to be reduced to maintenance output.

The bridge became very quiet.

Audren stood beside me, looking toward the status displays.

"Captain," he said softly, "we can shut down the emergency pumps."

I stared at him.

"Are you certain?"

"The repair frame has established external atmosphere support. The pumps are no longer necessary."

I looked toward the engineering station, where a young officer was waiting for my authorization. She had been awake for nearly thirty hours, and her hands were trembling badly enough that she had difficulty operating the controls.

"Shut them down," I ordered.

The pumps stopped.

For a moment, nobody moved.

Then someone on the bridge began crying.

I did not turn to see who.

I was afraid that if I did, I would begin as well.

The first Terran civilian I met was Helena Ström, senior yardmaster of Anchorage Seventeen's emergency reconstruction division. She was a tall, broad-shouldered woman in her late fifties, with close-cropped gray hair, a reinforced left hand, and the permanently exhausted expression of someone responsible for keeping several thousand engineers alive around machinery that could kill them through a moment's carelessness. She wore dark industrial coveralls rather than a military uniform, and her identification badge had been repaired so many times that its original lettering was almost unreadable.

She met me at the docking collar accompanied by two engineers, a medical officer, and a Federal security representative.

"Captain Thenn?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Helena Ström. I'm responsible for getting these ships into a condition where they won't kill their crews."

I bowed slightly, following the formal custom of my people.

"Yardmaster, I am grateful for your assistance."

She glanced toward the Enduring River, examining the enormous scars across its hull.

"Don't thank me yet. I've only seen the outside."

"Our vessels have suffered extensive damage."

"I noticed."

"We have no funds with which to pay."

Ström looked toward the Federal security representative.

"Did nobody explain the emergency custody arrangement to him?"

The officer cleared his throat. "Captain Thenn has been informed that the Federation will provide immediate humanitarian assistance pending a political determination."

Ström nodded.

"Good. Then we'll worry about the politicians later."

She turned back toward me.

"Captain, your ship is falling apart. I've got engineers waiting, medical teams ready, and a fabrication department that's going to spend the next several days complaining about your spare parts. What I need from you is permission to get started."

I hesitated.

"Yardmaster, our reactors and propulsion systems are of Veyran design. Your engineers may not be familiar with them."

"Then your engineers can teach mine."

"Our technical documentation is incomplete."

"Then we'll make new documentation."

"Some of our equipment is nearly a century old."

Ström looked up at the battered cruiser again.

"Captain, half the machinery that rebuilt Earth was older than that."

She studied me for a moment before asking a question I had not expected.

"Have you eaten?"

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

Audren answered for me.

"Not properly, Yardmaster."

Ström nodded as though this confirmed something she had already suspected.

"Right. Medical screening first, food afterward, and then we can argue about your engines."

I followed her through the docking collar, past armed security personnel and enormous pressure doors, into a station that smelled of hot machinery, disinfectant, recycled air, and something unfamiliar being cooked in large quantities.

Behind us, the Enduring River rested inside its cradle.

For the first time in eleven years, nobody was shooting at her.

I would later learn that the Federation had already opened an investigation into our arrival, that the Federal Security Office was attempting to reconstruct our transit route, and that Imperial representatives had demanded access to our navigation archives before we had even finished docking. I would learn that Commodore Rourke had placed our ships under armed protection as much to keep foreign intelligence services away from us as to prevent us from becoming a threat. I would also learn that several Terran analysts were deeply concerned by the damage patterns across our hulls, particularly the extraordinary precision with which our enemies had disabled sensors, communications, and propulsion without destroying the vessels themselves.

But I knew none of that when I entered the anchorage canteen.

I knew only that my people were hungry.

The Terrans had prepared food for thousands.

They had established separate dietary stations after consulting our surviving medical officers, adjusted the temperature and humidity of several accommodation decks for Veyran physiology, and provided warm mineral baths for crew members suffering from prolonged dehydration. Their cooks were exhausted, their medical personnel overworked, and their security officers plainly suspicious of us. Nobody pretended that our arrival was convenient. Yet food continued arriving, beds were assigned, and the wounded were carried into treatment areas without anyone asking whether they possessed money.

I sat at a long table beside Audren and watched my surviving officers eat.

Across from us, Pell was examining a bowl of Terran vegetable stew with profound suspicion. A young human technician sitting beside him attempted to explain the ingredients, but the translation software struggled with several of the names. Pell finally took a cautious mouthful, considered the flavor, and nodded solemnly.

"It is warm," he declared.

The technician smiled.

"That's generally the idea."

Pell took another mouthful.

"It is also good."

"Well, don't sound so surprised."

For the first time in longer than I could remember, I heard laughter from my crew.

It was a small sound.

A few exhausted sailors laughing over unfamiliar food in a heavily guarded military repair station.

But it was real.

And I had forgotten how much that mattered.

Later that night, Helena Ström visited the temporary quarters assigned to me. She carried a maintenance tablet, two cups of hot tea, and a preliminary engineering assessment that had clearly been assembled by people who had not slept enough. She placed the tablet on the table between us and activated a holographic model of the Enduring River. Red damage indicators covered nearly half the ship, while several structural sections flashed amber to indicate that further inspection was required.

"Your keel is badly compromised," she began. "The port engine frame needs rebuilding, your forward pressure bulkheads are beyond repair, and three of your main weapon mounts are unsafe to operate. We've also found evidence of repeated thermal stress in your reactor cooling system."

I nodded.

"Our engineers have been compensating for those failures for years."

"They've done a remarkable job."

I looked at her sharply.

Ström noticed.

"I'm serious, Captain. I've seen ships with a tenth of this damage break apart during ordinary docking operations. Your people kept this vessel operational through conditions that should have killed it several times over."

I studied the hologram.

"Then you believe she can be repaired?"

Ström hesitated.

That frightened me more than anything she had said.

"She can be made safe," she answered. "Whether we can return her to combat condition depends on what we find when we open the damaged sections. Some of your structural components will have to be fabricated from scratch. Your reactor may need a complete rebuild. And I won't authorize anyone to put a crew back aboard until I'm satisfied the ship can survive another transit."

"How long?"

"Too early to say."

"We may not have much time."

She looked at me.

"Because of the people chasing you?"

"Yes."

Ström sat down opposite me.

For several seconds, she said nothing.

Then she activated another display.

It showed the damage patterns across our seven surviving ships. Sensor arrays had been severed with extraordinary precision. Engine assemblies had been struck at vulnerable points that suggested detailed knowledge of their internal construction. Communications equipment had been destroyed while adjacent crew compartments remained largely intact. Several vessels carried strange scars where their armor had been cut open and resealed, apparently by machines whose tools did not match anything in our technical records.

Ström studied the images.

"Captain, I've worked on damaged warships for thirty-two years. I've seen pirate attacks, reactor failures, ammunition explosions, and the aftermath of Imperial weapons testing."

She enlarged the image of our ruined port engine.

"I've never seen anything quite like this."

"The Sorynth are exceptionally precise."

"Yes. That's what worries me."

I frowned.

"Why?"

She looked toward the display.

"Because whoever did this knew exactly how to stop your ships without destroying them."

"That is how they fight."

"I understand that."

She enlarged another image, this one showing a damaged navigation array recovered from the Grave Current.

"Your engineers recorded an unusual signal immediately before this system failed. The same signal appears in the logs of five other ships. Your fire-control software classified it as an enemy targeting pulse."

"It is."

"Perhaps."

I looked at her.

"Yardmaster?"

Ström folded her arms.

"I'm not a signals specialist, Captain. But I know enough to recognize when something doesn't fit. These pulses continued after your ships were disabled. Some continued for hours. A targeting system doesn't normally need to keep telling a dead engine that it's being targeted."

"The Sorynth use distributed targeting networks. Their machines communicate continuously."

"That may explain it."

She hesitated.

"Or it may not."

I studied the unfamiliar waveforms on the display.

For eleven years, we had learned to fear those signals.

They appeared before our ships lost their engines.

Before our navigation systems failed.

Before our communications went silent.

Before the recovery frames came to carry away our crews.

Every Veyran officer knew what they meant.

Death.

Capture.

Disappearance.

Yet Ström was looking at them as though they were an engineering problem.

"What do you believe they are?" I asked.

"I don't know."

She turned off the display.

"But I've asked our signals department to take a closer look."

I sat quietly for a moment.

Then I asked the question that had been troubling me since our arrival.

"Why are you helping us?"

Ström looked surprised.

"Because your ships needed help."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

"Your government may suffer political consequences. Your military has accepted unknown vessels into a heavily defended industrial region. Your engineers are using resources that could repair your own warships. You have no reason to believe we can repay you."

Ström considered my words before leaning back in her chair.

"Captain, Earth nearly died once. Not in some ancient legend. Our grandparents' grandparents lived through the aftermath. Whole countries disappeared. Cities drowned. People starved while warehouses full of food sat behind armed checkpoints because nobody could agree who owned it."

She looked toward the small window overlooking the repair cradle.

"We remember what happens when everyone decides someone else's survival isn't their problem."

I said nothing.

"Don't misunderstand me," she continued. "The Federation isn't charitable because we're particularly good people. Our government will want answers. Our intelligence services will examine every piece of equipment you brought here. The Navy will insist on knowing whether you've led an enemy fleet toward Sol. And if your pursuers come through that gate, our soldiers will kill them before they reach these yards."

She turned back toward me.

"But none of that changes the fact that your people needed food and your ships needed repairs."

I looked down at the cooling tea between us.

For a long time, neither of us spoke.

Then Ström rose.

"Get some sleep, Captain. Tomorrow we're opening your reactor."

She reached the door before stopping.

"One more thing."

"Yes?"

"Those signals."

I looked up.

"Have you ever tried transmitting one back?"

"Our signals officers did, years ago. They repeated a recorded pulse during a withdrawal. A Sorynth vessel changed course toward them, and the officers broke off before they could try again."

"Did it fire?"

"Not before they escaped. Our people could not tell whether it had reacted to the pulse or detected their transmitter. They recommended further tests, but the equipment and ships they needed were lost in the next campaign."

Ström considered that. "Could I see their report?"

"I will ask Audren to find it."

"Thank you, Captain." She paused at the door. "Your officers may have noticed something the rest of us missed."

I remained seated for a long time afterward, listening to the distant industrial sounds of the repair anchorage. Beyond the window, enormous cranes moved across the wounded hull of the Enduring River, and Terran engineers were already cutting away the first damaged armor sections. Welding arcs flashed against the darkness, illuminating the ship's scars one by one.

I thought about the Sorynth.

I thought about the twenty-seven inhabited systems we had lost, the fleets that had disappeared, and the millions of dead whose names filled our memorial archives.

And I thought about the strange signals that had accompanied every battle.

For eleven years, the signals had arrived with fear and loss. Our officers had questioned them before; I had let the demands of survival make their questions feel less urgent.

Later, I received a copy of a Federal analyst's report and spoke with her about the work she had begun that night. At a secured intelligence facility, she had compared our officers' recordings with the pulses from six separate engagements, aligning them against the times of the Sorynth attacks. At first, the patterns appeared to be ordinary distributed fire-control traffic, as our officers had reported.

Then she noticed something peculiar.

The signals did not change when the Sorynth switched targets.

They changed when the Veyran ships transmitted.

She checked the recordings again, comparing the pulses against diplomatic hails, emergency warnings, and automated weapons-control traffic. Each time the Veyrans transmitted, the Sorynth network responded with a slightly different sequence. The responses were consistent across multiple engagements, separated by years and dozens of light-years.

She isolated the earliest surviving recording in the archive, made before the first confirmed exchange of weapons fire. It preceded the disabling of our navigation buoys described in later patrol reports; it did not show what either side intended.

A pale Sorynth vessel approached a Veyran navigation buoy.

The buoy transmitted a standard territorial warning.

The Sorynth responded.

The Veyran patrol ship classified the response as a targeting lock.

Its captain ordered weapons free.

The first missiles launched.

According to her report, the analyst watched the sequence three times before contacting her supervisor. She later let me read a transcript of that conversation.

"Sir," she said, "I think we need to reconsider the first-contact report."

Her supervisor answered.

"What have you found?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then why are you calling?"

"Because I think the Veyrans may have fired on something that wasn't a weapon."

There was a pause.

"Are you certain?"

"No, sir."

"But I think somebody may have been trying to answer them."

The analyst did not know what had happened to our missing crews, or why our colonies had been emptied. Neither did I. A possible answer in an old recording did not bring any of them home.

That night, Mars turned beneath its immense orbital shipyards. Seven broken vessels rested in their repair cradles, and the surviving thousands of our people were being sheltered beneath Terran protection.

Beyond the authenticated lanes, we had to assume the Sorynth Veil was still searching for the fleet that had escaped.

The Terrans had given us sanctuary.

We did not yet understand what that might cost them.

End of opening installment.
