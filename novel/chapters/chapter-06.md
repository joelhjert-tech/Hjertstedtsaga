# Chapter 6: The Forgotten Road

## 1. Sightseeing

Kestrel had become busy enough that Joel could disappear in plain sight.

He preferred it that way.

Two days into another visit to Kestrel, he found Anton standing on an observation deck overlooking Kestrel Shield.

The new support station filled half the viewport.

Construction was still active around its outer sections. Tugs carried armor modules toward unfinished docking arms. Vigil frigates moved between patrol corridors. Civilian freighters queued beneath House-controlled traffic beacons.

Beyond them, the original Kestrel station glowed with habitation lights.

Anton held a cup of something unpleasant.

Joel walked beside him.

“We have been responsible for too many meetings recently.”

Anton looked at him.

“That sounds dangerous.”

Joel watched a mercenary destroyer leave one of the docks.

“Time for some sightseeing.”

Anton smiled immediately.

“Now that sounds better.”

Joel brought up a sector map.

Not the strategic command version.

A civilian navigation layer.

Kestrel sat at the center of a rapidly growing web of trade and mercenary traffic.

House protection had changed the routes.

Places that had once been too dangerous for regular shipping were reopening.

Minor stations were advertising contracts.

Old colonies were requesting survey crews.

Salvagers were returning to wreck fields abandoned years ago.

Young House officers were arriving from Vintergard looking for experience.

Independent mercenaries were coming because Kestrel had something rare:

work, repair facilities, reliable courts, and enough guns to make being cheated less likely.

That suited the House. Its wider identity had always been built around contracts, protection, arbitration, and controlled violence rather than ordinary territorial conquest.

Anton studied the map.

“What exactly are we sightseeing?”

Joel expanded the surrounding frontier.

“Everything.”

“That is not a destination.”

“It is a philosophy.”

Anton took another drink.

“You have been spending too much time with Lars.”

They entered Kestrel’s mercenary concourse without uniforms.

Not disguises.

Just ordinary clothes.

Joel wore a dark coat.

Anton wore something that failed completely at making him look harmless.

His size alone ruined the attempt.

They passed contract boards filled with offers.

Convoy escort.

Missing freighter investigation.

Anti-piracy patrol.

Mining claim protection.

Station security.

Survey expeditions.

Recovery work.

One contract offered an absurd payment for retrieving a navigation core from a wreck field.

Anton stopped.

“That one.”

Joel read it.

“No.”

“You did not finish reading.”

“It says seventy-three percent structural instability.”

“So?”

“It also says suspected reactor contamination.”

Anton nodded thoughtfully.

“Interesting.”

“No.”

They kept walking.

The biggest change was the people.

House cadets.

Young sworn-family officers.

Independent crews.

Former Ardent sailors.

Kestrel locals.

Vardos refugees who had become mechanics, dock workers, security officers, traders, and ship crews.

Even several minor foreign military observers were present openly now.

Nobody treated Kestrel like an abandoned station anymore.

It was becoming a frontier capital.

Not politically.

Culturally.

A place where people came because things happened there.

Joel noticed a group of young House officers near one of the contract boards.

They were arguing.

Loudly.

Anton noticed them too.

One wore a Winge family insignia.

Another carried a Skar branch badge.

Two more belonged to families Joel did not recognize immediately.

Anton leaned closer.

“Fresh ones.”

Joel nodded.

“Very.”

“They look confident.”

“They have never been shot at.”

Anton smiled.

“We can fix that.”

Joel looked at him.

“Professionally.”

“Of course.”

The young officers eventually noticed them.

Conversation stopped.

One of them straightened.

“High Marshal.”

Joel sighed.

“So much for sightseeing.”

The Winge cadet looked between Joel and Anton.

“Breach Commander.”

Anton looked offended.

“You said mine with less enthusiasm.”

The cadet immediately became terrified.

Joel rescued him.

“What are you doing?”

“Reviewing available contracts, sir.”

“Which one?”

The cadet pointed toward a small frontier job.

A mining settlement had lost contact with two cargo ships along a minor lane approach.

Nothing major.

No confirmed enemy.

No strategic importance.

Exactly the kind of work House von Hjertstedt had built its reputation on before Ardent made everyone think only in fleets.

Joel read the details.

“You want it?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Why?”

The cadet hesitated.

“For experience.”

Joel shook his head.

“Wrong answer.”

The group became uncomfortable.

Joel pointed toward the contract.

“People are missing.”

The cadet looked again.

Then understood.

“To find them.”

“And?”

“Bring them home if possible.”

Joel nodded.

“Better.”

House doctrine required proof, bounded escalation, and protection of civilian life rather than violence for its own sake. That remained true whether the House deployed three fleets or one frightened cadet crew.

Anton leaned toward Joel.

“You are ruining the fun.”

“I am preventing paperwork.”

“Same thing.”

Sera found them ten minutes later.

Mara was with her.

Sera looked at Joel.

“You said sightseeing.”

“Yes.”

“You are assigning missions.”

“No.”

The Winge cadet quietly said:

“He gave advice.”

Sera stared at Joel.

Joel pointed toward the cadet.

“See?”

Mara looked at Anton.

“And what did you do?”

Anton answered:

“Encouragement.”

One cadet muttered:

“He suggested boarding anything suspicious.”

Anton pointed at him.

“Context matters.”

Mara closed her eyes.

Sera looked at Joel.

“So where are you actually going?”

Joel brought the map up again.

He expanded beyond Kestrel’s immediate patrol zone.

Dozens of minor systems and stations appeared.

Some stable.

Some barely mapped.

Some outside meaningful major-power control.

The larger states claimed influence over many of them.

Few actually protected them.

Joel smiled slightly.

“Out there.”

Sera studied him.

“You are serious.”

“Yes.”

Anton understood now.

Not an inspection tour.

Not diplomacy.

Not another fleet campaign.

They were going back to the frontier.

Small ships.

Small stations.

Contracts.

People nobody powerful cared about yet.

The exact environment where House von Hjertstedt had always been strongest.

And now there was another reason.

Kestrel and Ardent had become the House’s first openly held frontier territory.

If they were going to send hundreds of young officers out from it—

Joel wanted to know what kind of frontier they were inheriting.

Anton finished his drink.

“So we take a cruiser?”

Joel shook his head.

“Too obvious.”

“Destroyer?”

“No.”

“Frigate?”

Joel smiled.

“Better.”

Anton grinned.

“Now I am interested.”

Sera stared at both brothers.

“You are going wandering around frontier space in one frigate.”

Joel nodded.

“With a small crew.”

Mara folded her arms.

“This will end badly.”

Anton looked almost delighted.

“That is why it qualifies as sightseeing.”

Joel turned toward the viewport.

Beyond Kestrel Shield, ships vanished into the lanes one after another.

Some toward Ardent.

Others toward territories nobody had bothered to defend properly in years.

Joel watched them go.

“Let us see what is out there.”

## 2. Four Brothers, One Frigate

Departure was scheduled for the next morning.

Joel intended to leave quietly.

That failed immediately.

He reached the assigned Vigil frigate and found Anton already aboard, arguing with the quartermaster about ammunition allocation.

“You do realize this is sightseeing,” Joel said.

Anton looked over.

“Yes.”

“Then why are you requesting breaching charges?”

“Sightseeing equipment.”

Joel kept walking.

He should have known that would not be the worst problem.

At the end of the docking collar stood Christer.

Behind him was Daniel.

Both carrying travel bags.

Joel stopped.

“No.”

Christer smiled.

“Yes.”

Daniel nodded toward the frigate.

“We heard you were going sightseeing.”

Anton appeared behind Joel.

“Excellent.”

Joel looked between them.

“You are supposed to be working.”

Christer shrugged.

“So are you.”

Daniel added:

“Technically you are High Marshal.”

“That is exactly why I can decide what I am doing.”

Daniel nodded.

“Then as senior officers, we have decided to accompany you.”

“That is not how rank works.”

Anton leaned toward Joel.

“It could be.”

The frigate commander looked increasingly uncomfortable.

She had expected to carry Joel and Anton.

That was already enough trouble.

Now four senior members of House von Hjertstedt were standing in her embarkation bay.

Joel noticed her expression.

“Captain?”

She straightened.

“Sir.”

“You may say it.”

She hesitated.

“This is excessive.”

Anton laughed.

Christer looked offended.

Daniel nodded.

“Correct assessment.”

Joel sighed.

The captain continued.

“With respect, High Marshal, this ship is configured for patrol, not family reunions.”

Joel looked toward the others.

“She has a point.”

Christer lifted his bag.

“I packed lightly.”

Daniel did the same.

Anton looked down at his own equipment case.

Joel stared.

“What is in that?”

Anton answered:

“Essentials.”

“That case weighs more than a cadet.”

“Some cadets are small.”

Sera arrived before Joel could throw anyone off the ship.

Mara came with her.

Sera looked at the four brothers.

Then at the frigate.

Then back at Joel.

“No.”

Joel frowned.

“What now?”

“You said a small crew.”

“It is.”

“You now have four Hjertstedts aboard.”

Anton said:

“That is still only four people.”

Mara looked toward him.

“Four people who have personally caused more fleet-level complications than most governments.”

Christer smiled.

“That sounds exaggerated.”

Mara answered:

“It is not.”

Daniel stepped closer to the tactical board.

“We are not taking command.”

The frigate captain visibly relaxed.

Joel immediately added:

“Unless required.”

The relaxation disappeared.

Sera laughed.

The mission itself remained simple.

Visit several minor stations.

Review the frontier routes.

Observe mercenary traffic.

Check how Kestrel’s influence was spreading beyond the official patrol zone.

Talk to local commanders without arriving behind a wall of capital ships.

Small-scale work.

Real work.

The kind of work that had built House legitimacy long before Ardent.

Joel wanted the trip to feel ordinary.

Christer ruined that in less than ten minutes.

He opened the contract feed.

“There is a convoy escort paying badly.”

Joel looked over.

“Then why are you interested?”

“Because someone is hiding something.”

Daniel checked another file.

“This mining colony has requested security assistance three times in six months.”

Anton leaned over his shoulder.

“That sounds promising.”

Joel looked at all three.

“We are sightseeing.”

Christer nodded.

“Yes.”

Daniel added:

“Through problems.”

Anton smiled.

“Best kind.”

The frigate captain finally interrupted.

“High Marshal.”

Joel turned.

“Yes?”

“If all four of you are coming, I need command boundaries established before departure.”

Joel approved immediately.

“Good.”

She brought up the bridge authority map.

“I remain ship captain.”

“Yes.”

“Normal navigation, watch rotation, weapons readiness, and crew discipline remain mine.”

“Yes.”

“None of you issue direct orders to my crew unless I transfer authority or there is an emergency.”

Anton opened his mouth.

Joel raised a hand.

“Agreed.”

Christer nodded.

Daniel nodded too.

The captain looked toward Anton.

Anton sighed.

“Agreed.”

She continued.

“If combat occurs?”

Joel answered:

“You command the ship.”

“And you?”

“I advise unless formally taking flag authority becomes necessary.”

The captain studied him.

“That is very carefully worded.”

Joel smiled.

“It is accurate.”

She nodded once.

“Accepted.”

That settled it.

House doctrine placed command, legal authority, and fleet roles inside formal boundaries for exactly this reason: too much rank aboard one hull could become more dangerous than the enemy if nobody knew who held the bridge.

Departure began twenty minutes later.

The frigate detached from Kestrel Shield.

Traffic control cleared the lane.

Joel stood on the bridge beside Anton while Christer and Daniel occupied observer positions behind them.

The captain looked back.

“Destination?”

Joel brought up the frontier map.

Several small systems appeared.

A repair station.

A mining colony.

A relay settlement.

A cluster of independent habitats.

Nothing important enough for a major power to care about.

Exactly what Joel wanted.

He pointed toward the first route.

“There.”

Daniel looked at the marker.

“That place has no strategic value.”

Joel nodded.

“Correct.”

Christer frowned.

“No major contract.”

“Correct.”

Anton looked interested.

“No warfleet.”

“Hopefully.”

The three of them looked at Joel.

Joel smiled.

“That is why we are going.”

The frigate entered the lane.

Kestrel disappeared behind them.

For the first time in months, none of the brothers commanded a major fleet.

No strategic chamber.

No Seraph invasion.

No occupation government.

No Sol delegation.

Just one frigate moving toward places that might never appear on a major power’s map.

Christer leaned back.

“This feels strange.”

Daniel looked toward him.

“What does?”

“Not having several thousand ships nearby.”

Anton smiled.

“I find it refreshing.”

Joel looked at him.

“You requested breaching charges.”

“I said refreshing, not irresponsible.”

The captain stared forward very hard.

Nobody on the bridge wanted to laugh first.

Then Daniel did.

Christer followed.

Anton joined.

Joel tried not to.

Failed.

The first frontier station appeared on sensors hours later.

Small.

Old.

Independent.

Its transponder had been repaired enough times that the identification packet was mostly maintenance warnings.

The station hailed them.

“Unknown Hjertstedt frigate, state business.”

The captain looked toward Joel.

Joel shook his head.

“Your ship.”

She answered normally.

“Patrol visit. No warrant. No hostile intent.”

A pause.

Then:

“Understood. Docking available.”

Nobody there seemed to know who was aboard.

Joel smiled.

Perfect.

Anton noticed.

“You are enjoying this.”

“Yes.”

Christer stood.

“So are we going in anonymously?”

Joel considered.

“As much as possible.”

Daniel looked at the three of them.

“That should last approximately nine minutes.”

Anton shook his head.

“Optimist.”

The frigate approached the station.

Four brothers watched the frontier come closer.

Not as conquerors.

Not as admirals.

For the moment—

just travelers looking around.

## 3. The Rust Bucket

The first frontier station turned out to be exactly what Joel had hoped for.

Old.

Busy.

Poor enough that everyone repaired everything twice.

Independent enough that nobody wanted a flag hanging over them.

And ordinary enough that four von Hjertstedts arriving aboard a Vigil frigate immediately ruined the experiment.

It took six hours.

By then, half the station knew.

By the next morning, everyone did.

The frigate captain found Joel in a maintenance café with Anton, Christer, and Daniel.

“We have a problem.”

Joel looked up.

“What kind?”

“The local traffic authority has reassigned three patrol routes because they believe your presence means House protection has expanded here.”

Christer winced.

Daniel muttered:

“That was fast.”

The captain continued.

“Two mercenary companies cancelled departure because they assume we are inspecting contracts.”

Anton smiled.

“Efficient.”

Joel looked at him.

“This is exactly what I did not want.”

The House had become too recognizable.

A proper Vigil frigate was not simply transport anymore.

It was a declaration.

House ships carried doctrine, reputation, warrants, and political meaning with them.

Joel leaned back.

“We send the frigate home.”

The captain blinked.

“Sir?”

“Back to Kestrel.”

Anton slowly smiled.

Christer immediately became suspicious.

Daniel closed his eyes.

“No.”

Joel looked toward him.

“I have not said anything yet.”

“I know you.”

Christer pointed at the docking map.

“What exactly do you intend to travel in?”

Joel opened the civilian sales registry.

Anton moved closer.

“Oh.”

Daniel looked.

Then became visibly unhappy.

Most ships listed were old commercial hulls.

Courier craft.

Converted cargo boats.

Mining transports.

A few armed freighters.

Nothing House-built.

Nothing impressive.

Nothing that would cause a station commander to panic because a High Marshal had arrived.

Joel scrolled.

Stopped.

Anton leaned over his shoulder.

“That one.”

Christer looked at it.

“No.”

Daniel looked at it.

“Absolutely not.”

Joel smiled.

The ship was a small independent long-range transport that had once been a cargo courier.

Once.

Probably several owners ago.

Its hull plating consisted of at least five visibly different repair batches.

The port engine nacelle had been replaced with one from another production series.

One maneuvering thruster was technically listed as “intermittent.”

Its sensor mast leaned slightly.

There was corrosion around one docking collar.

The registry carried seventeen maintenance warnings.

And somebody had painted a faded blue stripe along the side badly enough that it stopped halfway across an armor patch.

Anton looked delighted.

“It is perfect.”

The seller was named Pell Orven.

He had the expression of a man who had spent thirty years selling ships to people who could not afford better.

He met them in Dock Forty-One.

The vessel sat behind him.

Up close, it looked worse.

Christer stared upward.

“That cannot be spaceworthy.”

Pell looked offended.

“She is certified.”

Daniel checked the hull registry.

“Barely.”

“Certified is certified.”

Anton walked underneath the ship.

A small piece of insulation fell beside him.

He looked down.

Then up.

“I like her.”

Joel asked:

“How much?”

Pell gave the price.

Joel stared at him.

“That is robbery.”

Pell shrugged.

“You arrived aboard a House frigate.”

Joel looked toward Anton.

“This is your fault.”

“My fault?”

“You look wealthy.”

Anton spread his arms.

“I am wearing a jacket.”

“A very expensive jacket.”

Christer walked toward the engine assembly.

“This thing has mismatched reactor shielding.”

Pell nodded proudly.

“Redundancy.”

Daniel looked at him.

“That is not what redundancy means.”

They spent an hour inspecting it.

The main drive worked.

Mostly.

Life support was ugly but reliable.

The reactor had been rebuilt twice.

Navigation was old.

Sensors were worse.

The cargo bay had enough space for equipment, supplies, and a small ground vehicle.

The ship possessed two legal defensive gun mounts.

Only one currently worked.

Anton discovered an old concealed boarding locker beneath the cargo deck.

Joel looked at Pell.

“You did not mention that.”

Pell shrugged.

“Previous owner.”

“What did they do?”

“Salvage.”

Daniel looked inside.

Magnetic clamps.

Cutting gear mounts.

Old restraint brackets.

Christer nodded.

“Pirates.”

Pell looked offended again.

“Salvagers.”

Anton smiled.

“Very aggressive salvagers.”

The universe was full of ships like this: patched commercial hulls, converted freighters, improvised escorts, and vessels held together by crews who knew every repair personally. Sci-Fi Universe Outline

Joel liked that.

Nobody would see this ship and think High Marshal.

Nobody would see it and call a fleet.

Nobody would assume territorial expansion.

They might assume bankruptcy.

That was useful.

Joel bought it.

Pell stared at the transfer confirmation.

“You actually want it?”

Joel looked at him.

“You were selling it.”

“Yes, but…”

Pell stopped.

Anton laughed.

Even the seller had expected negotiation to fail.

Christer looked toward Joel.

“We are not taking this beyond local space.”

Joel nodded.

“Correct.”

Daniel relaxed slightly.

Joel continued:

“Until we service it.”

Daniel’s relief vanished.

The Vigil frigate departed that afternoon.

Its captain looked almost too happy.

She stood at the boarding collar before leaving.

“High Marshal.”

“Yes?”

“I am formally recording that I advised against this.”

“Good.”

“I am also informing Kestrel Command where you are.”

Joel frowned.

“That seems unnecessary.”

“It is absolutely necessary.”

Anton nodded toward her.

“Good captain.”

Joel ignored him.

The frigate undocked and burned toward the return lane.

For the first time since leaving Kestrel, the four brothers had no House warship nearby.

Only the rust bucket.

Christer watched the frigate disappear.

“This feels irresponsible.”

Anton looked at the battered transport.

“Now it feels like sightseeing.”

Before they started work, Daniel laid a thermal survey on the cargo deck.

“A private challenge,” he said. “Coolant loop within tolerance on the first certified run.”

Anton folded his arms.

“Against?”

“Your gun passing the same inspection without a lecture.”

“Name the bottle.”

“Settle the eastern one first.”

“That was disputed.”

Christer looked up from the sensor parts.

“Only by the debtor.”

Joel took the survey slate.

“The mechanics certify both. Nobody grades his own work.”

They spent two days making the ship trustworthy enough to travel.

Not good.

Trustworthy.

Daniel took engineering.

Christer rebuilt parts of the sensor suite using commercial components purchased from three different shops.

Anton repaired the defensive gun mount, then quietly improved it.

Joel discovered this and removed half the modifications.

Anton put some back.

Joel removed them again.

They compromised.

Eventually.

The station mechanics began watching.

Then helping.

Not because of rank.

Because four apparently wealthy idiots had bought the worst ship in the dock and were genuinely repairing it themselves.

That earned respect faster than uniforms did.

A mechanic named Tessa Hal offered Joel a replacement heat exchanger.

“It is ugly.”

Joel looked at the ship.

“That seems appropriate.”

On the third morning, the transport powered up.

The reactor coughed.

The lights dimmed.

Then stabilized.

Daniel stared at the engineering display.

“I hate this vessel.”

Christer answered from sensors:

“I hate the sensor array.”

Anton called from weapons:

“I like the gun.”

Joel sat in the pilot seat.

“That worries me most.”

Tessa's signed checks lay between them. The coolant loop had passed on its first run. The gun had required a long conversation about power draw.

Anton read the sheet twice.

“I am having the eastern bottle sent to Kestrel for you.”

Daniel looked over.

“You accept the bridge ruling?”

“No. I accept that you will never shut up.”

“That was always the strongest part of my claim.”

Anton sent the instruction to his cellar keeper, with Daniel named as the new owner. Christer recorded the transfer beside the original wager. Joel took the gun's certified limits to the pilot seat.

They had also reached another problem.

The ship needed a name.

Anton suggested Glorious Mistake.

Daniel suggested Bad Decision.

Christer suggested Inspection Failure.

Joel looked at the faded blue stripe outside through the dock camera.

Then at the rusted hull.

“No.”

He entered a name into the registry.

WAYWARD HOUND

Anton stared.

“That is surprisingly respectable.”

Joel smiled.

“Give it time.”

They undocked.

No escort.

No House transponder.

No ceremonial identification.

Just four brothers aboard an old independent transport heading deeper into the frontier.

The station controller called:

“Wayward Hound, you are cleared.”

Joel pushed the throttles forward.

The port engine vibrated.

Daniel immediately said:

“That noise is new.”

Anton looked toward him.

“Bad?”

“I do not know yet.”

Christer laughed.

Joel watched the stars shift ahead.

Behind them stood Kestrel and Ardent.

House banners.

New territory.

Fleet power.

Responsibility.

Ahead lay stations that barely appeared on strategic maps.

Minor colonies.

Independent crews.

Old lanes.

Bad contracts.

People who might never have seen a House warship.

Exactly what Joel wanted.

Anton leaned against the console.

“So.”

Joel looked toward him.

“So?”

“Where first?”

Joel expanded the frontier map.

A dozen questionable destinations appeared.

He smiled.

“Somewhere nobody expects us.”

## 4. The Forgotten Map

The Wayward Hound found work immediately.

Not glamorous work.

Survey work.

The kind of contracts larger mercenary outfits ignored because there was little money and even less glory.

A mining colony wanted its outer approach rescanned after three navigation buoys stopped reporting.

A farming habitat needed someone to verify whether an old relay beacon was still transmitting usable lane data.

A refinery consortium wanted a debris field mapped before reopening a shipping corridor.

Joel accepted all three.

Anton stared at the contract list.

“We used to capture capital ships.”

Joel checked the fuel estimate.

“Now we get paid to look at rocks.”

Anton considered.

“I preferred the capital ships.”

Daniel called from engineering.

“Rocks are less likely to shoot back.”

Christer answered from the sensor station.

“You have just guaranteed they will.”

The first job took them six hours.

The Wayward Hound moved slowly along the edge of a mining system while Christer rebuilt a local navigation picture from commercial sensors, passive observations, and old colonial charts.

Joel flew.

Anton complained.

Daniel monitored the engines because nobody trusted them.

The missing buoys turned out not to be sabotage.

A micrometeor storm had destroyed one and pushed the others far enough off position that their directional broadcasts were useless.

Simple.

They recovered one.

Marked the second for salvage.

Deployed a temporary beacon.

The colony paid them.

Not much.

But enough for fuel, food, and two replacement coolant pumps.

Joel looked pleased.

Anton looked insulted by the amount.

“That barely buys ammunition.”

“We are not buying ammunition.”

Anton looked at him.

“We should always buy ammunition.”

The second contract became more interesting.

An agricultural station called Morrow’s Rest had inherited navigation records older than most of its current population.

The station administrator met them personally.

“You are surveyors?”

Joel nodded.

“Today.”

She looked at the four men.

“You do not look like surveyors.”

Anton smiled.

“What do surveyors look like?”

“Tired.”

Daniel answered:

“Give us time.”

The administrator handed over a corrupted navigation archive.

Most of it was useless.

Then Christer found something buried underneath modern route information.

An old gate designation.

No current traffic.

No recognized authentication.

No confirmed operational status.

Just coordinates.

They went to look.

The gate floated at the edge of a dead system.

Ancient.

Dark.

Far larger than anything the local colony needed.

The Wayward Hound approached carefully.

Joel kept them outside any plausible activation envelope.

Anton stared through the forward display.

“Dead?”

Christer studied the sensors.

“Maybe.”

Daniel shook his head.

“No.”

Joel looked over.

“What?”

“Power residue.”

“How old?”

“Cannot tell.”

Christer adjusted the scan.

“There is internal activity.”

Anton became interested.

Joel immediately said:

“We are not entering it.”

Anton looked offended.

“I said nothing.”

“You were about to.”

“I was thinking.”

“That was enough.”

They recorded everything.

Gate geometry.

Energy readings.

Local debris.

Approach vectors.

Old navigation markers.

Any remaining transponder fragments.

Then Joel created a separate archive.

Not part of the colony survey.

Not yet.

UNVERIFIED VARANTHI INFRASTRUCTURE — LOCAL RECORD 001

Christer looked at him.

“You are building a map.”

Joel nodded.

“Someone should.”

That became the second purpose of the trip.

Everywhere they went, they surveyed more than the contract required.

Not secretly against clients.

Simply broader.

Old gates.

Dead relays.

Abandoned stations.

Forgotten fuel depots.

Collapsed mining habitats.

Military structures from governments that no longer existed.

Drifting wrecks whose registries belonged to states none of them recognized.

Sometimes nothing remained except an old navigation beacon still repeating the same message after a century.

Anton called them ghosts.

Joel called them infrastructure.

Daniel called them maintenance nightmares.

Christer recorded everything.

The Known Reach was officially mapped.

The frontier was not.

Official charts showed safe lanes and active stations.

They did not show everything between them.

And sometimes what had been marked dead was merely unattended.

Three weeks passed.

The Wayward Hound accumulated scars.

And modifications.

Daniel replaced the unreliable maneuvering thruster.

Christer mounted a secondhand long-range sensor array beneath the old cargo spine.

Anton repaired both defensive gun mounts and somehow added another armored weapons locker.

Joel discovered it.

He stared at Anton.

“You promised.”

“I promised not to install another gun.”

“That is a locker.”

“Correct.”

“What is inside?”

Anton smiled.

Joel decided not to ask.

The ship remained ugly.

But increasingly capable.

They worked wherever they found jobs.

A moon colony paid them to survey an ice-mining approach.

They discovered an abandoned patrol station beyond the moon.

A trade habitat hired them to map pirate hiding locations inside an asteroid belt.

They found an old Varanthi navigation pillar embedded in one of the larger rocks.

A small religious settlement wanted confirmation that a nearby lane was safe after strange drive blooms appeared.

The blooms were harmless commercial traffic.

But beyond the normal approach, Christer found a dead station.

No power.

No bodies.

No registry.

The station had been stripped almost clean.

Joel marked it.

UNKNOWN STATION — RECORD 014

Anton looked at the growing archive.

“How many now?”

Christer answered.

“Fourteen stations.”

Not magically.

But disturbingly fast.

Anton looked at the Thyrak.

“How long would one of you take?”

The commander answered:

“Longer.”

Anton smiled.

That clearly bothered him.

The Seraph subject returned immediately.

The Talyrn intelligence officer looked at the medical data.

Then at Joel.

“You said the Seraph studied your family.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Silence.

Joel chose his answer carefully.

“They have failed to convert members of our bloodline.”

The room changed.

The Talyrn commander leaned forward.

“Failed?”

“Repeatedly.”

“Why?”

“We do not fully know.”

He was not giving them everything he knew. The failed conversions were established; the rest was not for this room.

Christer added:

“The recovered trials record repeated failures.”

Daniel continued:

“They do not record a working explanation. That is what we can say from those files.”

Anton smiled without humor.

“They dislike us.”

The Talyrn civilian official looked between the four recovering brothers.

Fast healing.

Extreme augmentation tolerance.

Limiter release.

Seraph conversion failure.

A hereditary military House commanding hidden fleets beyond an unreachable home system.

Her expression became much more cautious.

The debrief had started with the Talyrn wondering whether House von Hjertstedt was legitimate.

Now another question was forming.

What, exactly, had the Known Reach been hiding on the other side of Gate Seventeen?

Joel saw it in their faces.

He smiled slightly.

“Now you understand why introductions take time.”

## 13. The Veyr-Khal

The Veyr-Khal arrived twelve hours later.

By then, all four brothers were walking.

That created its own diplomatic problem.

The Talyrn physicians had expected Joel to remain under assisted recovery for days.

Instead, he entered the meeting chamber under his own power.

Anton followed with his damaged shoulder immobilized beneath a temporary brace.

Christer still moved stiffly.

Daniel had stopped bleeding from the ear and was already complaining about not being allowed near the Wayward Hound.

The Talyrn medical delegation watched them enter.

One physician whispered:

“That is unreasonable.”

Anton heard him.

“Yes.”

Joel gave him a look.

Anton stopped smiling.

Mostly.

The Veyr-Khal waited alone at the far end of the chamber.

No ceremonial guard.

No throne.

No display of wealth.

Only an older woman wearing dark military clothing beneath a light armored command mantle.

Tall.

Grey hair cut close.

One artificial eye.

Both hands visibly scarred.

Her name translated approximately as:

Veyr-Khal Ortheva Sair.

Supreme military coordinator of the Talyrn Continuance.

She studied the four brothers before speaking.

“High Marshal.”

“Veyr-Khal.”

Joel stopped opposite her.

Ortheva looked toward Anton.

“The one who challenged the war boss.”

Anton nodded.

“That description is acceptable.”

Then Christer.

“Distributed tactical analysis identified your sensor work during the boarding.”

Christer inclined his head.

Then Daniel.

“You redirected a hostile capital ship’s engineering network while its reactor containment was failing.”

Daniel answered:

“It was poorly maintained.”

Ortheva stared.

“It was an Garooth flagship.”

“Yes.”

“That is assumed.”

Finally she looked back toward Joel.

“You ordered four senior officers into an enemy capital vessel.”

Joel corrected her.

“Three.”

Her eyebrow moved slightly.

“I ordered myself.”

Ortheva considered that.

“I am uncertain whether that improves the decision.”

“It does not.”

For the first time, she almost smiled.

The tactical table activated.

Ardent appeared.

Then Kestrel.

Then Gate Seventeen.

Ortheva pointed toward Ardent.

“You seized this state following Seraph involvement.”

“Yes.”

“You retained its strategic stations.”

“Yes.”

“You are expanding their defenses.”

“Yes.”

“You also maintain another affiliated territory here.”

Kestrel expanded.

“Yes.”

“And beyond all of this lies your hidden homeland.”

Joel did not answer.

Ortheva noticed.

“You protect its location.”

“I protect access.”

“There is a difference?”

“Yes.”

She accepted that without pressing.

Talyrn officers understood protected infrastructure.

Then she asked the real question.

“What happens when Gate Seventeen becomes stable?”

Silence.

Anton looked toward Joel.

Christer remained still.

Daniel studied the gate projection.

Joel answered:

“That depends on you.”

Ortheva's artificial eye narrowed.

“Explain.”

“You are not a lost colony.”

“No.”

“You are not asking to be rescued.”

“No.”

“You are a sovereign civilization.”

“Yes.”

“Then we do not cross your gate with fleets because we discovered you exist.”

That answer clearly surprised her.

Ortheva asked:

“What if the Throne Imperium demands access?”

“No.”

“The Hiigaran Restoration?”

“No.”

“Terra?”

“No.”

“What gives you the authority to deny them?”

Joel smiled faintly.

“Nothing.”

A pause.

“I simply will not give them the route.”

Ortheva stared at him for several seconds.

“That is an unusual diplomatic position.”

“It has worked for Vintergard.”

Anton smiled.

Daniel muttered:

“Do not encourage him.”

Ortheva ignored them.

“And House von Hjertstedt?”

Joel looked toward Gate Seventeen.

“If you invite us, we come.”

“If you do not, we remain outside.”

“And if another Garooth convergence attacks?”

Joel answered immediately.

“We ask.”

Ortheva's expression hardened.

“And if we cannot answer?”

That was more difficult.

Joel considered carefully.

“Then we decide whether waiting for permission means watching humans die.”

There.

The fault line.

Ortheva nodded slowly.

“The Talyrn would likely make the same decision.”

“I suspected that.”

“Which should concern both of us.”

“Yes.”

She changed the display.

Footage from the battle appeared.

Talyrn ships covering damaged formations.

The captured Garooth flagship ramming the second capital.

The Continuance counterattack.

Ortheva pointed toward the moment Joel ordered the Talyrn cutter away.

“My officers found this decision interesting.”

Anton leaned toward Christer.

“That is diplomatic language for stupid.”

Christer whispered:

“Quiet.”

Ortheva continued.

“You refused recovery assistance because the cutter had greater value to the wider formation.”

“Yes.”

“You then risked four people whose strategic value, by your own political structure, is enormous.”

“Yes.”

“That appears contradictory.”

Joel shook his head.

“No.”

“Explain.”

“The cutter belonged to your war.”

“And you?”

“We chose to enter it.”

The room went quiet.

Joel continued.

“Your people did not owe us their lives because our names happen to matter somewhere else.”

Ortheva watched him carefully.

That answer landed directly inside Talyrn philosophy.

Strength created obligation.

It did not erase everyone else's value.

Then Ortheva opened the Seraph file.

Her demeanor changed completely.

“You claim the Seraph cannot successfully convert your bloodline.”

Joel answered:

“I claim they have repeatedly failed.”

“You know more than that.”

Joel said nothing.

Ortheva did not push.

Instead:

“If that resistance could be understood, it might save millions.”

Anton’s expression became colder.

Daniel stopped looking at the tactical display.

Christer watched Joel.

Joel answered carefully.

“Our family has been dissected, imprisoned, tested, and harvested by people trying to understand exactly that.”

Ortheva became still.

“Project Shepherd.”

“Yes.”

“Then I understand your caution.”

Joel nodded.

“We can cooperate.”

“Medical exchange?”

“Limited.”

“Seraph samples?”

“Yes.”

“Host-rejection data?”

“Case by case.”

“Your blood?”

“No.”

Immediate.

Absolute.

Ortheva accepted it.

“Fair.”

She dismissed the Seraph layer.

Gate Seventeen remained.

“I have one final question.”

Joel waited.

“Do you intend to return to the Known Reach?”

“Yes.”

“With knowledge of us?”

“Yes.”

“Will you report our existence?”

Joel looked toward his brothers.

Then back at her.

“To my House.”

“Only your House?”

“For now.”

Ortheva studied him.

“Why?”

Joel answered:

“Because the first thing the Known Reach will see when it learns about you is territory, fleets, gates, and leverage.”

He pointed toward the battle record.

“That is not what I saw first.”

Ortheva asked:

“What did you see?”

Joel looked toward the image of Talyrn frigates covering a crippled cruiser.

“A civilization that did not leave its wounded behind.”

Silence.

Then Ortheva extended her hand.

Joel took it.

“High Marshal.”

“Veyr-Khal.”

She looked toward Gate Seventeen.

“When the road opens again, perhaps we should decide together who gets to walk it.”

Joel nodded.

“That sounds reasonable.”

Anton sighed.

Everyone looked toward him.

“What?”

He gestured toward the two commanders.

“You are both being far too sensible.”

Ortheva finally smiled.

“I was told you were the difficult brother.”

Daniel answered before anyone else.

“No.”

All three pointed at Joel.

For the first time, the Veyr-Khal laughed.

## 14. The Dead Gate

Gate Seventeen did not stabilize naturally.

The brothers helped make sure of that.

Daniel spent most of the next two days inside the Talyrn gate-control complex with engineers who had maintained systems older than their civilization’s written records.

Christer worked beside the navigation teams.

Joel and Anton mostly stayed out of the machinery.

Mostly.

The problem was simple.

Gate Seventeen could now open.

That was also the problem.

If the route became reliably usable, sooner or later somebody else would find it.

A trader.

A spy.

A Garooth scout.

A Seraph-controlled vessel.

Or one of the major powers of the Known Reach.

Joel stood above the gate-control pit while Daniel displayed the reconstructed authentication architecture.

“We cannot make the gate disappear,” Daniel said.

The Veyr-Khal nodded.

“But we can make it useless.”

That got her attention.

The solution came from combining two traditions.

The Talyrn knew Gate Seventeen.

They understood its ancient internal systems, maintenance logic, and the Odrava-side approach.

The Hjertstedts understood something different.

How to make a functioning Varanthi route look dead.

Vintergard had survived precisely because access depended on more than coordinates. A ship needed the correct approach, valid credentials, and the correct sequence of authentication events.

Christer proposed adapting the same principle.

Not copying the Winter Road.

Never that.

Building something new.

A Gate Seventeen Key Chain.

Daniel projected the sequence.

“First layer: passive recognition.”

A vessel approaching normally would receive nothing.

No beacon.

No guidance.

No handshake.

The gate would appear inert.

Anton leaned over the railing.

“Dead.”

“Exactly.”

Christer continued.

“Second layer requires an authorized approach vector.”

Not merely coordinates.

The ship had to enter along a narrow, changing geometry.

Wrong approach?

Nothing.

The gate remained black.

“Third layer,” Daniel said, “is the key.”

But not a fixed key.

That was important.

A stolen authorization code could not be allowed to open the road forever.

The Talyrn engineers proposed rotating cryptographic credentials.

House experience added vessel identity.

Crew authority.

Time window.

Mission authorization.

A key would therefore belong not simply to a ship—

but to a specific ship, under specific authority, during a specific period.

The Veyr-Khal studied the design.

“And if someone captures the vessel?”

Joel answered:

“The key expires.”

Then Anton contributed.

Everyone became nervous.

“What if the wrong people keep trying?”

The senior Talyrn Gatewright looked at him.

“The gate refuses them.”

Anton shook his head.

“That tells them there is something worth refusing.”

Joel slowly smiled.

Daniel noticed.

“Oh no.”

Anton continued.

“Make it boring.”

The room became quiet.

Christer understood first.

“False failure states.”

Daniel turned toward the gate architecture.

“Yes.”

A vessel without correct authorization would not receive a dramatic rejection.

No warning.

No security challenge.

No indication the gate was protected.

Instead the structure would behave exactly like a dead Varanthi gate.

Cold.

Unresponsive.

Ancient.

Useless.

Its visible power signature would remain at background-maintenance levels.

Any active diagnostics would return corrupted or incomplete responses consistent with age and system failure.

The wrong people would conclude what everyone had concluded for generations:

Dead gate. No route. Nothing here.

The Veyr-Khal looked toward Anton.

“That was unexpectedly subtle.”

Anton looked offended.

“I can be subtle.”

All three brothers stared at him.

Anton sighed.

“Occasionally.”

Joel added one final requirement.

“Authorized ships must also be able to verify the gate before commitment.”

Daniel nodded.

They built an anticipatory handshake.

A valid vessel would transmit a low-power encrypted challenge well before entering the throat.

The gate would answer only inside a narrow authenticated sensor exchange.

Nothing visible to ordinary traffic.

Nothing strong enough to advertise activation.

The ship would know:

Gate alive.

Credential accepted.

Transit available.

Only then would the approach sequence begin.

Christer called it Silent Recognition.

The Talyrn preferred Veiled Passage.

Anton suggested Dead Man’s Door.

Nobody chose Anton's version.

Testing began with an old Talyrn courier.

First pass:

No key.

The courier approached Gate Seventeen.

Nothing happened.

No light.

No response.

Its sensors showed only a dormant ancient structure.

The pilot transmitted a standard gate inquiry.

Silence.

A second inquiry.

Silence.

A diagnostic sweep.

Fragmentary static.

The ship withdrew.

Perfect.

Then the courier returned.

This time with authorization.

At long range, it transmitted the encrypted challenge.

For five seconds—

nothing.

Then one tiny symbol appeared on the pilot's navigation display.

RECOGNIZED

No visible gate activity.

The courier entered the authorized vector.

A second confirmation arrived.

ROUTE HELD

Only when the vessel reached final commitment did the gate wake.

Light spread around the ancient ring.

The transit plane formed.

The courier vanished.

Thirty seconds later, confirmation arrived from the far side.

Successful.

The Talyrn control room erupted.

Not cheering.

Talyrn officers seemed constitutionally incapable of proper cheering.

But several engineers hugged.

That was close enough.

Then Daniel implemented the dangerous part.

Revocation.

Every authorized vessel could be removed individually.

Every key could expire.

Every access class could be suspended.

If command integrity became uncertain, all non-essential access could be killed instantly.

A Seraph-compromised ship with yesterday’s credentials would arrive tomorrow—

and find a dead gate.

A captured House ship?

Dead gate.

A stolen Talyrn courier?

Dead gate.

A fleet trying to brute-force its way through?

Dead gate.

The road existed only for those both sides chose to recognize.

Joel looked through the observation window toward Gate Seventeen.

“That is better.”

The Veyr-Khal joined him.

“You have effectively helped us hide an operational gateway.”

Joel shook his head.

“No.”

She looked toward him.

“We helped you control who knows it is operational.”

That distinction mattered.

The first outbound authorization was issued to the Wayward Hound.

Daniel stared at the ship record.

Then at the battered transport itself.

“You are seriously making this the first recognized vessel?”

Joel smiled.

“She found the road.”

Anton nodded.

“She deserves it.”

Christer looked at the faded blue stripe.

“She deserves paint.”

“No,” Joel and Anton said together.

The Veyr-Khal almost smiled.

A sealed diplomatic archive was loaded aboard.

Limited Talyrn contact protocols.

Gate rules.

Emergency recognition procedures.

And one hard restriction:

No Known Reach vessel would receive Gate Seventeen access without joint authorization.

For now, the Talyrn and House von Hjertstedt would both have to agree.

The gate would not become a trade route.

Not yet.

It would become something far more valuable.

A controlled door.

And to everyone else—

a ruin.
