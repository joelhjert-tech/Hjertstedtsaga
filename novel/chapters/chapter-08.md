# Chapter 8: The Smaller Ships

## 1. The Work That Waited

Kestrel had changed while they were gone.

Not its name. Not its allegiance. Those arguments had already been settled.

The changes were smaller now, and harder to fit on a strategic display.

A civilian repair berth had opened where Sera had once stored emergency rations. A school had taken over two rooms beside the old responder offices. The corridor outside her command deck smelled of fresh paint instead of overheated wiring.

There were still people asking when their housing would be ready.

Mara's patrol schedule still contained more work than ships.

Joel found both women at a table covered in civilian complaints when he arrived.

Sera looked up.

“You survived sightseeing.”

“Apparently.”

She pushed a chair toward him.

“Then you can help with something difficult.”

The complaint concerned a fuel contractor who had priority access to a berth while a passenger ship waited outside with failing scrubbers.

Joel read it.

“Move the passenger ship in.”

“Already done,” Mara said.

“Then why am I reading it?”

Sera pointed to the second page.

“Because the contractor claims House affiliation makes his schedule untouchable.”

Joel read the attached agreement more carefully.

“It does not.”

“Good. Put that in writing.”

He did.

Weeks passed in work like that.

The House contingent beyond Gate Seventeen sent reports. Relief convoys carried spares and medical staff outward, wounded crews and damaged equipment back. Ortheva's messages described a front that was holding, not a war that had conveniently disappeared.

From Ardent came different lists.

Trials scheduled. Witnesses transferred. Restitution claims. New water systems connected to old habitation blocks.

Project Shepherd remained under investigation. Its archive was sealed. Sael-Vareth remained in guarded custody, its testimony checked against records rather than accepted because it had become useful. The surviving elders continued treatment. Recovering them had not undone what had been done to them.

Joel went to see Hakon during a medical transfer.

They did not discuss the campaign first.

Hakon asked about a house that had stood near a winter road on Vinterholm before he disappeared. Joel did not know whether it was still there.

He promised to find out.

When Annette joined him later, she brought a photograph from the local records office. The house survived. Someone had replaced the roof.

Hakon studied the image longer than he had studied the victory reports.

Afterward, Joel stood with Annette outside the medical rooms.

“There should be time for that,” he said.

“There is time. You keep allocating it to other things.”

He took her hand.

She let him remain silent.

At Kestrel Shield, Bjorn's Warden occupied a repair bay large enough for a small craft. The mechanists had removed its outer plates and arranged the damaged assemblies on numbered stands. Nothing about the machine looked ready for another war.

Bjorn visited under medical escort and argued over every discarded part.

Daniel argued back.

Neither of them called it operational.

Bjorn held up the slate showing the replacement shoulder assembly.

“Lighter than the old one. I might beat you to the next door.”

“You could beat Anton,” Daniel said. “He would stop to explain that he was ahead.”

The elder laughed, then had to pause for breath. Daniel lowered the slate until the medic was satisfied. The argument resumed at a volume she could tolerate.

The training halls nearby were crowded with younger officers.

Sera brought Joel there one afternoon. Through the observation window they watched a simulated boarding party discover that its extraction route had failed.

The cadets tried to continue toward command.

Their instructor stopped the exercise.

“Who is carrying your wounded?”

No one answered quickly enough.

Sera folded her arms.

“They have watched the Ghar-Kul recordings.”

“Which ones?” Joel asked.

“The exciting ones.”

Inside, the instructor reset the exercise with the same wounded markers and fewer working doors.

Joel watched a young officer surrender the lead position to take hold of a casualty sling.

“Better.”

Sera handed him another proposal.

Kestrel's proving-ground postings already existed, but they had grown through individual contracts and family recommendations. The new plan gave them a common standard: experienced captains, clear contract limits, medical reserves, independent records and a way to ask for help before everything failed.

“I can train crews,” Sera said. “I cannot put you on every ship.”

“You should not need to.”

“Then help me make that true.”

Joel looked back through the glass.

This time the cadets brought everyone to the extraction point before reporting their objective complete.

He opened the proposal.

## 2. The Smaller Ships

Captain Elara Vos arrived for the review with an engineering report instead of a speech.

“My second missile cell passes certification,” she said. “The boarding cutter has new seals. I need another coolant reserve and a replacement for the port sensor controller.”

Mara took the slate.

“How many trainees?”

“Twelve.”

Joel looked toward the dock beyond the office window.

ISS Wayfarer's Debt rested between two larger freighters. Its civilian markings covered old military plating. Repair crews were working beneath the spine, checking the cutter's mountings.

“Comfortable?” he asked.

“For twelve?”

“For you.”

Elara's scar moved when she smiled.

“Ask me after the first contract.”

The assignment was modest by the standards of the room. Escort three medical freighters. Investigate reports of missing miners along the return route. Come home with the ships, the records and the crew.

Elara read the extension conditions twice.

“The next settlement always has a reason you should stay,” she said.

Sera nodded.

“That is why you retain command. A family name does not allow a trainee to overrule you.”

“Even when the family answers their messages?”

Joel signed the command provision himself.

“Especially then.”

They inspected the ship together.

A Falken trainee was testing the passive sensor picture against a second receiver. An Eikholm engineer had half a maintenance panel open. Two Skar cadets were arguing over a weapons drill until Elara asked which of them had checked the ammunition inventory.

Both left to do it.

At an auxiliary station, a Winge cadet reviewed the contract.

He looked up when Joel stopped beside him.

“There is no fixed payment for passengers recovered during an emergency.”

“Would that stop you recovering them?”

“No, High Marshal.”

“Good.”

The cadet glanced at the slate.

“It could stop someone paying us afterward.”

Elara made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

Joel leaned against the empty chair.

“Keep both records. What you were hired to do, and what you had to do when the situation changed. You will need them for different reasons.”

The cadet began making a note.

“And if someone claims a rescued passenger is a prisoner?”

“Ask to see the authority.”

“If they refuse?”

“Call someone who can help you hold the question open.”

At the communications station, Mara checked the routing package. It did not depend on one direct link to Kestrel. An authenticated distress packet could move through available relays to an eligible House command.

It could also fail.

She made the Falken trainee demonstrate the fallback without the primary transmitter. Then without the nearest relay. Then with a delay long enough that the acknowledgment would not arrive during the exercise.

“You still have to command the ship while you wait,” she told him.

Across the wider frontier, House Banners continued their existing patrols and contracts. Kestrel's new traffic was only one part of that network. Mara added the expedition's credentials to its emergency routing, then closed the display before the cadets could mistake a list of possible responders for a fleet assigned to follow them.

The Wayfarer's Debt would leave under its own captain.

It would have help to call.

Those were separate facts.

On departure morning, Sera watched the cadets board with equipment cases that looked too large for them.

One returned for a forgotten bag.

Another was already arguing with a loader about where medical supplies should go.

Elara counted them through the collar. She counted again before allowing it to close.

Joel stood beside Sera at the command window.

“You could send a cruiser,” she said.

“And the three medical ships would still need escorts next month.”

Below them, the docking clamps released.

The Wayfarer's Debt moved away from Kestrel Shield with its freighters forming behind it.

Mara checked their transponders.

“All four ships clear.”

Sera watched until they passed beyond the outer platforms.

Then another request arrived, from a different captain with a different crew.

She opened it.

## 3. Dock Twelve

The medical freighters reached their destination.

The missing miners were found on a damaged service platform with failing power. The Wayfarer's Debt brought the survivors out and returned their identification records to the families waiting for them.

Elara filed the report from a repair berth.

Then she prepared to turn home.

The next request came from a convoy whose escort had withdrawn. It needed protection through one more lane. Elara checked fuel, ammunition and the age of the navigation data before accepting.

At the far end, they found evidence of a slave convoy.

After that came the abandoned colony.

None of those places knew the names of the people whose approval Elara had obtained at Kestrel. They knew the ship that had answered them.

The contracts accumulated. So did wear on the ship.

Elara refused extensions twice when the machinery needed work. She put the cadets through drills between assignments and made them take watches even when they thought the previous rescue should have earned a rest from instruction.

She also tried to point the bow toward home twice.

Neither attempt lasted.

By the sixth completed contract, the return voyage had become a serious piece of planning. Kestrel lay thousands of light-years away along a sequence of connected routes. Reports moved back through relays, arriving out of order and well behind the people who had sent them.

Mara maintained the expedition's overdue-report clock beside the other frontier crews.

She did not call an old acknowledgment a current position.

Joel saw the growing route during a visit to Kestrel Command before leaving on a wider circuit of House postings.

“Vos?” he asked.

“Still reporting. Still collecting people.”

“Is the ship sound?”

“At the date of the report.”

Joel nodded at the qualification.

“Keep the response credentials current.”

Mara already had.

Out on the frontier, Elara brought the Wayfarer's Debt to Calder Reach for coolant, provisions and work on a persistent sensor fault.

The station assigned them Dock Twelve.

Its approach beacon carried several generations of repair patches. The docking instructions included a warning that one set of guide lights could not be trusted. A civilian tug met them to finish the approach.

Elara took manual control for the last few hundred meters.

“Everyone stays aboard until we have atmosphere confirmation.”

The collar joined with a heavy knock.

Dock Twelve was old, but the seals held.

Beyond the station's outer structures, local defenders were dragging a crippled patrol vessel toward cover. Scorch marks crossed the nearest battery housing. Someone had painted civilian evacuation arrows over faded Directorate signs.

The Falken cadet enlarged the view.

“Recent fighting.”

Elara studied the damaged vessel.

“Keep a sensor watch.”

On Calder Reach, a man calling himself Varek Hale worked through the latest defensive inventory.

Four ships. An inadequate missile reserve. Batteries that had to be brought online separately because their controllers no longer agreed.

He marked the ones that would actually fire.

A station officer brought him the new arrivals list. He noticed the courier at Dock Twelve and the medical supplies declared among its cargo. One more crew waiting for a safe departure window.

He added it to the evacuation plot with the others.

“You can go off watch,” the officer told him.

Varek looked at the passive contacts beyond the mining lanes.

“In a moment.”

Far away, Kestrel's repaired docks were sending another small ship out beneath working lights.

At Calder Reach, the outer sensor station reported movement.

Varek put down the arrivals list and called his defenders.

## 4. The Man They Erased

The frontier station Calder Reach was under attack again.

Pirate missiles crossed the dark in crooked waves, cheap weapons launched from converted freighters and armed mining hulls. Most were bad shots.

Enough were not.

“Contact bloom, bearing zero-eight-seven!”

Commander Varek Solan stood behind the tactical pit as Calder Reach shook beneath another impact.

The station was old.

A Directorate survey-and-supply hub built decades earlier at the edge of mapped space, then quietly abandoned when richer routes were found elsewhere. Now twenty thousand civilians, miners, traders, refugees, and independent crews called it home.

And the pirates knew it was weak.

“Outer Battery Three is gone!”

“Dock Six reports pressure loss!”

“Pirate cutters moving behind the ore refinery!”

Varek looked across the tactical display.

Sixteen hostile hulls.

Maybe more hiding cold behind the asteroid belt.

Calder Reach had four defensive ships.

One could barely accelerate.

Another had no missiles.

The station’s point-defense grid had been rebuilt so many times that half the guns had civilian serial numbers.

Varek gave the order anyway.

“Responder One and Two, fall behind the refinery spine.”

His tactical officer stared.

“That gives them the approach.”

“It gives them what looks like the approach.”

Varek marked three industrial tugs.

“Cut the ore containers loose.”

The officer understood.

A smile appeared.

“Decoys.”

“Mass.”

The tugs released hundreds of tonnes of processed metal into the pirate approach.

Responder ships fired their engines and pulled away.

The pirates accelerated into what looked like a broken defense.

Then Calder Reach fired every working cargo-control thruster at once.

Containers moved.

Not fast.

Enough.

The pirate formation suddenly had thousands of tonnes of industrial debris crossing its path.

One raider turned.

Another tried.

A third hit a container at combat speed.

Its forward section vanished.

“Now,” Varek said.

Calder Reach’s surviving batteries opened fire.

The first pirate frigate lost its drive.

The second took a hit across the weapon mounts.

The rest scattered.

Not victory.

Breathing room.

Varek looked toward the civilian traffic trying to reach the inner docks.

“Evacuation convoy?”

“Seven minutes.”

“Then we need eight.”

That was how frontier wars worked.

Not with perfect fleets.

With whatever minutes you could steal.

Varek had once commanded much more than this.

Directorate destroyers.

Compliance cruisers.

Hunter groups.

Ships built for precision ambush, surveillance, and controlled kill-boxes—the kind of doctrine the Directorate of Ash preferred. 

Fleet bible adaptation guide

His last command had been the Ninth Compliance Group.

Twenty-seven ships.

Seven thousand personnel.

Enough surveillance equipment to map a system’s communications before its government realized it had been observed.

Now he commanded nothing.

Officially.

Three months earlier, Varek had been summoned to Directorate Strategic Review.

No charge was read.

No court was convened.

He was simply informed:

COMMAND AUTHORITY REVOKED.

ACCESS SUSPENDED.

OFFICER STATUS TERMINATED.

Then they tried to arrest him.

Varek escaped because the Directorate trained its senior commanders to think like hunters.

The mistake had been assuming he would forget the training when they started hunting him.

He had crossed five lanes under false transponders.

Changed ships three times.

Spent two weeks inside a cargo compartment carrying reactor coolant.

Then Calder Reach had found him.

Or perhaps he had found Calder Reach.

A station under pirate attack did not ask many questions when an experienced commander offered to help.

He gave them another name.

Varek Hale.

Nobody believed it.

Nobody cared.

“Commander!”

The sensor officer’s voice snapped him back.

A new contact had appeared beyond the pirate line.

One ship.

Cold-running.

Precise.

No pirate transponder.

Varek stared at the sensor behavior.

Then his stomach tightened.

He knew that profile.

“Kill active scans.”

The officer looked confused.

“What?”

“Now.”

The station sensors dropped to passive.

Too late.

The unknown ship transmitted once.

A narrow encrypted pulse.

Directorate format.

Varek felt every muscle in his back tighten.

The Directorate had found him.

The transmission repeated.

Not addressed to Calder Reach.

Addressed directly to him.

COMMANDER SOLAN.

The tactical pit went quiet.

His false name was gone.

The next line appeared.

YOU ARE ORDERED TO SURRENDER FOR CORRECTIVE CUSTODY.

One of the station officers slowly looked toward Varek.

“You said your name was Hale.”

Varek stared at the message.

“I lied.”

“That ship military?”

“Yes.”

“Yours?”

“No.”

A pause.

“Used to be.”

That was worse.

Outside, the pirate fleet began reforming.

They had noticed the newcomer too.

Varek watched their ships change course.

Not fleeing.

Moving aside.

The Directorate hunter vessel passed straight through the pirate screen.

No shots exchanged.

No challenge.

Nothing.

Varek’s expression hardened.

The station captain beside him understood.

“They know each other.”

“Maybe.”

“You think the Directorate hired the pirates?”

Varek looked at the civilian evacuation markers.

Thousands of people still exposed.

“I think the Directorate does not care who dies while they collect me.”

That fit the state he had served too well.

Cold authoritarian control.

Hunter fleets.

Surveillance.

Erasure when control failed. 

External factions overview

The hunter ship opened another transmission.

SURRENDER WITHIN TEN MINUTES.

Then:

CALDER REACH WILL NOT BE HELD RESPONSIBLE FOR HARBORING YOU IF IT COOPERATES.

The station captain read it.

“That is a threat.”

“Yes.”

“Can we fight them?”

Varek looked at the pirate contacts.

Then the Directorate hunter ship.

Then Calder Reach’s battered defenses.

“No.”

The captain swallowed.

“What do we do?”

Varek looked toward the evacuation convoy.

Five minutes from safety.

The station needed time.

Again.

He reached for his old Directorate sidearm.

The only piece of his former life he had kept.

“We make them think I’m running.”

The captain frowned.

“Are you?”

Varek looked toward the docks.

A small mercenary courier waited there.

Fast.

Badly armed.

Enough.

“Yes.”

He started walking.

The captain grabbed his arm.

“You leave, the pirates hit us again.”

Varek stopped.

That was true.

He looked through the viewport at Calder Reach.

A station that owed him nothing.

People who had given him shelter without asking why he was hunted.

The Directorate had trained him to calculate acceptable losses.

Calder Reach had reminded him that losses had names.

Varek slowly pulled his arm free.

“I’ll draw the hunter ship.”

“And the pirates?”

He looked back toward the tactical display.

“Those I haven’t solved yet.”

Then the first new pirate missile wave launched.

And the Directorate hunter ship accelerated toward the station.

## 5. The Cadet Ship

Varek reached Dock Twelve with ninety seconds to spare.

The mercenary courier waiting there was not what he expected.

It was too clean.

Not new.

Not elegant.

But maintained with the obsessive care of people who expected every component to work when someone started shooting.

The hull carried civilian mercenary markings over older military plating.

ISS Wayfarer’s Debt.

A reinforced courier-frigate built for independent contracts.

Fast engines.

Heavy point defense for its size.

Two missile cells.

One boarding cutter attached beneath the spine.

Enough weapons to survive trouble.

Not enough to win a real fleet action.

Exactly what Varek needed.

He crossed the docking tube as another pirate missile struck Calder Reach.

The station shook behind him.

“Close the collar!”

The inner hatch sealed.

Varek turned—

—and stopped.

There were twelve armed people waiting for him.

Too young.

Far too young.

Most looked somewhere between late teens and early twenties.

Combat jackets.

Partial armor.

Different House-family insignia.

Falken.

Skar.

Eikholm.

One Winge rose-and-wing badge.

And on the bulkhead behind them—

a small black-and-crimson crest.

House von Hjertstedt.

Varek stared.

“You are House.”

A young woman sitting on an ammunition crate looked up.

“Technically.”

“That word worries me.”

“It worries our instructors too.”

The captain pushed through them.

She was older.

Maybe forty.

Scar across her jaw.

Grey mercenary coat.

“Captain Elara Vos.”

“Varek Solan.”

“I know.”

Varek’s hand moved slightly toward his pistol.

Elara noticed.

“So does half the station after that Directorate broadcast.”

Fair.

She pointed toward the young personnel.

“My crew.”

Varek looked over them again.

“Those are cadets.”

A young Skar man objected immediately.

“Probationary field officers.”

Elara looked at him.

“Cadets.”

He sighed.

“Cadets.”

Varek stared at her.

“You brought House cadets to the far side of the galaxy?”

“They brought themselves.”

That was somehow worse.

The Wayfarer’s Debt was part of the new Kestrel proving-ground system.

Young House personnel were being sent into frontier regions where the major powers had weak control, taking legitimate mercenary contracts under experienced commanders before they were trusted with larger formations.

Normally they operated closer to Kestrel.

This crew had gone farther.

Much farther.

Their original assignment had been simple.

Escort three medical freighters.

Investigate missing miners.

Return.

Then contracts followed contracts.

A convoy here.

A rescue there.

A pirate pursuit through two unstable sectors.

Now they were thousands of light-years from Kestrel with six completed contracts and one captain rapidly acquiring grey hair.

Varek looked toward Elara.

“You let them come this far?”

She folded her arms.

“I tried turning around twice.”

One Falken cadet spoke from the sensor station.

“Then we found the slave convoy.”

Another added:

“And then the abandoned colony.”

Elara pointed toward them.

“This is the problem.”

Varek almost smiled.

He understood exactly what she meant.

A warning alarm interrupted them.

“Directorate vessel accelerating!”

The cadets moved instantly.

That caught Varek’s attention.

No panic.

No confusion.

Seats occupied.

Armor sealed.

Systems came online.

Young, yes.

Untrained, no.

The Falken cadet took sensors.

An Eikholm engineering trainee headed aft.

Two Skar cadets moved toward weapons control.

The Winge cadet strapped into an auxiliary command station and began recording the engagement.

Varek stared at him.

“You are logging this?”

“Everything.”

“We are being hunted.”

“That usually increases the importance of documentation.”

House.

Definitely House.

Captain Elara dropped into her chair.

“Cast off.”

Dock Twelve released them.

The Wayfarer’s Debt kicked away from Calder Reach just as another pirate salvo crossed the station’s defensive envelope.

Point-defense guns hammered around them.

Tracer fire filled the dark.

Varek grabbed a support rail.

“Turn directly toward the Directorate ship.”

Elara looked at him.

“That was not my plan.”

“It will be theirs.”

The young tactical officer turned.

“Explain.”

Varek looked across the main display.

The Directorate hunter was approaching with perfect discipline.

Pirates were spreading away from it.

“They expect me to run for the outer lane.”

“So?”

“So we don't.”

Varek marked the hunter vessel.

“We burn toward them.”

Silence.

Then the Skar cadet smiled.

“I like him.”

Elara sighed.

“Of course you do.”

The engines fired.

The Wayfarer’s Debt accelerated straight toward the ship hunting Varek.

The Directorate vessel reacted immediately.

Its weapons rotated.

Its approach tightened.

Varek watched the response.

Predictable.

Directorate doctrine.

Control the target.

Push it into a corridor.

Strip away options.

Then capture.

He had taught officers to do exactly this.

“Wait.”

The distance fell.

Hammer Range approached.

A cadet called:

“Enemy targeting sensors active.”

Another:

“Missile doors opening.”

Elara looked toward Varek.

“Your idea is becoming significantly less attractive.”

“Wait.”

The hunter transmitted.

COMMANDER SOLAN. POWER DOWN.

Varek ignored it.

Closer.

Closer.

Then he pointed toward a cluster of pirate ships behind them.

“Cut thrust.”

Elara understood.

“Oh.”

The engines died.

The Wayfarer’s Debt suddenly stopped accelerating.

The Directorate hunter continued forward.

Its predicted firing solution changed.

Its own momentum carried it deeper toward Calder Reach.

And directly across the pirates' approach.

The pirate formation reacted badly.

Several ships turned weapons toward the Directorate vessel.

Varek smiled.

“They were willing to cooperate while the Directorate was hunting me.”

The Falken cadet understood.

“But now the Directorate is entering their attack corridor.”

“Yes.”

Pirates did not like authority.

Especially authority that suddenly looked like competition.

“Pirate targeting blooms!”

Then:

“Missile launches!”

Not at Calder Reach.

At the Directorate ship.

Elara looked at Varek.

“You weaponized their distrust.”

“I used to work for the Directorate.”

“That did not answer me.”

“Yes.”

The Directorate hunter's point defense ignited.

Pirate missiles died by the dozen.

But the hunter had to turn.

Its weapons moved away from the Wayfarer’s Debt.

Varek pointed toward the outer lane.

“Now run.”

Elara did not need convincing.

Engines slammed to maximum.

The mercenary frigate accelerated away.

Calder Reach's defenders opened fire behind them.

Pirates engaged the Directorate ship.

For several precious minutes, the entire system became confused.

Exactly what Varek wanted.

Then the Winge cadet spoke.

“Captain.”

Elara looked over.

“We have a legal problem.”

Varek almost laughed.

“We are escaping execution.”

“Yes.”

The cadet remained completely serious.

“But Commander Solan has not formally contracted us.”

Everyone looked toward Varek.

Even while missiles crossed the tactical display.

Elara rubbed her forehead.

“Winge.”

The cadet nodded proudly.

Varek stared.

“You want a contract now?”

“Yes.”

“We're being fired upon.”

“Which means terms should be established before further combat.”

Varek looked toward Elara.

“Is he always like this?”

“Yes.”

The cadet corrected:

“Usually worse.”

Elara opened a contract screen.

“Emergency passenger extraction.”

Varek shook his head.

“I need more than extraction.”

“What do you need?”

He looked back at Calder Reach.

Pirates were reorganizing.

The Directorate hunter was still alive.

And eventually both would come after him.

“I need a ship.”

The Skar cadet smiled.

Elara did not.

“You cannot afford this ship.”

“I don't want to buy it.”

“That is encouraging.”

“I need to hire it.”

“For what?”

Varek looked toward the House cadets.

Young people thousands of light-years from home, deliberately learning war where the great powers had failed to impose order.

Then he looked at the Directorate symbol closing behind them.

“To find out why my own government wants me dead.”

The bridge went quiet.

The Winge cadet leaned forward.

“That sounds like an investigation.”

Varek looked at him.

“Yes.”

The cadet smiled.

“Now I am interested.”

Captain Elara stared at the ceiling for one long second.

“I hate proving-ground assignments.”

Then the Directorate hunter transmitted again.

This time the message was different.

WAYFARER’S DEBT.

SURRENDER SOLAN.

HOUSE PERSONNEL WILL BE RETURNED UNHARMED.

Every cadet on the bridge went still.

The Skar cadet slowly looked toward Elara.

“They know who we are.”

Varek's expression hardened.

More importantly—

the Directorate knew they were House.

And still expected them to surrender a man without warrant, hearing, or contract.

The Winge cadet began recording.

“Captain?”

Elara looked toward Varek.

Then toward the pursuing Directorate vessel.

“Commander Solan.”

“Yes?”

“Welcome aboard.”

The Wayfarer’s Debt accelerated for the outer lane.

And behind them, the hunt truly began.

## 6. Reinforcement Request

The Directorate hunter fired first.

Not a warning shot.

A real one.

The beam crossed the Wayfarer’s Debt bow and tore armor from the forward plating.

The bridge shook hard.

“Direct hit!”

“Forward armor down twelve percent!”

“Point defense online!”

Captain Elara Vos gripped her chair.

“That settles the legal question.”

The Winge cadet was already working.

“Confirmed hostile act against contracted House personnel.”

Varek looked toward him.

“You sound pleased.”

“I am not pleased.”

The cadet opened another record.

“I am legally satisfied.”

Elara muttered:

“Winge.”

The Directorate hunter fired again.

This time the Wayfarer’s Debt turned sharply.

The shot passed close enough to light the bridge windows white.

A Skar cadet shouted from tactical:

“They are walking fire onto us!”

Varek recognized the firing pattern from Directorate capture operations.

No reactor shots.

No catastrophic kill.

Weapons.

Engines.

Communications.

They wanted the ship crippled.

Then boarded.

That was Directorate doctrine at its cleanest.

“They still want me alive,” Varek said.

Elara glanced toward him.

“And us?”

Varek watched another targeting solution appear.

“Preferably.”

The Skar cadet smiled without humor.

“Comforting.”

Then the Falken sensor cadet spoke.

“Captain, we still have narrow-beam access to the outer relay.”

Elara turned.

“How stable?”

“Bad.”

“Can you reach Kestrel?”

“Not directly.”

The cadet expanded the relay network.

The distance was enormous.

They were on the far side of the galaxy from the new proving grounds.

But House communications did not depend on one simple call.

Relay to relay.

Authenticated packet.

Priority routing.

Eventually the message would reach a House command node.

Maybe Kestrel.

Maybe a Falken patrol command.

Maybe something closer.

Elara looked toward the House cadets.

“Send it.”

The Winge cadet turned toward communications.

“I’ll authenticate.”

The first transmission failed.

Too much interference.

The Directorate vessel was jamming them.

The second packet broke apart before reaching the relay.

The Falken cadet looked frustrated.

“They know what we are doing.”

Varek nodded.

“Of course they do.”

He had once commanded the people trying to stop them.

“Can you hide the burst inside navigation telemetry?”

The cadet looked at him.

“That would violate relay formatting.”

The Winge cadet answered before Varek could.

“Emergency hostile-action exemption.”

The Falken cadet smiled.

“I like you today.”

“Record that.”

The Wayfarer’s Debt rolled beneath another Directorate attack.

Weapons fire tore through its wake.

Pirate ships remained behind them, but two had begun accelerating in pursuit.

Everyone wanted a piece of the same target now.

Elara looked toward Varek.

“Buy them ten seconds.”

“With what?”

“My ship.”

Varek understood.

He moved toward tactical.

“Turn us toward the hunter.”

The Skar cadet laughed once.

“You really do like doing that.”

“Weapons?”

“Two missile cells. Forward guns. Boarding cutter.”

“Load everything.”

Elara stared.

“You are not firing everything.”

“No.”

Varek marked the Directorate hunter’s sensor spine.

“We are making them think we will.”

The Wayfarer’s Debt turned.

The Directorate ship reacted immediately.

Point defense shifted.

Electronic warfare focused forward.

Its jamming field tightened around the mercenary ship.

Exactly what Varek wanted.

“Now.”

The Falken cadet transmitted.

Not a normal distress call.

A compressed authenticated burst hidden inside what looked like corrupted lane-navigation telemetry.

The relay caught it.

For half a second, nothing happened.

Then the display returned:

PACKET RECEIVED.

The bridge erupted.

The Winge cadet immediately opened the authentication chain.

“House credential verified.”

Elara looked toward him.

“What did you send?”

“House cadet expedition vessel Wayfarer’s Debt.”

“Under active attack by Directorate military asset.”

“Confirmed live weapons fire.”

“Civilian frontier station Calder Reach also under heavy pirate assault.”

“Request immediate reinforcement, medical support, and lawful intervention authority.”

He paused.

“I included Commander Solan.”

Varek looked toward him.

“As what?”

“Person under disputed Directorate custody claim.”

Varek almost smiled.

“Disputed?”

“There is no warrant aboard this ship.”

That was the most Winge answer possible.

The packet moved through successive relays. It had escaped the jamming; now someone had to read it.

The Directorate hunter fired again.

This shot struck the Wayfarer’s Debt aft quarter.

The ship lurched violently.

Alarms screamed.

“Engine Two thermal spike!”

“Coolant loss!”

“Rear point-defense cluster offline!”

Elara slammed one hand against the console.

“Damage control!”

The Eikholm cadet was already running aft.

Two others followed.

No hesitation.

No panic.

Varek watched them leave.

They were cadets.

But they were House cadets.

That distinction was becoming important.

Then the communications board flashed again.

The Falken cadet stared.

“Captain.”

Elara looked over.

“What?”

“We received a response.”

Too fast.

Varek knew that immediately.

Kestrel could not possibly have answered yet.

The cadet opened it.

HOUSE AUTHENTICATION ACCEPTED.

Then another line.

DISTRESS ROUTED TO NEAREST ELIGIBLE COMMAND.

Elara leaned forward.

“Nearest?”

The final line appeared.

HOLD POSITION IF POSSIBLE.

Then:

REINFORCEMENT AUTHORITY ACKNOWLEDGED.

No ship name.

No commander.

No arrival time.

Just confirmation.

Somewhere in this part of the galaxy—

House von Hjertstedt already had forces.

Varek looked at the screen.

“How close?”

The Falken cadet shook his head.

“Unknown.”

The Winge cadet added:

“Command deliberately did not transmit force disposition.”

“Good security.”

Varek approved despite himself.

Then the Directorate ship launched missiles.

Six.

Fast.

Disciplined.

Too many.

Elara looked toward tactical.

“Can we stop them?”

The Skar cadet answered:

“Four.”

“And the others?”

Silence.

Varek looked toward the approaching contacts.

Then toward Calder Reach behind them.

House reinforcements were coming.

But not yet.

They had to survive long enough to matter.

“Bring us toward the pirates,” Varek said.

Elara stared at him.

“Again?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He looked at the missile tracks.

“Because the Directorate understands us.”

Then he highlighted the chaotic pirate formation.

“But pirates understand nobody.”

The Wayfarer’s Debt turned hard.

Behind them, six Directorate missiles followed.

Ahead—

the pirates opened fire.

## 7. Into the Graveyard

The hunter changed its attack pattern. Varek suspected the distress packet had been intercepted: the Directorate was no longer probing for surrender. It was closing to disable them.

“New transmission,” the Falken cadet called.

The Directorate message appeared across the bridge.

PRIORITY OVERRIDE.

SOLAN IS TO BE RECOVERED BEFORE EXTERNAL INTERVENTION.

Then came the part not intended for the Wayfarer’s Debt.

A fragment caught by the ship’s electronic warfare suite:

COLLATERAL RESTRICTIONS REMOVED.

Captain Elara Vos stared at it.

“They are no longer treating our people as a limit on their fire.”

The Winge cadet’s face went cold.

“I have the transmission recorded.”

Varek looked toward the Directorate hunter.

“That will matter if we live.”

Behind them, Calder Reach was failing.

Pirates had stopped chasing the Wayfarer’s Debt.

Most were turning back toward the station.

They had realized the Directorate wanted Varek more than it wanted them.

That left Calder Reach exposed.

Three pirate frigates pushed directly toward the habitation ring.

Boarding cutters followed.

Marauder transports were moving toward Dock Four.

“Station defenses are collapsing,” tactical reported.

Elara swore.

Varek stared at the system map.

They had succeeded in drawing away the Directorate hunter.

But not the pirates.

Not enough.

If they continued running, Calder Reach would be boarded before House reinforcements arrived.

The station captain appeared over emergency comms.

His face was illuminated by red warning lights.

“Wayfarer, we've lost the outer batteries.”

Another impact shook the transmission.

“Pirates are preparing boarding operations.”

Varek looked at him.

“How long?”

“Maybe twenty minutes.”

Not enough.

Then the Falken cadet expanded the local navigation chart.

“There might be another option.”

A region beyond the system’s outer mining lanes flashed onto the display.

No normal navigation routes passed through it.

Varek recognized the hazard warning.

THE KEROS GRAVE.

A battlefield from a war more than a century old.

Thousands of pieces of wreckage.

Asteroids.

Destroyed stations.

Abandoned warships.

Some still carrying unexploded magazines.

Nobody sane crossed it at speed.

Elara stared.

“No.”

The cadet continued.

“There is an old emergency jump coordinate inside.”

“That coordinate is eighty-seven years old.”

“Yes.”

“So whatever was at that coordinate could now be somewhere else.”

“Yes.”

Elara looked at him.

“You understand why I said no?”

“Yes.”

Varek leaned closer.

“How dense?”

“Extremely.”

He smiled slightly.

Elara saw it.

“No.”

Varek highlighted the Directorate hunter and three pirate ships still pursuing them.

“We make an emergency jump.”

Elara shook her head.

“We could emerge inside a cruiser.”

“We could.”

“Or an asteroid.”

“Yes.”

“Or a live minefield.”

“Also possible.”

Someone at their academy had encouraged this, Varek thought.

Elara stared at him.

“And this helps Calder Reach how?”

Varek marked their current vector.

Then the Grave.

“Broadcast the jump preparation openly.”

The Winge cadet understood first.

“They follow.”

“The Directorate has to.”

Varek pointed toward himself.

“They need me before House reinforcements arrive.”

Then he marked the pirate ships.

“And pirates will assume we're running somewhere valuable.”

The Skar cadet grinned.

“So we pull every ship we can away from Calder.”

Elara looked at the station.

Then at the debris field.

Finally:

“Prepare emergency jump.”

The Wayfarer’s Debt transmitted its drive buildup deliberately.

No attempt to hide it.

The Directorate reacted immediately.

Its engines flared.

The three pursuing pirate ships accelerated too.

Then two more pirate contacts broke away from Calder Reach.

Five.

Good.

The station captain realized what Varek was doing.

“Solan, don't.”

Varek answered quietly.

“You need time.”

“You'll die in there.”

“Then make the time useful.”

The transmission went silent for several seconds.

Finally:

“Calder Reach copies.”

The jump drive screamed.

This was not a stable gate transit.

It was a short emergency cut through local space—a brutal tactical hop normally reserved for collision escape or desperation.

The Eikholm cadet shouted from engineering.

“Drive coils above tolerance!”

Elara answered:

“Just get us there!”

“That's not how machinery works!”

“Today it is!”

The Directorate fired.

A shot crossed behind them.

Another struck the outer hull.

Then space folded.

For less than a second, everything became pressure and light.

Then—

darkness.

The Wayfarer’s Debt emerged inside hell.

“DEBRIS!”

The bridge exploded into warnings.

A dead destroyer filled the forward display.

“Hard port!”

Elara threw the ship sideways.

They passed beneath the broken warship with barely hundreds of meters to spare.

A kilometer-wide asteroid rotated directly beyond it.

“Down!”

Thrusters fired.

The frigate dropped between two enormous stones.

Metal fragments hammered against the armor.

Point defense opened automatically.

Not against missiles.

Against wreckage.

The Keros Grave surrounded them.

Old cruisers drifted without power.

Frigates broken in half.

Ancient troop ships covered in impact scars.

Asteroids moved between them like slow mountains.

Sections of shattered stations spun through the dark.

Some warships still carried faded insignia from states that no longer existed.

The Falken cadet stared at the sensor board.

“I can't see anything.”

Varek looked around.

“Good.”

The cadet corrected himself.

“No. I mean anything.”

Every scan returned ghosts.

Metal.

Stone.

Old reactors.

Residual radiation.

Dead engines.

Ancient electronic noise.

Thousands upon thousands of contacts.

Then another jump bloom appeared.

The Directorate hunter emerged behind them.

Immediately alarms lit its hull as it nearly struck a drifting cruiser.

Then another bloom.

A pirate frigate.

Then another.

The pirate vessel emerged too close to an asteroid and disappeared in a flash of armor and atmosphere.

The Skar cadet stared.

“One pirate gone.”

Elara did not celebrate.

“We could be next.”

The Wayfarer’s Debt shut down everything it could.

Main engines.

Active sensors.

External lights.

Even unnecessary internal systems.

They drifted.

Cold.

Quiet.

Hidden among the dead.

Varek watched the Directorate vessel attempt to scan.

Its sensors were powerful.

Here, power meant little.

Every direction contained warships.

Every shadow could hide another hull.

Every engine burn announced position.

The Grave did not favor the strongest ship.

It favored the most patient.

The Falken cadet whispered:

“They can't find us.”

Varek shook his head.

“Not easily.”

A pirate ship passed less than forty kilometers away without seeing them.

Beyond it drifted an enormous battleship, split through the middle, its gun decks open to space.

The Wayfarer’s Debt slipped behind the corpse.

For the first time since leaving Calder Reach, Varek allowed himself to breathe.

Then a weak signal appeared.

Not Directorate.

Not pirate.

Old.

Very old.

Coming from inside the wrecked battleship beside them.

The Falken cadet turned pale.

“Captain…”

Elara looked over.

“What?”

The signal repeated.

Three words.

LIFE SUPPORT ACTIVE.

Nobody spoke.

The battleship had supposedly been dead for more than a century.

## 8. The Dead Battleship

The signal came again.

LIFE SUPPORT ACTIVE.

Captain Elara Vos stared at the dead battleship drifting beside them.

Its hull was enormous.

Old armor.

Broken gun decks.

Half its superstructure torn open.

The ship should have been a tomb.

Instead, something inside was still breathing.

“Do not answer,” Varek said.

The Falken cadet nodded.

“No transmission.”

The Wayfarer’s Debt continued drifting behind the battleship’s broken spine.

Then the first impact hit.

Not weapons fire.

Debris.

A chunk of armor plate the size of a shuttle came spinning out from behind an asteroid and struck the frigate’s lower hull.

The entire ship lurched.

“Impact!”

“Deck Three pressure loss!”

“Forward maneuvering cluster offline!”

Then a second hit.

Smaller.

Worse.

A jagged section of ancient structural frame punched across the starboard engine housing.

The bridge lights flickered.

The Eikholm cadet shouted over comms.

“ENGINE TWO IS DONE!”

Elara grabbed the command rail.

“Done as in damaged?”

“Done as in it is now several separate engineering problems!”

Another alarm.

Coolant pressure collapsed.

The ship began rotating.

Slowly at first.

Then faster.

The Falken cadet looked at the navigation display.

“We’re drifting out from behind the battleship.”

Varek saw the problem immediately.

If they moved into open space—

the Directorate would find them.

And with one engine crippled, they would not escape.

“Can we stop rotation?”

“Port maneuvering only.”

“Use it.”

Elara fired thrusters.

The frigate fought itself.

The rotation slowed.

Then the damaged starboard engine coughed once.

A violent pulse of thrust shoved the ship sideways.

Straight toward the battleship.

“Kill that engine!”

“I DID!”

The Eikholm cadet sounded personally insulted.

“It apparently disagrees!”

The distance collapsed.

Five kilometers.

Four.

Three.

The battleship’s hull filled the forward display.

Varek could see ancient impact craters now.

Armor peeled back like dead skin.

One enormous hangar aperture lay ahead.

Its doors were broken open.

Darkness inside.

Elara stared.

“We’re going to hit.”

The Skar cadet answered:

“Then aim for the hole.”

Everyone looked at him.

“What?”

“If we hit armor, we die.”

He pointed at the hangar.

“If we hit that, we might only die.”

Elara looked toward Varek.

He nodded.

“He’s right.”

“I hate this crew.”

The Wayfarer’s Debt fired every remaining maneuvering thruster.

The frigate rolled.

Missed a broken antenna mast by meters.

Its port side scraped along the ancient battleship’s outer armor.

Metal screamed through the entire ship.

Compartments shook.

Panels exploded.

Then the frigate crossed the ruined hangar mouth.

For a moment there was nothing.

Then the old battleship’s internal gravity caught them.

Weak.

Erratic.

Enough.

The Wayfarer’s Debt dropped.

Hard.

The frigate hit the hangar deck sideways.

Landing gear collapsed.

One wing of armor tore away.

The boarding cutter beneath the spine snapped loose and skidded across the deck in a shower of sparks.

The ship slid hundreds of meters.

Then stopped against the wreck of an ancient strike craft.

Silence.

For several seconds nobody moved.

There was a moment in which someone might have made a joke.

Nobody here did.

Elara slowly opened her eyes.

“Status.”

The answers came one after another.

“Main reactor stable.”

“Port engine damaged.”

“Starboard engine destroyed.”

“Jump coils unstable.”

“Point defense offline.”

“Primary comms dead.”

“Secondary comms intermittent.”

“Hull breaches sealed on Decks Two and Three.”

The Eikholm cadet paused.

Then:

“We are not flying out of here soon.”

Varek looked around the bridge.

“That was the good news?”

“Yes.”

Outside the viewport lay darkness.

The dead battleship’s hangar was immense.

Old cranes.

Collapsed gantries.

Rows of ruined small craft.

Containers still locked into magnetic clamps.

Most disturbing of all—

lights.

Very faint.

Along the far wall.

Emergency strips.

Still active.

After more than a century.

The Falken cadet whispered:

“That should not be possible.”

The Winge cadet answered:

“It is possible because we are looking at it.”

No one appreciated that.

Then another sound moved through the hull.

A deep metallic thump.

Not their frigate.

The battleship.

Something far inside had activated.

Then another.

Power moving through ancient systems.

The life-support signal strengthened.

LIFE SUPPORT ACTIVE.

A second line appeared.

SECTION 19 PRESSURIZED.

Then:

CREW STATUS: UNKNOWN.

Elara stood.

“We stay aboard.”

Varek shook his head.

“No.”

She looked at him.

“Our ship is damaged.”

“Exactly.”

“If something is alive in there—”

“We need it.”

Silence.

Varek pointed toward engineering data.

“We cannot repair this ship without parts.”

The Eikholm cadet nodded reluctantly.

“He’s right.”

Varek continued.

“This battleship has power.”

“Atmosphere.”

“Possibly fabrication.”

“Possibly tools.”

“And if we can restore even limited systems, we may be able to hide here until House reinforcements reach the system.”

The Skar cadet smiled.

“And if the Directorate comes inside?”

Varek looked toward the dark hangar.

“Then they lose the advantage of having a warship.”

That smile grew.

Elara sighed.

“You really were Directorate.”

“Yes.”

They armed themselves.

Not everyone.

The ship still needed guards.

Elara kept half the cadets aboard.

Varek took a small team.

One Falken cadet.

One Eikholm engineer.

Two Skar cadets.

And the Winge cadet, who refused to stay behind.

“Why are you coming?” Varek asked.

“Unknown occupied military vessel.”

“That does not answer me.”

“Potential salvage claim, possible surviving crew, legal status uncertain.”

Varek stared.

The cadet added:

“And I want to know what happened here.”

Better answer.

They cycled through the damaged boarding hatch.

The battleship’s hangar still held atmosphere.

Thin.

Cold.

Ancient.

Their boots touched the deck.

The scale of the place was overwhelming.

The Wayfarer’s Debt looked tiny behind them.

A small wounded frigate inside the belly of a dead giant.

The Eikholm cadet swept a scanner across the nearest systems.

“I have power conduits.”

“Old.”

“Very old.”

“Still active.”

Then the Falken cadet raised one hand.

“Movement.”

Everyone froze.

“Where?”

He pointed toward a maintenance doorway far across the hangar.

A shadow had moved.

Human-sized.

Then disappeared.

The Skar cadets raised weapons.

Varek did not.

Not yet.

He called out.

“This is Commander Varek Solan.”

No response.

“Your ship transmitted active life support.”

Still nothing.

Then a speaker above them cracked to life.

Static.

A voice followed.

Weak.

Mechanical.

Old.

“Commander…?”

Varek looked up.

The voice continued.

“State fleet identification.”

Varek hesitated.

Then answered:

“No fleet.”

Static.

Then:

“Unauthorized answer.”

Lights came on across the hangar.

One row.

Then another.

Then another.

Ancient defense turrets unfolded from the ceiling.

The Skar cadets raised rifles.

Varek shouted:

“Hold fire!”

The Winge cadet stared upward.

“How many?”

“Too many.”

The ancient voice spoke again.

“State identification.”

Varek looked at the House cadets.

Then at the dead battleship.

And made a decision.

“Independent mercenary vessel under House von Hjertstedt-associated charter personnel.”

Silence.

Long enough to be frightening.

Then every turret stopped moving.

The voice returned.

Different now.

Almost curious.

“House… von Hjertstedt.”

The Falken cadet looked toward Varek.

“It knows the House.”

The House name still meant something to its surviving systems.

This ship had been presumed dead for more than a century.

Then the ancient voice said:

“Proceed to Section Nineteen.”

A bulkhead door opened at the far end of the hangar.

Warm light spilled through.

And from somewhere beyond it—

someone coughed.

A real human cough.

## 9. The Orb in Section Nineteen

Varek led the team through the opened bulkhead.

Section Nineteen was nothing like the ruined hangar.

It was clean.

Not new.

Not polished.

But maintained.

Ancient corridors glowed with steady white light. Atmosphere was warmer here. Repair drones moved along the ceiling rails. Old blast doors stood open as if someone had been expecting them for a very long time.

The Eikholm cadet looked around in disbelief.

“This part is alive.”

“Yes,” said a voice ahead of them.

They stopped.

A small machine floated out from the next corridor.

It was a smooth metallic orb about the size of a human torso. Three articulated arms unfolded from its sides, each ending in different tools—one delicate manipulator, one heavy utility claw, and one multi-sensor assembly glowing pale blue. A narrow band of light moved around its center like an eye.

It hovered in front of them with complete control.

The Skar cadets raised their weapons.

Varek raised a hand.

“Hold.”

The orb rotated toward him.

“Wise.”

Its voice was the same one from the hangar speaker, but clearer now. Old, precise, and somehow tired.

“I am Custodian Intelligence Unit Aster-9,” it said. “Acting command authority of battleship Saint Varos.”

The Winge cadet blinked.

“Acting command authority?”

Aster-9’s light dimmed for a moment.

“Captain dead. Executive dead. Tactical dead. Engineering dead. Chain of command collapsed. I remained.”

Varek appreciated the simplicity of the answer.

He stepped forward slightly.

“You transmitted life support.”

“Yes.”

“You asked for fleet identification.”

“Yes.”

“You recognized House von Hjertstedt.”

The orb turned toward the cadets.

“Archived recognition markers. Old. Valid enough.”

The Falken cadet frowned.

“How old?”

“Older than you.”

That irritated him.

Elara would probably have approved.

Varek studied the machine.

“You said the ship never died.”

Aster-9 floated closer.

“Incorrect historical conclusion spread by outside observers. The Saint Varos was crippled, isolated, and written off. Not dead.”

The Eikholm cadet looked around.

“You kept all this running by yourself?”

“Not by myself.”

From wall alcoves and side passages, more small drones emerged. Not intelligent like Aster-9, but clearly networked to it. Maintenance units. Power carriers. Fabrication assistants.

Aster-9 rotated one arm toward the corridor ahead.

“Section Nineteen survived. Therefore, I survived.”

Then alarms blared across the compartment.

Red light flashed.

The calm vanished.

Aster-9’s light sharpened.

“Update,” it said. “Unwelcome guests entering Hangar Six.”

Varek swore quietly.

The Directorate.

Aster-9 continued immediately.

“Boarding craft detected. Hull signatures correspond to modern hunter doctrine. Probability: hostile retrieval operation.”

The Skar cadet smiled grimly.

“So they came inside.”

Varek looked at the orb.

“Can they reach us?”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Depends how much of the ship I am allowed to wake.”

That was an important sentence.

Varek stepped closer.

“How much of the ship is left?”

Aster-9’s central light brightened.

“Enough to make boarding unpleasant.”

The Winge cadet asked the next question first.

“Legal owner of the ship?”

Everyone looked at him.

He shrugged.

“It matters.”

Aster-9 answered.

“Original state dissolved. Command crew deceased. Vessel abandoned by law of every registry I possess.”

The Winge cadet nodded.

“Excellent. Temporary emergency salvage-defense claim may be arguable.”

Varek stared at him.

“We are about to be boarded.”

“Yes,” said the cadet. “I am staying organized.”

Aster-9 turned one sensor arm toward him.

“I approve of this one.”

“Thank you.”

Back aboard the Wayfarer’s Debt, Elara received the same warning at the same time.

“Multiple hostile boarders!” one crewman shouted.

The damaged frigate could barely defend itself. Half its systems were offline, and the hangar around it provided too many shadowed angles.

Elara opened the squad channel.

“Varek.”

His voice came back at once.

“We know.”

“Directorate teams are inside.”

“So are we.”

“Useful.”

“Working on it.”

Elara checked the hangar display. Three Directorate boarding teams had entered through different breaches in the battleship’s hull. Smart. Methodical. They were not rushing straight at the frigate. They were spreading out to find and trap.

Exactly the kind of thing Varek himself might once have ordered.

Elara keyed the general alarm.

“All hands, defensive positions. If they take the Wayfarer’s Debt, we lose our fallback.”

One of the younger crew asked the obvious question.

“What fallback?”

Elara thought about it.

“The idea of a fallback.”

Section Nineteen changed shape around them.

Bulkheads sealed.

Corridors narrowed.

Ancient tactical displays flickered to life.

Aster-9 projected a crude map into the air between its arms.

Blue lines marked their position.

Red lines spread through the outer ship.

“Hostiles entering from Hangar Six, breached port spine, and dorsal gunnery access.”

Varek studied the map.

“They’re herding.”

“Yes.”

“Toward the frigate?”

“Yes.”

“Then they still think I’m aboard.”

Aster-9 turned slightly.

“Are you?”

“For the moment, no.”

“Then we may exploit their mistake.”

The orb extended one mechanical arm and opened a wall compartment. Inside were old weapons. Simple, rugged, heavy.

The Skar cadets both smiled.

“Now this,” said one, “looks promising.”

Aster-9 ignored him.

“Section Nineteen possesses internal defense capability. Limited. Ancient. Reliable.”

Varek picked up one of the weapons. Solid. Magnetic action. Old warship security rifle.

“Can you guide us?”

“Yes.”

“Can you seal them in?”

“Sometimes.”

“Can you kill them?”

Aster-9 paused.

“I can assist motivated organics.”

That was good enough.

The Winge cadet looked toward the map.

“They’ll eventually search inward.”

Varek nodded.

“Then we don’t wait.”

He pointed at the nearest red path.

“We hit the first team before the others coordinate.”

The Falken cadet stared at him.

“We’re attacking trained Directorate boarders inside a dead warship.”

“Yes.”

“Good,” said the Skar cadet. “That sounds like a proper assignment.”

The first Directorate team entered the service corridor outside Section Nineteen three minutes later.

Eight soldiers.

Disciplined.

Tight formation.

Two drones ahead.

Weapons scanning every angle.

Their leader transmitted calmly.

“No visual on Solan. Continue inward.”

Varek crouched behind an armored doorway with the cadets on either side.

Aster-9 floated in darkness above them, lights dimmed completely.

The first drone passed.

Then the second.

Aster-9 dropped silently and drove one articulated arm through the lead drone’s sensor cluster. It died without a sound.

The second drone turned—

and the corridor erupted.

Ancient security guns fired from hidden ports in the walls.

The Skar cadets opened up from both sides.

Varek stepped into the lane and shot the team leader first.

The Directorate boarders reacted fast.

But not fast enough.

One tried to fall back and found the rear bulkhead slamming shut behind him.

Aster-9 had sealed the corridor.

The fight became brutal and short.

When it ended, six Directorate soldiers were down, one was crawling, and one had sealed himself behind a portable shield farther back.

He shouted through the smoke.

“Commander Solan! Surrender and this ends!”

Varek stepped forward into view.

“Liar.”

The boarder fired.

Varek dropped aside.

Aster-9 cut the corridor lights.

The surviving boarder screamed once.

Then stopped.

Silence returned.

The orb floated back into view.

“Your former associates are impolite.”

Varek reloaded the old rifle.

“Yes.”

Behind them, new red markers changed direction on the holographic map.

The other Directorate teams had heard enough.

Now they were coming straight for Section Nineteen.

## 10. The Saint Varos Wakes

The second Directorate boarding team came faster.

They had learned.

No drones this time.

No careful sweep.

They hit Section Nineteen with breaching charges.

The first blast bent the outer bulkhead inward.

The second tore it open.

Aster-9 snapped upward.

“Defensive grid awakening.”

Varek checked his rifle.

“How much?”

“Insufficient.”

“That is becoming a common answer.”

“Then stop asking unfortunate questions.”

The Skar cadet laughed.

Then the Directorate came through.

They entered under shields.

Heavy infantry first.

Two armored troopers pushed into the breach with portable barriers while rifle teams fired through the gaps.

Ancient metal screamed under impacts.

Varek dropped behind cover.

“LEFT SIDE!”

A Skar cadet fired.

Rounds hammered against the lead shield.

Nothing.

Too heavy.

The Winge cadet dragged a wounded Falken trainee behind an old machinery block.

“Armor breach!”

“Seal it!”

“I AM!”

Aster-9 disappeared through an overhead maintenance opening.

Then the corridor lights turned red.

“Where is it going?” Varek shouted.

Aster-9 answered through the ship itself.

“Finding larger arguments.”

The Directorate advanced.

Five meters.

Four.

Three.

Their squad leader shouted:

“SOLAN! YOU ARE ORDERED TO SURRENDER!”

Joel looked at the six-armed Mechanist on another feed.

“I assumed that was why you brought half a salvage command.”

“Full salvage command.”

“That does not improve the sentence.”

Then Joel turned his attention toward the Directorate fleet.

His expression hardened.

“Directorate commander.”

A reply came.

“High Marshal.”

“You fired on House cadets.”

“They were harboring a fugitive.”

“You fired on House salvage vessels.”

“They violated security exclusion.”

Joel leaned slightly forward.

“Provide lawful claim over the Keros Grave.”

Silence.

“Provide warrant for Solan.”

More silence.

“Provide justification for live fire against cadets under House charter.”

Nothing.

Joel nodded once.

“Understood.”

The Directorate commander changed tone.

“This is internal Directorate business.”

Joel answered:

“It stopped being internal when you shot my people.”

That sentence spread across every House ship.

The Grave seemed to become colder.

Then the Directorate did something unexpected.

More ships entered from their rear.

Reinforcements.

Heavy ones.

The tactical display filled.

Torvald stopped looking amused.

Varek felt something old and unpleasant settle in his stomach.

He recognized the formation.

“Exorcist hunters.”

Joel looked toward him over secure channel.

“You know them?”

“Yes.”

“What are they for?”

Varek watched the Directorate ships take position.

“Destroying evidence they cannot recover.”

Aster-9's central light brightened.

“Clarification.”

Varek looked at it.

“They're not here to take the Grave anymore.”

He looked toward Joel.

“They're here to burn it.”

And across the debris field, Directorate missile doors began opening.

## 14. The Wall in the Grave

The Directorate launched first.

Not a probing salvo.

Not a warning.

Hundreds of missiles broke from the reinforced formation and spread through the Keros Grave.

Some targeted House ships.

Others did not.

Varek saw that immediately.

“They’re firing at the wrecks.”

Joel saw it too.

“Confirmed.”

Aster-9’s arms snapped outward.

“They are targeting Saint Varos.”

“And the other active signatures,” Varek said.

The Directorate intended to erase the evidence.

Joel’s response came without hesitation.

“All House elements.”

His voice crossed every ship in the Grave.

“Defensive Wall.”

The fleet moved.

Vigil frigates accelerated ahead of the Exorcist salvage vessels.

Destroyers took the gaps.

Oath-cruisers formed behind them.

Joel’s Banner capital settled at the center.

Not a clean formation.

The Grave made that impossible.

Instead, House ships used the wrecks themselves.

A cruiser positioned behind the broken hull of an ancient battleship.

Two frigates disappeared between rotating asteroids.

A destroyer drifted cold beside the remains of a station spine.

Every vessel took responsibility for a section of the debris field.

Joel looked over the tactical display.

“Nothing reaches the salvage teams.”

His tactical officer answered:

“That includes wreck targets?”

“Yes.”

“High Marshal, some of those wrecks are larger than our cruisers.”

“Then they should be easy to hide behind.”

The first missiles arrived.

Point defense became weather.

The Grave lit up.

Thousands of defensive rounds crossed the darkness.

Interceptor missiles launched from House escorts.

Beam systems cut through incoming warheads.

Ancient wrecks took impacts intended for living ships.

One missile struck an asteroid and shattered it into a cloud of lethal fragments.

“DEBRIS BLOOM!”

House point defense immediately shifted priorities.

Fragments vanished under defensive fire.

Not all.

A Vigil frigate took several impacts across its armor.

Another lost a sensor mast.

Neither left formation.

Joel watched casualty reports appear.

“Hold the wall.”

Inside the Wayfarer’s Debt, the cadets watched the battle from behind two House frigates.

The damaged mercenary ship shook as fragments struck its patched hull.

Captain Elara looked toward Varek.

“Your former government seems determined.”

“They are.”

“To kill you?”

Varek shook his head.

“Not anymore.”

He pointed at the old wrecks.

“This is bigger.”

Aster-9 floated toward the tactical display.

Several of its mechanical arms trembled slightly.

Elara noticed.

“You all right?”

“I have previously explained—”

“Yes. You don’t possess that function.”

Aster went quiet.

Then:

“Those ships were once part of my fleet.”

Varek looked at it.

“You recognize them?”

“Some.”

The AI highlighted three wrecks.

A cruiser.

A carrier.

Another battleship buried partially inside a tumbling mass of stone and metal.

“Designation records damaged.”

Then another.

“And that one.”

Its light dimmed.

“Crew complement: eleven thousand four hundred and six.”

The Directorate fired another salvo toward it.

Aster-9 said quietly:

“Do not permit them to destroy it.”

Varek looked toward the House wall.

“I don’t think Joel intends to.”

Torvald Krenn had received the same order.

Nothing reaches the salvage teams.

He interpreted that in his own way.

“Continue work.”

His deputy stared at him.

“We are under fleet bombardment.”

“Yes.”

“The wreck we are attached to is being targeted.”

“Yes.”

“You want us to keep cutting?”

Torvald’s mechanical arms continued manipulating three separate control systems.

“Yes.”

The deputy looked toward the viewport.

House frigates were exchanging fire less than forty kilometers away.

“Master Mechanist—”

Torvald turned his augmented eye toward him.

“If Joel did not want me working, he would have told me to withdraw.”

Another impact shook the salvage cruiser.

Torvald smiled.

“He told me to work.”

The cutting teams continued.

The Directorate tried to break the wall.

Destroyers accelerated through the wreck field.

Fast.

Dangerously fast.

They were betting House doctrine would stay defensive.

Joel allowed them to believe that.

“Hold.”

The Directorate ships closed.

Hammer Range.

“Hold.”

One destroyer fired.

A House cruiser absorbed the strike across reinforced armor.

Damage warnings spread.

Joel waited.

Then:

“Now.”

Three hidden Vigil frigates emerged from behind wreckage.

A House destroyer rose from beneath an asteroid.

The Directorate formation suddenly found itself exposed from four angles.

House fire concentrated.

Not reactors.

Drives.

Weapons.

Sensors.

The first Directorate destroyer lost propulsion.

It continued forward under momentum and slammed into a wrecked cruiser.

The second lost its forward weapon mounts.

The third tried to reverse.

A House frigate destroyed its maneuvering thrusters.

Joel opened a channel.

“Directorate ships. Power down and surrender.”

No reply.

Instead, their command fleet launched again.

Varek watched the new trajectories.

“They are adapting.”

Joel heard him over the command net.

“How?”

“They’re going around your line.”

The tactical map expanded.

Several Directorate cruisers were moving deeper into the Grave using old wreck corridors.

Not attacking the wall.

Flanking the Exorcists.

Joel understood.

“Torvald.”

The Mechanist answered instantly.

“Busy.”

“You are about to become significantly busier.”

“I assumed.”

“Enemy cruisers moving toward your salvage groups.”

Torvald looked at his own tactical picture.

Then smiled.

“Good.”

Joel stared at the feed.

“That is not the correct response.”

“I have salvage tugs.”

“You have what?”

Torvald extended two mechanical arms toward his officers.

“Release tow anchors.”

Across the Grave, massive House salvage tugs detached from ancient wrecks.

Not retreating.

Moving.

Several carried heavy magnetic grapples.

Others had powerful industrial thrusters designed to move dead capital ships.

Torvald marked three drifting wrecks.

“Push those.”

The deputy stared.

“Tow them?”

“No.”

Torvald pointed toward the Directorate flanking corridor.

“Push.”

The tugs fired.

Ancient warships began moving.

Slowly.

Thousands of tonnes of dead metal shifted into the Directorate approach.

One cruiser suddenly found an entire dead frigate drifting across its firing line.

Another had to burn hard to avoid the bow of an ancient battleship.

The neat flanking maneuver collapsed.

Joel watched the tactical display.

For several seconds he said nothing.

Then:

“Torvald.”

“Yes?”

“Remind me never to fight you in a scrapyard.”

“Excellent policy.”

Then the Directorate command ships accelerated.

This time the objective was obvious.

Break the center.

Reach the Saint Varos.

Destroy Aster-9’s ship before House salvage teams could secure its archives.

Joel stood from his command chair.

“Wall elements.”

The House formation tightened.

Damaged ships stayed.

Exorcists worked behind them.

The Wayfarer’s Debt remained protected at the center.

“Whatever they came here to erase,” Joel said, “we are going to learn why.”

The Directorate fleet entered Hammer Range.

Joel’s eyes narrowed.

“Hold.”

Missile doors opened.

Spinal weapons aligned.

Point-defense systems screamed across the Grave.

Then Aster-9 suddenly turned toward the Wayfarer’s Debt sensor display.

“High Marshal.”

Joel heard it.

“What?”

Aster highlighted something behind the Directorate formation.

A faint signature.

Then another.

House authentication.

Heavy.

Very heavy.

Aster-9 said:

“Additional friendly fleet approaching.”

Joel looked at the incoming authority code.

His expression changed.

Not surprise.

Recognition.

“Father.”

The heavier force dispatched from Vintergard had completed its approach. Lars von Hjertstedt was coming.

And he was not coming with a patrol group.

## 15. When the Primarch Arrived

The Directorate capital ship broke through Joel’s center line.

Not cleanly.

Two House frigates crippled its forward batteries. An Oath-cruiser tore open its port armor. Torvald’s salvage tugs pushed a dead destroyer across its escort screen.

But the capital ship kept coming.

Straight toward the Saint Varos.

Varek watched it from the Wayfarer’s Debt.

“They’ve committed everything.”

Joel saw the same thing.

“Center elements, close.”

His damaged wall compressed around the ancient battleship.

The Directorate ship fired.

A House cruiser took the first strike.

Its shields collapsed.

Armor broke open along the bow.

Still it remained in position.

The second Directorate volley passed through the gap.

Toward the Saint Varos.

Aster-9 watched the incoming fire.

For once, the AI said nothing.

Then space behind the Directorate fleet changed.

A drive bloom appeared.

Huge.

Then another.

Then dozens.

House authentication flooded the tactical network.

Heavy capitals.

Oath-cruisers.

Destroyer groups.

Vigil screens.

Sanctuary support.

And behind them came more formations still.

Not a reinforcement package.

A war fleet.

At its center was the Primarch’s command vessel.

Lars von Hjertstedt had arrived.

The Directorate stopped firing.

Almost instantly.

Varek stared at the tactical display.

“That fast?”

Joel understood.

“Yes.”

The Directorate capital still advancing toward the Saint Varos cut thrust so violently that its drive signature spiked.

Its escorts turned.

Missile doors closed.

Electronic warfare shifted from attack to withdrawal coverage.

The entire Directorate battle line began disengaging.

No final strike.

No attempt to finish Varek.

No effort to destroy the ancient wrecks.

They were leaving.

Captain Elara looked toward Varek.

“They were ready to start a war with Joel.”

Varek watched Lars’s fleet enter formation.

“They were ready to gamble against Joel.”

Aster-9 rotated toward the massive incoming signatures.

“And this individual alters the probability?”

The Winge cadet answered:

“Significantly.”

Lars did not shout.

That somehow made his arrival worse.

His voice entered the shared command network.

“Joel.”

Joel stood straighter.

“Father.”

“Status.”

“Cadets alive.”

“Good.”

“Wayfarer’s Debt damaged.”

“Recoverable?”

Torvald answered before Joel could.

“Yes.”

Lars paused.

“Torvald.”

“Primarch.”

“You brought the full Exorcist fleet.”

“Yes.”

Another pause.

“We will discuss that later.”

Torvald looked almost pleased.

Joel continued.

“Directorate fired repeatedly on House personnel and salvage assets. Winge has full records.”

Mikael’s people immediately transmitted the evidence package.

Lars reviewed only the summary.

“That is enough.”

He looked toward the retreating Directorate fleet.

“Do not pursue.”

Several House captains had clearly been waiting for permission.

Anton’s voice came over a secondary command channel.

“That is disappointing.”

Joel turned toward the feed.

“You came too?”

Anton smiled from another bridge.

“Father said heavy response.”

Lars spoke without looking at him.

“I also said discipline.”

Anton’s smile disappeared.

“Understood.”

On the family circuit, Christer observed, “Father has stopped the whole fleet without boarding anything.”

“No breach credit,” Anton said.

Lars answered before Joel could.

“Keep the credit. I will keep the ships.”

Joel muted his microphone long enough to laugh once. Then he returned to the recovery plot. None of the House vessels pursued.

The Directorate commander finally transmitted.

“Primarch von Hjertstedt.”

Lars answered.

“Commander.”

“We are withdrawing to prevent unnecessary escalation.”

Lars looked at the tactical display.

“No.”

Silence followed.

The Directorate officer stiffened.

Lars continued.

“You are withdrawing because you lost control of the situation.”

The channel remained open.

“You fired upon House cadets.”

“You fired upon House vessels.”

“You attempted to destroy assets under active House recovery.”

“You failed to produce a lawful warrant for Commander Solan.”

The Directorate commander answered carefully.

“Solan remains Directorate property under internal security authority.”

Varek’s face hardened.

Lars looked directly into the transmission.

“A human being is not property.”

The Winge cadet quietly marked the statement into the legal record.

Lars continued.

“If you possess charges, send them.”

“If you possess evidence, send it.”

“If you possess a warrant recognized outside your own guns, send that too.”

“Until then, Varek Solan remains under House protection while this matter is reviewed.”

Varek looked sharply toward Joel.

Joel gave him the smallest shrug.

Apparently that had just happened.

The Directorate commander said:

“You are interfering in sovereign Directorate affairs.”

Lars answered:

“You made it my affair when you fired on my family.”

The channel closed.

The Directorate fleet continued retreating.

Faster now.

House ships moved through the Grave.

Not chasing.

Securing.

Damaged vessels received escorts.

Sanctuary ships moved toward casualties.

Winge teams began collecting battle evidence.

Torvald immediately resumed salvage operations as though a fleet battle had merely interrupted his schedule.

His tugs moved toward the Saint Varos.

Aster-9 watched them.

“Those vessels intend to dismantle my ship?”

Torvald opened a channel instantly.

“Absolutely not.”

Aster-9 turned toward the speaker.

Torvald continued.

“I intend to save it.”

A pause.

“How?”

Torvald’s mechanical arms unfolded behind him.

“Carefully.”

The Eikholm cadet whispered:

“That is a lie.”

Torvald heard him.

“Mostly carefully.”

Lars’s fleet reached the central wreck field.

His command ship passed between the ancient dead hulls while House survey drones mapped safe corridors ahead.

For the first time, the Keros Grave no longer looked abandoned.

Navigation beacons appeared.

Recovery markers.

Medical corridors.

Salvage exclusions.

House heraldry moved among ships that had been dead for a century.

Joel transferred to the Wayfarer’s Debt personally.

When he entered its bridge, the cadets immediately stood.

He looked at the patched walls.

The damaged controls.

The repair drones.

Then Aster-9 floated toward him.

“High Marshal Joel von Hjertstedt.”

Joel stopped.

“You know me?”

“Public command records supplied by your Winge cadet,” Aster-9 said.

Joel looked toward the cadet.

“A local copy,” the cadet said. “Names and ranks. The engineering restrictions remain.”

“How much did that tell you?” Joel asked.

“Insufficient.”

“For now, it will have to do.”

Then Lars appeared on the main display.

His attention settled on Varek.

“Commander Solan.”

Varek straightened automatically.

“Primarch.”

“I have read the first report.”

Varek waited.

Lars continued:

“Your government sent a war fleet into a graveyard, fired on my cadets, attacked my salvage force, and attempted to destroy century-old military assets rather than allow us to inspect them.”

“Yes.”

Lars leaned forward.

“So now you are going to tell us why.”

Varek looked toward the retreating Directorate fleet.

Then toward Aster-9.

Then at the House forces filling the Grave.

For the first time since he had been relieved of command, he was somewhere the Directorate could not simply make him disappear.

“All right,” he said.

“I’ll tell you everything.”

## 16. The Prisoner of Winter

Varek expected interrogation.

Instead, Lars gave him a choice.

“Come with us.”

Varek looked across the command deck toward the wreck field.

“To where?”

Lars did not answer.

Joel did.

“Somewhere the Directorate cannot reach you.”

That narrowed it less than Varek would have liked.

Behind them, the Exorcist fleet was spreading through the Keros Grave. Torvald’s salvage groups had already attached themselves to the Saint Varos, while Aster-9 argued with three House engineers simultaneously.

The Directorate fleet was retreating.

For now.

But Varek understood Directorate doctrine.

They would return.

Next time they would come prepared.

Lars apparently understood that too.

“You will explain everything,” the Primarch said.

Varek nodded.

“I said I would.”

“Not here.”

Lars looked toward the wreck field.

“Too many sensors. Too many ships. Too many people listening.”

Varek folded his arms.

“And wherever you're taking me?”

Lars’s expression did not change.

“You will be a prisoner.”

That surprised him.

Joel stepped beside his father.

“Protected prisoner.”

Varek looked toward him.

“There is a difference?”

“Yes.”

“Explain.”

Joel answered calmly.

“No one tortures you.”

“No one disappears you.”

“No Directorate extraction team reaches you.”

“You receive food, medical care, legal representation, and a Winge review.”

Varek waited.

“And the prisoner part?”

“You do not leave.”

Clear enough.

The Winge Justicar assigned to the operation entered carrying a custody slate.

“Commander Varek Solan.”

Varek looked at him.

“Yes.”

“Pending verification of your claims, you are being placed under protective Charter custody.”

The Justicar displayed the terms.

No interrogation under chemical influence.

No physical coercion.

Medical confidentiality except where security required otherwise.

Right to challenge detention before a senior Justicar.

But also:

Restricted communications.

No unsupervised network access.

No weapons.

No navigation data.

No contact with Directorate representatives without Winge authorization.

Varek read it.

“You've prepared this quickly.”

The Justicar almost looked offended.

“We are Winge.”

Joel smiled faintly.

Varek read the right to challenge detention a second time. The restrictions were real; so was the promise that he would have someone to speak for him. He looked back toward the Grave, where Elara’s ship was still being repaired.

He would not ask her cadets to carry this risk farther.

Varek signed.

The Justicar added the House seal.

“Custody accepted.”

Lars turned.

“Move.”

The ship waiting for them was built for speed.

Not a capital ship.

Not even a cruiser.

A long, narrow House courier with oversized engines, heavy point defense, and barely enough crew space for extended operations.

Hrafn’s Shadow.

Varek studied it as they crossed the docking tube.

“Fast.”

Joel nodded.

“Very.”

“Armed?”

“Enough.”

“Where are we going?”

Joel smiled slightly.

“You already asked.”

“And nobody answered.”

“That should tell you something.”

Two Black Guard waited inside.

Not cadets.

Not ordinary marines.

Black armor.

No wasted movement.

Varek immediately understood that running was not an option.

One Guard extended a hand.

“Sidearm.”

Varek looked at Lars.

Lars waited.

Varek removed the old Directorate pistol.

The weapon that had followed him through five lanes.

His last piece of rank.

He placed it in the Black Guard’s hand.

“Do I get that back?”

“If the review clears its return,” Lars said.

“And if I don't?”

Joel answered:

“It stays with your recorded effects while the review continues. You can contest the decision.”

The Hrafn’s Shadow departed the Keros Grave within the hour.

Joel came.

Lars came.

That alone told Varek how seriously they were taking him.

A High Marshal and a Primarch did not personally escort ordinary prisoners.

The first transit took them away from Calder Reach.

Then another.

Varek tried to follow the navigation.

The displays around him went blank before every major transition.

At first he assumed it was because he was a prisoner.

Then he noticed even the corridor windows polarized during certain approaches.

Authentication codes never appeared on visible systems.

Navigation personnel operated behind sealed compartments.

He looked toward Joel.

“This isn't normal security.”

“No.”

Another gate transit began.

Varek felt the deck change beneath him.

“Where are you taking me?”

Joel finally answered.

“Vintergard.”

Varek went still.

He knew the name.

Or rather—

the rumor.

Directorate intelligence had spent decades trying to prove where House von Hjertstedt came from.

Hidden anchorage.

Secret fortress.

Mobile headquarters.

Some analysts believed Vintergard did not exist at all.

Varek stared at Joel.

“Vintergard is real.”

Joel looked amused.

“You're about to become extremely inconvenient to Directorate intelligence.”

Even as prisoner, Varek was not permitted to see the Winter Road.

A copied credential alone could not open it. The route depended on layered authentication, correct approach, changing House authority, and further credentials beyond the first transit.

Still, the House took no chances.

Before the final sequence, the Black Guard escorted Varek into an internal cabin.

No windows.

No navigation access.

The door locked.

Joel entered with him.

Varek sat opposite him.

“Afraid I'll memorize something?”

“Yes.”

“At least you're honest.”

“Yes.”

Varek studied him.

“You trust me enough to bring me into your home.”

Joel shook his head.

“No.”

That answer came immediately.

“We trust our security enough to bring you there.”

Different thing.

Varek almost respected it more.

Several hours later, the cabin door opened.

Lars stood outside.

“We are in Vintergard.”

Varek stepped into the corridor.

He expected to see nothing.

Instead, when they reached a secured observation chamber, the armored shutters opened.

He stopped walking.

Traffic.

Thousands of lights.

Civilian freighters.

Patrol craft.

Orbital habitats.

Distant shipyards.

Traffic lanes full of vessels.

Not a hidden military base.

A civilization.

Varek stared.

Joel stood beside him.

“The Directorate always assumed we were hiding a fortress.”

“You were hiding this.”

Joel looked toward the living system beyond the glass.

“Yes.”

Varek understood immediately why Lars considered it the safest place in the galaxy.

The Directorate could not raid this place.

It could barely prove it existed.

Lars turned toward him.

“Your quarters are prepared.”

Varek looked back.

“Cell?”

“Comfortable cell.”

“That is reassuring.”

“You remain under Winge authority until your claims are verified.”

Varek nodded.

“And then?”

Lars's crimson eyes settled on him.

“Then we decide whether you are a hunted man telling the truth—”

Joel finished quietly:

“—or a Directorate weapon that walked willingly through our front door.”

Varek looked out across Vintergard.

For the first time since his command had been stripped away, he was safe.

And for the first time—

being safe felt very much like being trapped.
