# Chapter 8: The Price of Victory

## 1. The Ships That Did Not Return

The evacuation convoy was seventeen minutes from Gate Seventeen when the first Garooth missiles appeared.

Joel von Hjertstedt stood behind the tactical officer aboard the House command escort, studying the long procession of vessels making their way toward the protected transit corridor. Medical transports occupied the center, surrounded by refugee carriers, engineering ships, and damaged warships returning from Ghar-Kul. Several carried thousands of former prisoners who had spent years believing they would never see their homes again. The escorts had maintained formation for six hours without incident. Ahead, the gate's reception beacons transmitted a steady green approach corridor. Behind them, the victorious joint fleet continued its patrols around the captured fortress.

"Three unidentified launches," the tactical officer reported. "Bearing two-eight-six. No active engine contacts."

Joel looked toward the indicated wreck field. "Classification?"

"Missile signatures. Garooth pattern. Additional launches detected."

"Convoy defensive formation. All escorts, weapons free against confirmed threats."

The first defensive interceptors were already leaving their tubes when the wreck field erupted.

Two Garooth missile barges emerged from behind the broken remains of an industrial carrier. Their engines had been cold, their hulls concealed within a mass of metal that the recovery teams had classified as battlefield debris. Now their launch racks opened together. Hundreds of missiles scattered into separate trajectories before turning toward the convoy.

"Contact bloom! Multiple hostile vessels!"

Anton reached the tactical rail. "How did those bastards get behind the screen?"

"Ask later," Joel answered. "Protect the transports."

The first interception wave destroyed most of the incoming weapons. Frigates rotated toward the threat, point-defense batteries filling the darkness with overlapping streams of fire. But the Garooth had launched from dangerously close range, and several missiles had already crossed the defensive envelope.

Transport Six carried 2,416 liberated prisoners and medical personnel.

Its captain reported the first impact against the starboard engine housing. The second missile struck a passenger compartment, rupturing two decks and severing the main power trunk. Emergency bulkheads closed, but pressure alarms spread faster than the crew could isolate them.

"We have casualties," the captain transmitted. "Requesting immediate assistance. We have over two thousand people aboard."

Joel ordered the nearest frigate to cover the transport.

Before it could arrive, four additional missiles struck.

The transport's rear section separated from its passenger modules. A chain of internal explosions passed through the damaged structure. On Joel's tactical display, the vessel broke into three independent contacts.

Then its transponder disappeared.

Nobody on the bridge spoke.

"Transport Nine taking fire!" the tactical officer shouted.

The second carrier had 1,873 people aboard, most of them families recovered from Ghar-Kul's industrial prison sections. Its captain had seen what happened to Transport Six and attempted an emergency course change. The ship accelerated toward the protective shadow of a House destroyer.

It almost reached it.

A Garooth missile struck the forward maneuvering assembly. Another penetrated the passenger spine. The carrier's hull twisted under uneven thrust, tearing open compartments that had survived the initial explosions.

The destroyer moved alongside it, extending rescue craft before the transport had stopped breaking apart.

Then the Garooth fired again.

A heavy projectile passed through the destroyer's defensive screen and struck its reactor compartment. The warship vanished inside a brilliant white flash. Fragments tore through the crippled transport beside it, destroying the remaining inhabited sections.

"Three ships lost," the tactical officer reported, his voice unsteady. "Multiple escape pods detected. Rescue numbers unknown."

Joel gripped the command rail.

"All available rescue craft launch. No pursuit beyond the escort boundary. Protect the survivors."

***

Aboard the House medical transport *Mercy*, Bjorn von Hjertstedt was dying.

The first indication had been a sudden fall in blood pressure during the emergency maneuver. The elderly Crimson Warden veteran had been recovering from severe injuries sustained inside Ghar-Kul, but the compression damage to his chest and abdomen had been worse than the original examinations suggested.

A weakened blood vessel had ruptured.

Within minutes, internal bleeding overwhelmed the treatment that had kept him stable for six days.

The medical team transferred him directly into surgery.

Two senior House trauma surgeons worked alongside a Talyrn vascular specialist. Automated systems maintained circulation while surgical instruments opened the damaged tissue. Synthetic blood products entered through multiple lines. The chief surgeon identified the rupture and began repairing it.

"Pressure falling."

"Increase circulatory support."

"Cardiac rhythm unstable."

"Prepare direct pacing."

Bjorn's heart stopped.

The surgical team began immediate resuscitation. Mechanical circulation maintained blood flow while the surgeons repaired the vessel with a synthetic graft. For thirty seconds, the heart resumed beating.

Then the monitors began alarming again.

"Second hemorrhage. Deep abdominal cavity."

The Talyrn specialist looked toward the chief surgeon.

"We can isolate it."

"Do it."

Outside the operating chamber, another impact shook the transport.

The *Mercy* had been caught near the edge of the Garooth attack. Its escorts were forcing the surviving raiders away, but the ship's captain had ordered emergency acceleration toward the gate.

Inside surgery, the medical team fought to keep Bjorn alive.

***

Joel received the medical report while directing the recovery operation.

"His condition is critical," the surgeon told him. "We have repaired the primary vessel, but he has sustained extensive internal trauma. His circulation is failing."

"Can you stabilize him until we reach Vintergard?"

"We are attempting to."

"Use everything you have."

"We already are, High Marshal."

Joel looked toward Anton.

His brother had heard the exchange.

For once, Anton had nothing to say.

"Give me a direct medical channel," Joel ordered.

The surgeon connected him.

Bjorn lay beneath surgical equipment, his face pale against the sterile sheets. The old man's breathing was controlled by the machines surrounding him. His body had endured centuries of accumulated injuries, campaigns, and the final destruction of his beloved Warden.

Joel watched the medical team work.

"Come on, old man," Anton whispered.

A fresh alarm sounded.

The surgeon checked the monitors.

"We've lost effective circulation again."

"Restart it."

"We're trying."

They administered additional clotting treatment, replaced damaged blood components, and increased mechanical support. The Talyrn specialist attempted another vascular repair while the senior surgeon worked directly around the failing heart.

For several minutes, the monitors showed improvement.

Then the electrical activity became irregular.

The chief surgeon attempted another resuscitation.

Nothing.

Again.

Still nothing.

The medical team continued until the surgeon confirmed that circulation could no longer be restored and the damage was beyond recovery.

She looked toward the communication display.

"High Marshal."

Joel did not answer immediately.

"I'm sorry. We could not save him."

The chief surgeon recorded the time of death.

Anton lowered his head.

The bridge around them remained filled with casualty reports, rescue requests, and warnings from damaged vessels. The convoy was still moving. Somewhere behind them, thousands of people were waiting for rescue craft that might never reach them.

Joel closed the medical channel.

For several seconds, he remained motionless.

Then he turned toward the tactical officer.

"Status of our recovery teams?"

"Still operating among the wreckage, sir. We have hundreds of pods requesting pickup."

"Keep them covered."

Anton looked at his brother.

"Joel."

"I know."

Their eyes met.

Neither spoke of the wager. Neither mentioned the bottle Bjorn had intended to win.

Joel looked back toward the tactical display, where three great clouds of wreckage marked the ships that would never return.

"Inform Lars," he said quietly. "And tell the medical teams to keep searching."

Outside, House frigates maneuvered through burning debris, their rescue craft moving from one distress signal to another.

The battle had been won at Ghar-Kul.

But the Garooth had made certain that victory would be remembered differently.

And somewhere aboard a separate recovery ship, the battered Crimson Warden that had saved thousands of lives was still making its journey home.

Without its pilot.

---

## 2. The Names of the Lost

The first surviving transport crossed Gate Seventeen with eighty-three bodies in its emergency morgue and another two hundred wounded occupying every available treatment space.

Joel von Hjertstedt watched it emerge on the tactical display from the command escort's bridge. Beyond the gate, the Talyrn reception fleet had established a protected corridor toward three hospital stations. Medical shuttles were already moving toward the incoming ships, their running lights blinking against the enormous silhouettes of waiting cruisers. The convoy had survived the Garooth ambush, but its formation was broken. Some transports were missing entire sections. Others had arrived with emergency seals covering holes that had once been passenger compartments.

"Final vessel through," the tactical officer reported. "All surviving convoy elements accounted for."

Joel studied the display. "And the rescue groups?"

"Fourteen craft still operating in the wreck field. Two House frigates and a Talyrn escort remain on station. No additional survivors recovered in the last twenty minutes."

"Maintain their protection until the recovery commanders declare the search complete."

The officer acknowledged the order, but Joel could hear the exhaustion in his voice. Everyone aboard had been listening to distress transmissions for hours. Some had ended with explosions. Others had simply stopped.

***

The Talyrn reception station had prepared for thousands of liberated prisoners. Its medical decks were supplied, temporary accommodation had been opened, and families had gathered behind the protected arrival galleries.

Nobody had prepared for the names that would follow.

Joel entered the casualty coordination center beside Anton. Across the chamber, Talyrn and House officers worked together at long tables covered with identification records, survivor manifests, and transport assignments. A large display showed three columns: confirmed survivors, confirmed dead, and unaccounted for.

The final column was growing.

A Talyrn registration officer approached with a slate.

"High Marshal. We had 2,416 registered aboard Transport Six and 1,873 aboard Transport Nine. Those figures include the assigned medical and transport personnel. Some passengers were transferred before departure, and some were moved between ships after the boarding records closed. We are still reconciling the changes."

"How many confirmed recovered?"

"Four hundred and eleven from both transports. Another ninety-six emergency signals remain unresolved."

Anton stared at the figures. "And the destroyer?"

"Partial crew manifest recovered. We have escape-pod telemetry, but several pods stopped transmitting during the second missile attack."

Joel looked at the enormous list of names.

"Do not mark anyone dead because their signal disappeared."

The officer hesitated. "That will leave thousands listed as missing."

"Then thousands remain missing until we have evidence to say otherwise."

***

Lars von Hjertstedt joined the conference through a secured command link. His image appeared above the central table, the bridge of his own vessel visible behind him. He had already received the initial casualty report, but the information about Bjorn had reached him separately.

Joel could see that his father had not changed clothes since the operation began.

"How many ships?" Lars asked.

"Two passenger transports and one destroyer confirmed destroyed. Several others heavily damaged. The search is continuing."

"And Bjorn?"

Joel looked toward Anton before answering.

"He died aboard the *Mercy*. The surgeons attempted everything available. His injuries were worse than anyone understood when we evacuated him."

Lars closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, his expression remained controlled, but his voice had changed.

"I spoke to him before the assault on Ghar-Kul," he said. "He promised he would stop taking unnecessary risks."

Anton looked down.

"He never intended to keep that promise."

"No," Lars answered. "He never did."

For a moment, nobody spoke.

Then Lars asked, "Have Mikael and Rasmus been informed?"

"Not yet," Joel said. "I wanted them told personally."

"Do that. And make certain his family hears it from us before the casualty register is released."

Joel nodded.

Lars studied the casualty numbers again.

"His Warden?"

"Recovered. Returning separately."

"Good. Bring them both home."

***

The first public casualty notice was issued three hours later.

It did not announce a final death toll.

Instead, it listed every vessel involved in the ambush, the confirmed losses, and the locations where survivors were receiving treatment. Families were given access to dedicated identification officers rather than being forced to search through incomplete public manifests.

For many, the distinction offered little comfort.

A Talyrn woman stood before one of the registration desks holding a small child's hand. Her husband had been aboard Transport Nine. He had survived imprisonment at Ghar-Kul, helped evacuate wounded prisoners, and finally boarded a ship that was supposed to carry him home.

The registration officer checked his name twice.

"I'm sorry. We have no confirmed recovery under that identification."

The woman looked toward the medical arrival board.

"But the transport reached the gate?"

The officer swallowed.

"No. It was destroyed before reaching the gate."

The child tightened his grip around her hand.

"My father was on that ship."

The officer lowered the slate.

"We are still searching for survivors."

Joel heard the exchange as he passed. He stopped beside the desk, but he had no better information to offer.

The woman recognized the House insignia on his uniform.

"You rescued him," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you lost him."

Joel did not look away.

"His ship was attacked. We failed to protect it."

For several seconds, she studied him with an expression he could not interpret.

Then she took her child's hand and walked toward the waiting area.

Anton watched them disappear behind the medical partitions.

"She survived Ghar-Kul," he said quietly.

Joel looked at the registration display.

"So did her husband."

Neither brother found anything else to say.

***

The news of Bjorn's death reached the surviving Crimson Wardens aboard the medical reception station.

Mikael stood beside Rasmus when Joel entered the room. Both men were still wearing parts of their damaged combat equipment. Their armor had been cleaned only where medical personnel needed access to wounds and pressure fittings.

Mikael rose immediately.

"Where is he?"

Joel remained standing.

"Bjorn died during medical treatment. The surgeons couldn't stop the internal bleeding."

Rasmus looked toward the door, as though expecting the old man to appear behind him.

"He was speaking when we left."

"I know."

"He complained about the stretcher."

"I know."

Mikael lowered himself back into his chair.

"He carried those prisoners," he said. "Even after the Warden failed."

Joel nodded.

"Twenty-seven thousand had been found before we finished clearing the block. He helped bring them through."

Mikael stared at the floor.

"We told him to leave."

"He knew."

"He always knew."

Rasmus removed the damaged gauntlet from his right hand and placed it on the table. The fastening mechanism was broken, and the old crimson paint had been scorched almost black.

"His hammer," he said.

"Recovered," Joel answered. "With the Warden."

"Keep them together."

"We will."

Mikael finally looked up.

"And the bottle?"

Anton had entered quietly behind Joel.

Nobody answered immediately.

Then Anton said, "He intended to win it."

Mikael's expression changed. Not quite a smile.

"He would have argued about it for another fifty years."

Anton sat beside him.

"Probably longer."

The silence that followed was different from the one before it. There was no relief in it, but there was something familiar enough to endure.

***

That evening, Joel returned to the casualty coordination center.

Annette had established a direct connection from Ardent and was working with Talyrn officials to prepare recovery and identification arrangements. Medical records were being compared against transport manifests. Salvage teams were cataloguing hull fragments containing identifiable compartments. Every surviving escape pod received an individual case number.

The process would take days.

Possibly weeks.

Ortheva appeared on the secure display from the continuing joint-defense command.

"Your father has informed me about Elder Bjorn," she said. "Our people remember what he did inside Ghar-Kul."

Joel glanced toward the casualty lists.

"They also remember the transports."

"They do."

Ortheva's voice remained steady.

"The Garooth have lost their fortress. But we have lost people who should have been home by now."

Joel studied the display. Transport Six. Transport Nine. The destroyed House destroyer. Thousands of names waiting for confirmation.

"We will continue the recovery," he said.

"And afterward?"

"We identify the dead. We return whoever we can. We tell the families what happened."

Ortheva nodded slowly.

"That will not be easy."

"No."

Joel reached across the table and selected the first unresolved passenger record.

He had spent his life commanding warships, signing contracts, and deciding when force was necessary.

Now, for the next several hours, he helped reconcile names.

Outside the station windows, the surviving convoy lay beneath repair scaffolds. Medical shuttles moved steadily between damaged hulls, while transport crews carried the living toward their waiting families.

Beyond the gate, the search continued.

And on a medical ship preparing for the long journey toward Vintergard, Bjorn's body rested beneath the crimson banner of the House.

His Warden was coming home.

So was he.

But thousands of others had not yet been found.

And Joel refused to let their names disappear into a single number.

---

## 3. The Last Journey of Bjorn

The Crown of Winter had received returning warships for generations. Some came home with their armor shattered, their gun decks burned, and their crews reduced to a fraction of their original strength. Others returned victorious, accompanied by captured enemy vessels and the wounded they had rescued. Lars von Hjertstedt had stood in the great orbital fortress many times to welcome such formations home. He had watched damaged flagships enter the repair docks, greeted commanders who had survived impossible engagements, and received the bodies of friends whose names were now carved into the memorial halls of Vintergard.

But he had never expected to receive Bjorn this way.

Beyond the observation gallery, the medical transport *Mercy* approached its assigned berth. Two frigates escorted it through the inner defense perimeter, their formation deliberately reduced to the slow speed of the wounded vessel. Behind them came a heavy recovery ship carrying the remains of Bjorn's Crimson Warden. The ancient machine rested on an industrial cradle beneath protective restraints. Its left leg was destroyed, its right shoulder hung at an unnatural angle, and the crimson armor that had once made it recognizable from across a battlefield was almost entirely blackened.

Lars watched the recovery vessel adjust its approach.

"Docking control reports both vessels cleared for arrival," a naval officer informed him. "Medical reception is prepared. The ceremonial guard is assembled."

"How many?"

"Two hundred, High Commander. Additional formations have requested permission to attend."

Lars considered the request. Hundreds of ships remained committed beyond Gate Seventeen, and the Garooth had already demonstrated how quickly apparent victory could turn into catastrophe.

"Nobody abandons an operational assignment," he ordered. "Those who can attend without weakening the defense may do so. The others will receive the memorial transmission."

The officer acknowledged him.

Lars looked back toward the damaged Warden.

"Bring them home."

***

The medical transport settled into its berth without ceremony.

Joel and Anton stood beside the transfer platform when the inner airlock opened. A medical honor detail emerged first, followed by two surgeons wearing the dark uniforms of the House medical service. Behind them came the sealed coffin, carried on a low antigravity platform beneath a crimson covering.

The House crest rested above Bjorn's name.

No weapons were raised. No trumpets sounded.

Only the heavy machinery of the docking bay continued working somewhere beyond the assembled personnel.

Lars approached the coffin slowly.

The chief surgeon stepped forward.

"My lord, the complete medical report has been transferred to the House archives. We performed a further examination after his death. His injuries were consistent with the trauma sustained during the Ghar-Kul operation."

Lars looked at her.

"Was there anything else you could have done?"

The surgeon held his gaze.

"We examined every available treatment option. We repaired the ruptured vessels and maintained circulation as long as his body responded. The accumulated damage was too extensive. By the final arrest, several organs were failing despite mechanical support."

She paused.

"I wish I could give you a different answer."

Lars studied the exhaustion in her face. He had read the medical record twice during the journey, searching for something that might have been missed. There was nothing in it that changed the outcome.

"You remained with him until the end?"

"Yes."

"Then you did your duty. Thank you, Doctor."

The surgeon bowed her head.

Lars stepped beside the coffin and rested his hand upon the crimson covering.

For almost a century, Bjorn had been a familiar voice in his life. A man who challenged commanders, corrected young officers, and treated the most dangerous military operations as opportunities to prove that age had not diminished his competence.

Now there was no familiar insult waiting behind the silence.

"Welcome home, old friend," Lars said quietly.

***

The recovery ship docked twenty minutes later.

Daniel supervised the unloading of the Crimson Warden personally. He had already instructed the technicians not to remove any damaged armor before the machine reached the House engineering facilities. Every broken plate, severed actuator, and burned control connection had been preserved.

Mikael and Rasmus waited beside the cradle.

When the restraints opened, the enormous machine became visible beneath the hangar lights.

Rasmus approached first.

He placed one hand against the ruined chest armor.

"This is where we found him," he said.

Daniel nodded. "The cockpit survived. Most of the damage is structural. His emergency controls were still functioning when we removed him."

Mikael studied the shattered left leg.

"He should have withdrawn when the cooling system began failing."

"He knew that," Daniel answered.

"Then why didn't he?"

Daniel looked toward the Warden's broken hands.

"Because there were still people behind the door."

Mikael closed his eyes.

He had known Bjorn long enough to understand the answer before asking the question.

Anton approached carrying the old man's hammer. Its handle had been cleaned, but several deep marks remained along the striking head.

"The hammer stays with him," Anton said.

Rasmus turned.

"With the Warden?"

"Where else?"

Mikael reached out and accepted the weapon. For a moment, its weight seemed greater than he expected.

"Do you remember Skold?" he asked.

Anton frowned.

"Which time?"

"The training exercise when Bjorn refused to surrender the final corridor. Three hours after the simulation had officially ended, he was still arguing with the instructors."

Anton gave a small, unwilling smile.

"He claimed nobody had told him the corridor was secure."

"He knew perfectly well."

"Of course he did."

Mikael looked toward the Warden.

"He simply wanted them to prove it."

Neither man laughed.

But for a few seconds, the memory made the hangar feel less empty.

***

Later, the family gathered in a private chamber overlooking Vinterholm.

Below them, the planet's northern continents lay beneath pale winter clouds. City lights followed the coastlines, and the orbital traffic moving between the Crown and the surface continued according to its normal schedules.

Millions of people were living their lives beneath those clouds.

Most would never know what Bjorn had done at Ghar-Kul.

Annette joined by secure communication from Ardent. Christer remained beside her, having delayed his departure until the local command arrangements were complete. Daniel stood with Joel and Anton near the long table, while Mikael and Rasmus occupied the seats nearest Lars.

"We should hold the funeral at Vinterkrona," Annette said. "The old memorial grounds can accommodate the family and the Wardens. I can arrange to return, but I will need time."

"You'll have it," Joel answered. "Nobody is asking us to bury him before his family can gather."

Lars remained silent for a moment.

"The memorial must also acknowledge the people we lost after Ghar-Kul," he said.

Anton looked toward him.

"Bjorn deserves his own farewell."

"He will have it."

"Then why—"

"Because he would be furious if we filled the halls with praise for him while thousands of people remained unaccounted for."

Anton stopped.

Lars continued more quietly.

"We will honor Bjorn as we should. But we will not pretend his death was the only loss worth remembering."

Joel nodded.

"The casualty commission is still working. The final manifests will take time."

"Then we give them time," Lars answered. "No invented certainty. No names declared dead without proof."

Mikael rested his hands on the table.

"And the Warden?"

Daniel looked up.

"It can be preserved. Restored to operational condition would require replacing much of its structure. I would rather not make that decision now."

"Neither would I," Lars said. "Leave it as it returned until we have held the funeral."

***

The decision was transmitted throughout Vintergard that evening.

Bjorn would be laid to rest at the ancestral memorial grounds outside Vinterkrona. The Crimson Wardens would provide the honor guard, joined by representatives of the formations that had fought beside him at Ghar-Kul.

The damaged Warden would accompany the procession upon its recovery cradle.

Its hammer would remain with it.

But no victory parade would be held.

At the Crown of Winter, the memorial staff began preparing a second chamber for those lost aboard Transport Six, Transport Nine, and the destroyed House destroyer. Their names would be added only as the casualty commission established their fates.

The distinction mattered.

Thousands were still missing.

Some might be alive.

The House would not bury them in its records while recovery crews were still searching.

***

Late that night, Joel found Lars alone in the observation gallery.

The recovery ship had been moved into a maintenance berth. Through the distant windows, Joel could still see the damaged Warden resting beneath its temporary protective covering.

Lars stood with his hands behind his back.

"He taught me something when I was younger," he said without turning.

Joel approached.

"What?"

"That the hardest part of command was not giving an order that might get someone killed. It was looking at the consequences afterward and refusing to hide behind the necessity of the decision."

Joel stood beside him.

"He saved thousands."

"Yes."

"And he knew the risk."

"He did."

Lars turned toward his son.

"Neither fact makes losing him easier."

Joel had no answer.

For a long time, they watched the workers moving beneath the great shipyard lights.

Then Lars spoke again.

"When we go down to Vinterholm, I want the surviving Wardens beside him. Not because they were the oldest. Because they were there."

"They'll be there."

"And Anton?"

Joel glanced toward him.

"What about him?"

"Tell him the argument over that bottle is finished."

Joel almost smiled.

"He'll disagree."

"I know."

Lars looked once more toward the damaged machine.

"Then tell him he can argue with Bjorn about it when his own time comes. Until then, the bottle stays unopened."

Outside, a maintenance crew carefully lowered another protective covering over the Crimson Warden.

The machine that had carried thousands of prisoners to safety would never again hear its pilot's voice.

And for the first time in generations, the surviving Crimson Wardens would have to stand together without the man who had always insisted he could hold the corridor a little longer.

## 4. The Oldest Bottles

The bells of Vinterkrona began sounding at dawn.

Across the capital of Vinterholm, people stopped beside market stalls, industrial loading platforms, and the entrances of government buildings. Trains continued moving through the city, but their destination displays carried a narrow crimson memorial band. Above the mountain fortress, the banners of House von Hjertstedt hung at half-mast. The bells were not announcing a victory. They were marking the return of a man whose service had stretched across generations, and whose final battle had ended far beyond the safety of his ancestral home.

Elder Bjorn had come home.

The procession began at the eastern gates of the ancient fortress. Eight Crimson Wardens walked beside the ceremonial transport carrying his coffin. Behind them came the battered machine that had carried him through his final battle at Ghar-Kul. Its damaged armor had not been repaired. The left leg remained locked, the right shoulder was broken, and the great hammer rested across its chest.

Lars von Hjertstedt had insisted that nothing be polished or replaced.

"Let them see what it cost him," he had told the engineers.

Thousands lined the road toward the memorial grounds. Veterans stood beside civilians. Shipyard workers bowed their heads as the procession passed. Children watched from behind their parents, some too young to understand why the enormous warrior machine was being carried rather than walking.

At the entrance to the memorial grounds, Joel, Anton, Lars, and the surviving elders waited.

Then an officer approached.

"High Marshal. The Talyrn delegation has arrived."

Joel turned.

Five visitors had been admitted through Vintergard's restricted reception arrangements, their onward movement carefully escorted. Their vessel had never received the Winter Road's navigation credentials. Ortheva had remained with the joint defense beyond Gate Seventeen, but had sent representatives who had personally witnessed Bjorn's final actions.

Among them stood the Thyrak commander who had helped recover the old man from Ghar-Kul.

Beside him was a woman wearing simple civilian clothing.

She carried a folded piece of fabric.

Joel recognized it immediately.

"The names," he said.

The woman nodded.

"I was in Prison Block Nine. When the old warrior opened the door, I gave you the names of the workers taken below us."

"I remember."

"We found them. Your people found every one on that list who was still there."

She looked toward the Crimson Warden.

"We wanted to thank him."

Joel glanced at the coffin.

"Then you've come to the right place."

***

The funeral began beneath the great stone arches overlooking Vinterkrona.

Hundreds of Crimson Marines and House veterans stood in ordered formations. Among them were officers who had fought beside Bjorn decades earlier, men and women whose own children now served aboard the House's warships.

Lars stepped forward.

He looked at the coffin for a long moment before addressing the assembled mourners.

"I have known Bjorn for longer than most of you have been alive. I knew him when he was young enough to believe every disagreement could be settled with a hammer."

A few veterans smiled.

"He never entirely abandoned that belief."

Lars paused.

"We fought together. We lost friends together. We argued over tactics, over command decisions, and over bottles that neither of us intended to surrender. He could be stubborn beyond reason. He could insult a superior officer with such precision that the officer sometimes thanked him before understanding what had happened."

Quiet laughter passed through the older ranks.

"But when a corridor had to be held, when civilians needed protection, or when a wounded comrade could not walk, there was no man I trusted more."

His voice lowered.

"At Ghar-Kul, he was ordered to withdraw. He knew his machine was failing. He knew his body could not endure much more. Yet he remained because there were still people behind him."

Lars placed one hand upon the coffin.

"He did not die because he sought glory. He died because he could not accept leaving someone else to face the darkness alone."

The Thyrak commander stepped forward when Lars finished. With permission, he placed his unit's small service insignia beside Bjorn's name.

"In the Continuance," he said, "we believe strength creates obligation. At Ghar-Kul, Elder Bjorn showed us that this belief does not belong to our people alone."

The woman approached next.

She unfolded the cloth.

Names covered its surface, written in different hands.

"These were people he helped save," she said. "Some could not come. Some are still in hospital. But they asked me to bring this."

She laid the cloth beside the insignia.

Mikael bowed his head.

Rasmus turned away, his jaw working as he struggled to control himself.

Anton stared at the names for several seconds. Then he approached the coffin, rested one enormous hand against it, and spoke.

"You miserable old bastard. You still owe me an argument."

His voice broke.

He stepped back before anyone could answer.

***

That evening, after the public ceremonies had ended and the coffin had been laid to rest, Joel invited the family, surviving Wardens, and Talyrn guests into a private hall overlooking the memorial gardens.

A long table stood prepared with food, water, and small drinking cups.

At its center were three extraordinary bottles.

Their bodies were dark, almost black beneath the protective cases that had held them for generations. The metal seals bore markings so ancient that few living historians could identify them without consulting the House archives.

Daniel stopped when he saw them.

"Joel."

His voice was unusually serious.

"Where did you get those?"

"From my private cellar."

Daniel approached the table.

"No. I recognize those cases. Those are from the oldest collection."

"They are."

Anton leaned forward.

"How old?"

Joel carefully lifted the first bottle.

"Older than the collapse of the Varanthi Dominion."

Nobody spoke.

The old Dominion had fallen three thousand years earlier. These bottles came from surviving pre-collapse stores, recovered and preserved through generations of careful custody. Their contents had been studied, their seals inspected, and their provenance recorded, but their original makers belonged to an age that had disappeared before Vintergard became the civilization it was today.

Mikael stared at the bottle.

"Those belong in an archive."

"They've spent enough time in archives," Joel answered.

Lars examined the ancient seal.

"Do you understand what you're opening?"

"Yes, Father."

"Those may be among the oldest surviving spirits in human possession."

Joel looked toward the empty chair reserved for Bjorn.

"Then I cannot think of a better occasion."

He broke the first seal.

A faint, rich aroma rose from the neck of the bottle. Joel poured carefully measured portions into the small ceremonial cups. Most received only a little. Those who preferred something milder were offered it without question, and the Talyrn visitors accepted nonalcoholic drinks. The other two ancient bottles remained sealed beside the first.

Anton looked at his cup.

"How much is this worth?"

"More than your last warship," Daniel answered.

Anton raised an eyebrow.

"Which warship?"

"The expensive one."

"That narrows nothing."

Even Lars smiled.

Joel raised his cup.

"To Bjorn."

The others followed.

"To Bjorn."

They drank.

For several minutes, nobody spoke. The unfamiliar taste was less important than the knowledge that something preserved through millennia had been opened for a man who would never sit at their table again.

Then Mikael began recounting a disastrous training exercise on Skold, when Bjorn had accidentally demolished the wrong fortified door and spent three days arguing that the architects had placed it incorrectly.

Rasmus remembered Bjorn attempting to repair a damaged Warden using equipment borrowed without permission from an admiral's personal transport.

Daniel remembered discovering that the old man's repair instructions consisted largely of insults directed at future engineers.

Anton laughed until he had to put down his cup.

And gradually, the room filled with stories.

Not legends of an invincible warrior.

Stories of a stubborn, difficult, generous old man who had spent his life refusing to leave people behind.

***

Much later, Lars found Joel standing beside the table.

The first bottle was nearly empty. The other two remained untouched.

"Why three?" Lars asked.

Joel looked toward the memorial gardens.

"One for those who knew him before us. One for those who fought beside him. And one for the people who will never have the chance."

Lars regarded the bottles.

"And the last?"

"It stays sealed until the missing from the convoy have been accounted for as far as we can manage. Then we decide when to open it."

Lars nodded.

Outside, the last visitors were leaving the memorial grounds. The lights around Bjorn's grave remained lit, and beyond them the great city continued its ordinary life.

For a moment, Joel imagined the old man sitting at the table, complaining that the ancient drink was inferior to his own reserve.

He almost heard the insult.

Almost.

Lars rested a hand on his son's shoulder.

"He would have hated this funeral."

"Every minute."

"Too many speeches."

"Not enough food."

"And someone else carrying his hammer."

Joel smiled faintly.

"He would have demanded a rematch."

Lars looked toward the empty chair.

"He always did."

For the first time since the news of Bjorn's death, Joel allowed himself to sit.

The family remained at the table long into the night.

And the oldest bottles in Vintergard stood among them, witnesses from a civilization long dead, opened to remember a man who had spent his final strength keeping others alive.
