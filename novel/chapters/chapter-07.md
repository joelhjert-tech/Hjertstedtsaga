# Chapter 7: The Living First

## 1. The First Envoys

The Wayward Hound returned to Kestrel looking worse than when it had left.

That impressed people.

The battered transport limped into Kestrel Shield with mismatched armor, fresh burn scars, temporary Talyrn repairs, and one engine running slightly hotter than the other.

Mara Vale stared at it from command.

“What did they do?”

Sera stood beside her.

“Sightseeing.”

Mara looked at her.

“That word has lost all meaning.”

Then the passenger list arrived.

Joel.

Anton.

Christer.

Daniel.

Alive.

Followed by a sealed priority package for House command.

Sera immediately stopped smiling.

Lars reached Kestrel less than a day later.

He did not come alone.

Three elders traveled with him.

Elder Mikael Winge came first—old, broad-shouldered, carrying the quiet authority of a man whose family had served beside the Hjertstedts for generations.

Elder Ingrid Falken followed, silver-haired and severe, one of the House’s oldest legal and diplomatic voices.

The third was Elder Rasmus Skar, a former Banner commander whose damaged left eye had been replaced decades earlier and who still walked as though every corridor belonged to a warship.

Annette arrived separately from Ardent.

By the time Lars entered Kestrel’s secure strategic chamber, Joel had already prepared the first briefing.

The doors sealed.

No recording left the room.

Joel looked around the table.

Lars.

Annette.

Sera.

Mara.

The three elders.

Anton, Christer, Daniel.

Then he opened the Talyrn file.

THE TALYRN CONTINUANCE

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

Lars finally looked at Joel.

“You went sightseeing.”

Anton smiled.

“Yes.”

Lars looked at the image of two fused Garooth capital ships.

Then at Joel.

“Your definition is becoming hereditary.”

The briefing lasted four hours.

The Odrava.

The Talyrn.

Their distributed command systems.

Their Thyrak.

Their recovery culture.

The Garooth invasion.

Gate Seventeen.

The new authentication system.

The fact that the Talyrn knew the Seraph.

The fact that they had deliberately severed one of their own systems rather than risk wider contamination.

That drew the elders’ full attention.

Then Joel played the message from Veyr-Khal Ortheva Sair.

Her image appeared above the table.

Formal.

Controlled.

“To House von Hjertstedt: Gate Seventeen may open again under joint authorization. We are prepared to receive a limited diplomatic mission. Military transit beyond agreed escort strength will not be accepted.”

The message ended.

Lars leaned back.

“A limited mission.”

Annette nodded.

“Good.”

Anton looked disappointed.

“How limited?”

Ingrid Falken answered without looking at him.

“More limited if you keep asking.”

Lars brought up Gate Seventeen.

“No major fleet.”

Joel nodded.

“That would send the wrong message.”

“No Seraph Hunters.”

Annette agreed.

“No Black Guard formation beyond personal security.”

Mikael Winge looked toward Joel.

“You are going.”

“Yes.”

“Anton?”

“Yes.”

Ingrid sighed.

“Why?”

Anton answered:

“Cultural exchange.”

Nobody believed him.

Christer and Daniel were also included because both had worked directly with Talyrn officers and Gate Seventeen’s new access architecture.

Then Lars made the decision that changed the scale of the meeting.

“I am going too.”

Joel looked at him.

“You do not need to.”

“I know.”

“That makes it worse.”

Lars ignored him.

“This is first contact with an isolated human civilization that possesses functioning war fleets, unknown infrastructure, Seraph experience, and authenticated access to routes the Known Reach believed dead.”

He looked toward the elders.

“That is not a junior diplomatic assignment.”

Mikael Winge nodded.

“Agreed.”

Ingrid Falken:

“I am joining.”

Rasmus Skar gave a dry smile.

“I assumed that was why I was dragged here.”

Lars nodded.

“Then three elders.”

Joel looked toward Annette.

She shook her head.

“I remain in Ardent.”

That surprised him.

Annette continued.

“Someone must remain outside the mission with full authority if Gate Seventeen closes.”

Kira whispered:

“Statistically sensible.”

Lucy added:

“Also prevents the entire senior family from becoming trapped beyond an unstable gate.”

Anton muttered:

“Pessimistic machines.”

Lucy answered:

“Correct machines.”

The mission structure appeared.

One House diplomatic cruiser.

Two Vigil frigates.

One Sanctuary tender.

No capital ships.

No heavy assault formation.

No grand fleet demonstration.

The ships would approach Gate Seventeen openly, stop outside activation distance, transmit the joint authorization, and wait.

Only after Talyrn confirmation would the gate wake.

Sera studied the plan.

“This is the first time outsiders are deliberately going through?”

Joel nodded.

“From our side, yes.”

“And if someone follows?”

Daniel answered:

“They see a dead gate.”

“Even after you transit?”

“Yes.”

Christer added:

“The activation closes behind authorized traffic. No persistent beacon. No useful response to standard scans.”

Lars approved.

“Good.”

Then he looked at Joel.

“The Talyrn trust you enough to open the road.”

Joel shook his head.

“They trust the system enough to control it.”

Lars smiled faintly.

“Better.”

Three days later, the mission departed Kestrel.

No public announcement.

No diplomatic broadcast.

Officially, Lars was traveling to inspect frontier defenses.

Foreign intelligence noticed anyway.

They always did.

But nobody knew where the small House formation went after it left normal traffic.

Gate Seventeen appeared ahead.

Dark.

Ancient.

Dead.

The House formation stopped well outside the throat.

Lars stood on the bridge beside Joel.

Mikael Winge, Ingrid Falken, and Rasmus Skar watched from the command tier.

Anton stood near tactical.

Christer and Daniel monitored the authentication sequence.

Daniel transmitted the first challenge.

Nothing happened.

Ingrid looked toward him.

“Problem?”

“No.”

A second passed.

Then another.

One tiny encrypted symbol appeared.

RECOGNIZED

Christer smiled.

“Talyrn side has accepted us.”

The gate remained dark.

Then Daniel transmitted the final mission authorization.

For several more seconds, nothing changed.

Then ancient lights moved around the ring.

Slowly.

Deliberately.

The dead gate woke.

Lars watched the transit plane form.

“So this is the door.”

Joel nodded.

“To another human civilization.”

Anton smiled.

“And hopefully another war.”

Ingrid turned toward him.

“No.”

Anton looked at Lars.

Lars gave him the same answer.

“No.”

The fleet entered.

On the far side, the Talyrn were waiting.

Not hundreds of warships.

A formal escort.

Precise formations.

Broad, armored vessels holding disciplined separation.

No weapons targeted.

No ambiguity either.

A transmission arrived.

Veyr-Khal Ortheva Sair appeared.

Her artificial eye fixed immediately on Lars.

“Primarch Lars von Hjertstedt.”

Lars inclined his head.

“Veyr-Khal.”

Ortheva looked past him toward the elders.

Then Joel and his brothers.

Her expression shifted slightly when she saw Anton.

“You brought him back.”

Lars glanced toward his son.

“I was advised against it.”

Anton looked betrayed.

Ortheva almost smiled.

Then her voice became formal.

“House von Hjertstedt, welcome to the Odrava.”

Lars looked through the forward display at the unknown human ships waiting beyond the gate.

For the first time in generations, two human civilizations that had believed the road between them dead were meeting deliberately.

Not through war.

Not by accident.

At a table.

“Lead the way,” Lars said.

## 2. We Do Not Tolerate the Garooth

The Talyrn war map opened in layers.

At first, Lars watched in silence.

Seven inhabited systems.

Industrial moons.

Transit complexes.

Population centers.

Fleet yards.

Then the red markers appeared.

Hundreds.

Then thousands.

The battle Joel and his brothers had stumbled into appeared near Gate Seventeen.

A massive engagement.

Capital ships.

Fortified approaches.

Hundreds of Talyrn hulls.

A Garooth convergence large enough to have terrified most frontier states.

Then Veyr-Khal Ortheva Sair widened the map.

Their battle became almost insignificant.

Anton stopped smiling.

Four major fronts burned across the Odrava.

Garooth warbands were not raiding anymore.

They were moving inward.

System by system.

One captured industrial moon had become a scrap forge.

A civilian orbital yard now produced Garooth boarding craft.

An entire mining network had been stripped and converted into mobile supply infrastructure.

Behind the current fronts, still more clan formations were converging.

Christer stared at the movement tracks.

“This is migration.”

Ortheva nodded.

“Yes.”

Daniel studied the production estimates.

“They are eating your industrial base.”

“Yes.”

“And converting every loss into additional fleet mass.”

“Yes.”

Then Ortheva revealed the projection beyond the known warbands.

A moving concentration.

Huge.

Its true size uncertain.

Surrounded by forge hulks, captured yards, resource convoys, capital ships and entire lesser warbands following in its wake.

Rasmus Skar leaned forward.

“What is that?”

“We believe it is becoming their primary convergence.”

“How many ships?”

“We do not know.”

“How many Garooth?”

“We do not know.”

Then Ortheva gave them the final number.

Not ships.

Time.

“Without a major strategic change, our central industrial network begins failing within fourteen to twenty-two months.”

The chamber went silent.

Lars moved.

Immediately.

No council.

No deliberation.

No whispered conversation with the elders.

He turned toward Joel.

“Secure channel to Vintergard.”

Joel knew that voice.

So did Anton.

Anton slowly smiled again.

Ortheva looked at Lars.

“Primarch, we did not show you this to demand military—”

Lars raised one hand.

“Veyr-Khal.”

She stopped.

Lars looked at the red map.

At inhabited systems being eaten.

At people fighting a war the wider human sphere had not even known existed.

Then at the Garooth convergence.

“We do not tolerate the Garooth.”

The secure House system came alive.

It took several authentication layers before Vintergard answered.

The Crown of Winter appeared on the tactical display.

Annette stood in the command chamber.

Behind her were House officers, Justicars, fleet coordinators and logistics chiefs.

“Lars?”

“Full strategic alert.”

Annette's expression changed instantly.

“Against what?”

Lars transferred the Talyrn map.

For several seconds she said nothing.

Then Kira began processing.

Lucy followed.

Numbers filled the screen.

Known Garooth concentrations.

Industrial losses.

Probable fleet reproduction rates.

Talyrn defensive lines.

Gate Seventeen.

The moving convergence.

Annette looked back at Lars.

“How much?”

Lars answered:

“Everything we can move without weakening Vintergard below home-defense threshold.”

The room around Ortheva went completely still.

Joel looked sharply toward his father.

Even he had not expected that.

Annette asked:

“Clarify everything.”

Lars pointed toward Gate Seventeen.

“Anchor the gate.”

“Permanent House gate command?”

“Joint with the Talyrn.”

“Defenses?”

“Massive.”

“Fleet staging?”

“Yes.”

“Sanctuary?”

“Yes.”

“Engineering?”

“Yes.”

“Marine?”

“Yes.”

“Reserve Banners?”

“Yes.”

Annette paused.

“Capital assets?”

Lars looked at the Garooth convergence.

“All deployable assets appropriate to the theater.”

Anton whispered:

“Oh, this is going to be magnificent.”

Ingrid Falken looked toward Lars.

“The major powers will see the movements.”

“I know.”

“They will demand explanations.”

“I know.”

“The Hiigarans will assume we found another gate network.”

“Yes.”

“Terra will think we are beginning an expansion.”

“Yes.”

“The Imperium may mobilize.”

Lars finally looked toward her.

“Then they may scream from Sol while we kill Garooth.”

Ortheva stared at him.

“You are committing military forces before a treaty exists.”

Lars turned toward her.

“Yes.”

“You have not seen our full logistics picture.”

“Then show it to my logisticians when they arrive.”

“You do not know whether the Ordan will authorize foreign strategic forces.”

“Then place them at Gate Seventeen until it does.”

She frowned.

“You would move that much military power through a gate without certainty you can deploy it?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Lars pointed toward the destroyed Talyrn systems.

“Because those are human worlds.”

Then toward the Garooth lines.

“And those are Garooth.”

For Lars, that apparently completed the legal argument.

Vintergard began moving before the diplomatic chamber had finished processing what he had ordered.

Annette's screen populated with mobilization notices.

Reserve Banner readiness.

Capital-ship crews recalled.

Vigil formations rerouted.

Oath cruisers reassigned.

Sanctuary tenders activated.

Fuel reserves opened.

Marine formations placed on deployment status.

Engineering fleets recalled from non-critical contracts.

Gatewright teams assembled.

Mobile defense platforms prepared for transport.

Prefabricated fortress modules released from storage.

Joel stared at the accumulating force-state.

“This is going to expose a lot.”

Lars nodded.

“Yes.”

“Fleet depth.”

“Yes.”

“Mobilization speed.”

“Yes.”

“Industrial capacity.”

“Yes.”

Anton leaned toward Joel.

“You say that like it is bad.”

Joel ignored him.

Lars looked toward his sons.

“We spent generations making sure Vintergard could survive without the galaxy.”

Then he looked toward the Talyrn war map.

“That does not mean we watch another human civilization die because secrecy is convenient.”

Mikael Winge nodded slowly.

That was the end of the argument.

Daniel enlarged Gate Seventeen.

“If we anchor it properly, we can create a secure logistics throat.”

Christer followed.

“Talyrn-side staging outside the civilian traffic lanes.”

Joel added:

“Authentication remains joint.”

Lars nodded.

“No foreign access.”

“Known Reach powers?”

“Dead gate.”

“House ships?”

“Authorized.”

“Talyrn?”

“Authorized.”

“Anyone else?”

Lars looked toward Ortheva.

She understood.

“Nothing.”

Daniel smiled faintly.

“Then we build the door into a fortress.”

A new projection appeared.

The first House deployment estimate.

Even Rasmus Skar stopped speaking.

Not one fleet.

Not three.

A sustained expeditionary force.

Combat Banners.

Reserve formations.

Tenders.

Repair groups.

Fuel ships.

Marine carriers.

Gatewright vessels.

Engineering convoys.

Mobile fortifications.

The kind of deployment that did not merely reinforce a battle.

It created a front.

Ortheva looked at Lars.

“Your other human powers will believe you are preparing for war.”

Lars answered:

“They will be correct.”

“With whom?”

Lars pointed toward the Garooth convergence.

“With them.”

His eyes hardened.

“Anchor Gate Seventeen.”

He looked at Annette through the secure channel.

“Send everything.”

Then at Ortheva.

“When your government is ready, we discuss command boundaries.”

A pause.

“But the Garooth does not get fourteen months.”

Anton smiled.

Joel did too.

Because somewhere beyond the dead gate, Vintergard had just begun opening its arsenals.

And the Known Reach was about to discover that House von Hjertstedt had considered the Garooth a nuisance only because none had yet given the House a reason to mobilize properly.

## 3. The Gate Opens and Sol Screams

Gate Seventeen changed in less than six days.

What had once been a dead ruin now became a fortress throat.

Talyrn engineers raised control spines around the gate perimeter. House Gatewrights arrived with prefabricated command modules, power relays, armored shield nodes, and authentication towers. Mobile defense platforms were towed into anchored positions above and below the transit plane. Missile bastions unfolded from transport cradles. Sensor webs spread outward in layered shells. Sanctuary tenders took station behind the main defense line. Fuel ships, repair docks, marine carriers, and logistics barges formed a growing wall around the route.

Ortheva watched the construction from the command gallery.

“They really build like this all the time?”

Joel stood beside her.

“No.”

She looked at him.

“This is restrained?”

Joel said nothing.

That was answer enough.

Then the first heavy formation arrived from Vintergard.

Gate Seventeen woke.

A House battleship came through first, black and iron beneath crimson-lit armor seams. Then two Oath cruisers. Then escorts. Then tenders. Then more carriers and engineering ships. The formation spread with practiced discipline and immediately began integrating into the defensive shell.

Talyrn officers murmured quietly.

The scale alone was impressive.

Then the second wave came.

This time the gate stayed bright longer.

Something vast emerged.

At first it looked like a station moving under its own power.

Then the Talyrn plot resolved the shape.

A dreadnought.

Ancient lines.

Immense armor.

Layered spinal weapons.

Broadside fortresses built into a mobile hull.

Black and crimson House heraldry burned across its flanks.

Another followed.

Then another.

Eight in total.

Eight monster ships.

Eight dreadnoughts so large that some Talyrn officers instinctively checked whether the gate could actually support the passage of such mass. These were not ordinary capital ships. They were relic-beasts from a scale of war most modern human powers only remembered in broken archives, the kind of hulls not commonly seen since the Fall shattered older eras of grand fleet warfare. They carried the visual weight of history and the practical weight of annihilation.

Anton looked genuinely pleased.

“There they are.”

Ortheva stared at the eighth dreadnought clearing the gate.

“You had eight of those hidden.”

Lars answered from behind her.

“Yes.”

He had remained at Gate Seventeen’s primary command station since giving the order. There was no triumph in his voice. Only intent.

The dreadnoughts formed the core of the new line. Around them came more ships: Vigil frigates, destroyers, marine transports, repair vessels, heavy ammunition carriers, and what looked like half a fortress district under tow. Mobile fortifications followed in segmented masses—defense citadels, mass-driver platforms, fighter bastions, armored logistics hubs, and expandable shield nodes that could be locked together into temporary war stations.

Daniel watched the deployment plot update and smiled despite himself.

“We are building a front.”

Christer corrected him.

“We are building a wall.”

“No,” Lars said.

They all looked at him.

His gaze remained on the red zones deeper inside the Odrava.

“We are building a fist.”

Sol detected the mobilization before the fourth dreadnought finished transit.

The first message reached Annette at Ardent Central.

Then the second.

Then twelve more.

Terran Protectorate observers reported unprecedented House fleet movement.

Zenith frontier arrays tracked mass transfers disappearing into a supposedly dead gate corridor.

Hiigaran intelligence requested emergency clarification.

Imperium channels demanded to know why House reserve deployments now exceeded previous public strategic estimates by a margin nobody wished to state aloud.

Annette stood in Central’s strategic chamber with Kira and Lucy processing the flood.

Kira spoke first.

“Sol emergency assembly requested.”

Lucy added:

“Correction: demanded.”

Annette touched the pink rose in her hair.

“Who is screaming loudest?”

Lucy answered immediately.

“Terrans first. Hiigarans second. Zenith third. Imperium least loudly, but with the most military focus.”

That sounded correct.

The main chamber opened as House officers entered with updated frontier plots. More fleet movements had been detected leaving Vintergard-adjacent shadow routes. Ardent’s three major stations were also reinforcing, not by stripping themselves bare, but by rotating fresh defensive groups into place. Anyone watching would see the truth.

House von Hjertstedt was not improvising.

It had depth.

And now the galaxy could see it.

Annette opened the first Sol transmission.

The Terran chairwoman appeared immediately.

“Head Justicar, explain these deployments.”

Annette answered calmly.

“No.”

The woman stared.

“No?”

“You asked for an explanation as though that grants authority over House fleet movement.”

“You are mobilizing dreadnoughts.”

“Yes.”

“Eight.”

“Yes.”

“That class has not been seen openly in centuries.”

“That sounds like a failure in your records.”

The chairwoman’s expression hardened.

“You are moving mobile fortifications and expeditionary logistics through an unregistered corridor.”

“Yes.”

“Toward what?”

Annette looked at her for a long moment.

“A war.”

The Hiigaran admiral entered the call as priority override.

“With whom?”

“The Garooth.”

The chamber quieted.

Not because the answer was reassuring.

Because it was worse.

If House von Hjertstedt was mobilizing eight ancient dreadnoughts and an anchored fortified front against the Garooth, then either the House had lost all restraint—

or it had found something terrible enough to justify it.

The Zenith representative appeared next.

“Where?”

Annette smiled faintly.

“That is the correct question.”

“And the answer?”

“No.”

The Terran chairwoman looked furious.

“You expect us to accept this blind?”

Annette answered with perfect calm.

“I expect you to do what House von Hjertstedt is currently doing.”

“Which is?”

“Killing Garooth.”

At Gate Seventeen, the last of the eight dreadnoughts took position.

The anchored fortress line was now fully visible from the Talyrn command galleries. House and Talyrn defenses interlocked around the gate like the armored throat of a new shared frontier.

Ortheva stood beside Lars.

“You were serious.”

Lars looked at the deployment.

“I usually am.”

The Veyr-Khal watched the monster ships settle into formation.

“For generations, we believed Gate Seventeen opened only toward ghosts.”

Lars nodded once.

“Now it opens toward allies.”

Behind them, another wave of House ships emerged.

And across known space, Sol kept screaming.

## 4. The First Hammer

The first joint offensive began before Sol’s delegates reached Ardent.

Lars wanted it that way.

Not because he cared about political theater.

Because the Garooth had already started stripping the industrial system of Kharos Reach.

Waiting for diplomacy would cost people.

The tactical map showed three Talyrn habitats under direct pressure, two orbital foundries burning, and a mining moon partly occupied by Garooth forge crews.

Ortheva stood beside Lars at Gate Seventeen command.

“We can still evacuate the inner habitats.”

Lars looked at the population numbers.

“And abandon the yards?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

Ortheva glanced toward him.

“You intend to hold the system.”

“I intend to recover it.”

That distinction she understood.

The attack force was enormous.

But it was not purely Hjertstedt.

Talyrn formations led the route.

Their Seryth corvettes and Varyk frigates built the sensor picture first, cross-checking every contact across multiple nodes.

Behind them came Kelvar destroyers and Othrenn cruisers.

Then the House.

Vigil frigates.

Destroyers.

Oath cruisers.

Marine carriers.

Sanctuary tenders protected deep inside the formation.

And behind all of them—

two of the eight dreadnoughts.

The others remained anchored around Gate Seventeen or held in reserve.

Even two were enough.

The first Talyrn crews to see them translating into formation stopped referring to them as ships.

They called them moving fortresses.

Lars approved neither description nor correction.

Whisper Range lasted nine minutes.

Christer sat inside the joint tactical command, watching Talyrn sensor nodes and House witness systems argue with one another.

“Multiple false blooms.”

A Talyrn operator answered:

“Corroborated. Decoy hulks.”

“Real formation behind them.”

Ortheva pointed.

“Mark.”

The Garooth had filled the approach with wreckage, heat sources, junk transmitters, and captured civilian transponders.

Talyrn corroboration stripped away the lies.

House witness drones confirmed them.

By the time the Garooth realized its deception had failed, Hammer Range had already begun.

Lars gave one order.

“Break the forge line.”

The dreadnoughts fired.

Space turned white.

Their main batteries did not target the largest enemy ships.

They targeted the infrastructure holding the Garooth offensive together.

Mobile scrap forges.

Engine barges.

Ammunition hulks.

Boarding-carrier spines.

The first salvo shattered the enemy’s ability to sustain the fight.

Then the Talyrn moved.

Not in one heroic charge.

In layers.

One formation crippled drives.

Another forced vectors.

A third isolated targets.

House destroyers punched through the openings.

Oath cruisers followed.

The Garooth tried to collapse the battle into Knife Range.

Anton was already waiting.

“Now,” he said.

House marine carriers opened.

Boarding craft flooded forward.

Thyrak assault teams launched beside them.

For the first time, Talyrn Keth Var and Hjertstedt White Warrant doctrine hit the same battlefield together.

Fix.

Blind.

Root.

Seal.

Declare.

Breach.

Take the heart.

Different traditions.

The same enemy.

At Ardent Central, diplomacy was considerably less elegant.

The Terran delegation arrived first.

Hiigaran ships followed.

Then Zenith.

Then the Imperium.

Not warfleets.

But each delegation brought enough armed escort to demonstrate displeasure.

Annette watched them approach Central through the observation gallery.

Behind them, House banners covered the station.

Farther out, the new defensive platforms tracked every arriving ship.

Lucy whispered:

“Terran delegation requests priority audience.”

Kira added:

“Hiigaran delegation has submitted a formal gate-right inquiry.”

Annette sighed.

“Of course they have.”

The diplomatic chamber filled quickly.

Terran representatives.

Hiigaran admirals.

Zenith strategic officers.

Imperial observers.

All of them wanted the same answer.

Where had the House sent the fleet?

Why were eight dreadnoughts moving?

Why had mobile fortifications vanished into a route nobody else could identify?

Why had unprecedented logistics tonnage been mobilized?

The Terran chairwoman began.

“We detected two of your dreadnoughts entering combat deployment.”

Annette nodded.

“They are.”

“Against whom?”

“Garooth.”

“Where?”

“No.”

The Hiigaran admiral leaned forward.

“That is becoming unacceptable.”

Annette looked at him.

“To whom?”

“To every major power whose security could be affected by an unregistered gate network.”

Annette smiled faintly.

“There it is.”

“What?”

“Gate network.”

She leaned forward.

“You are not concerned that humans are being attacked.”

“You are concerned we found a road before you did.”

The admiral’s expression hardened.

“That is an unfair characterization.”

“Then prove me wrong.”

Silence.

A tactical alert interrupted the chamber.

Annette looked toward Kira.

“Battle update?”

“Confirmed.”

A projection opened.

Sanitized.

No gate coordinates.

No route data.

Only the battlefield.

The delegates saw a Talyrn fleet for the first time.

Unknown human warships.

Hundreds.

Disciplined.

Broad armored hulls.

Distributed formation control.

Then House ships among them.

And two Hjertstedt dreadnoughts driving directly through the Garooth center.

The entire chamber went silent.

The Terran chairwoman whispered:

“Who are they?”

Annette answered:

“Human.”

The Hiigaran admiral stood.

“From where?”

“Somewhere you cannot reach.”

Then the battle feed updated.

KHAROS REACH — FORGE LINE BROKEN

CIVILIAN HABITATS — SECURED

TALYRN COUNTEROFFENSIVE ADVANCING

HOUSE BOARDING GROUPS — KNIFE RANGE

The Imperial representative stared at the display.

“You found an entire human civilization.”

Annette said nothing.

That was confirmation enough.

Back at Kharos Reach, the Garooth center finally broke.

Talyrn destroyers cut down fleeing assault craft.

House boarding teams seized three mobile forges intact.

Thyrak teams recovered hundreds of trapped civilians from a partially captured habitat.

Sanctuary tenders moved in before the shooting had fully stopped.

The mining moon remained contested.

But the system was no longer falling.

Ortheva looked at Lars.

“First front stabilized.”

Lars shook his head.

“No.”

She frowned.

He pointed toward the retreating Garooth formations.

“First front opened.”

Then toward the deeper red concentrations.

“We keep going.”

At Ardent, the delegates watched the same advance.

The Zenith representative finally asked:

“Is House von Hjertstedt entering another war?”

Annette looked at the tactical display.

“Yes.”

“On behalf of this unknown civilization?”

Annette corrected him.

“Beside them.”

The distinction mattered.

The Terran chairwoman stared at the Talyrn formations.

“How large is this war?”

Annette looked at the red markers still beyond Kharos Reach.

“Large enough that Lars stopped caring how loudly Sol screams.”

Nobody laughed.

Because eight dreadnoughts had already answered the question.

## 5. Four Ways to Kill a War

The mining moon of Kharos Reach was not a moon anymore.

The Garooth had turned it into a machine.

Mining shafts became troop tunnels.

Ore processors became ammunition works.

Cargo elevators carried armored mobs instead of iron.

Captured civilian habs had been welded into defensive blocks.

Deep beneath the surface, surviving Talyrn workers were still trapped behind sealed industrial sections.

That made orbital bombardment unacceptable.

So Joel chose the slower option.

Boarding.

The Talyrn assigned two Thyrak intervention companies to the assault.

Their commander studied the operational plan.

“Your family will remain with command?”

Joel looked at him.

“No.”

The Thyrak commander paused.

“Of course not.”

Four assault craft descended separately.

Four objectives.

Long, narrow, over-armored, with a brutal impact wedge at the bow and spinal boarding torpedo tubes running half its length, it was built for one purpose only: reach something that should not be boarded, survive long enough to cut itself into the hull, and deliver violence inside.

Joel stood on the assault bridge in his personal anti-Seraph warplate. Anton stood beside him in his heavier boarding armor, broad as a machine bulkhead. Christer and Daniel manned the tactical and breach-control feeds behind them. Across the command rail stood Dennis Rask in Proctor armor with Duskbringer waiting below.

Ahead, Ghar-Kul filled the main display.

It was less a ship than a moving industrial blasphemy. Captured stations had been chained into its frame. Hollowed asteroids served as ammunition vaults. Forge hulks hung beneath armored arms while hundreds of smaller vessels crawled over its surface like parasites feeding a larger beast.

Anton stared at it and smiled.

“That is worth at least two bottles.”

Dennis answered:

“If we live, I want Bjorn’s.”

At that exact moment the boarding officer spoke.

“We have unauthorized personnel aboard.”

Joel closed his eyes.

“Who?”

The answer came with visible reluctance.

“Three elders.”

Anton began laughing before the internal camera feed appeared.

Elder Mikael Winge.

Elder Rasmus Skar.

Elder Bjorn.

But they were not in ordinary combat armor.

They stood inside Crimson Warden assault-frames.

For the first time, several Talyrn observers on the shared command feed stopped talking altogether.

House von Hjertstedt did not build war machines to look heroic. It built them for the moment when ordinary armor stopped being enough, and the Crimson Warden was one of the oldest expressions of that philosophy. Each frame was something between power armor and a walking siege engine, layered in Jarn-steel, heavy-limbed, horn-crested, marked in crimson. The horns had begun as practical reinforcement for sensor and communications housings during boarding and urban fighting. Over generations, practicality became identity. The horns remained. So did the crimson.

Among House troops, crimson did not mean rage.

It meant commitment.

A Warden was deployed when something had to be held, breached, or recovered regardless of what waited on the other side.

Mikael’s Warden carried a siege shield and heavy breach axe.

Rasmus wore one fitted for command-breaching, with layered comms, a mounted heavy rifle, and reinforced forearms for structural work.

Bjorn’s frame was the oldest-looking of the three, broad as a cargo loader, carrying an enormous hammer in one hand and a casualty-lift claw mounted to the other arm.

Joel opened the command channel.

“No.”

Mikael looked up through the Warden’s horned helm.

“We are already aboard.”

“You were not authorized.”

Rasmus answered in a dry voice through external speakers.

“That sounds administrative.”

Bjorn lifted the Warden’s claw slightly.

“I heard people were betting my liquor.”

Dennis pointed at the screen.

“I am still claiming that bottle.”

Bjorn answered immediately.

“Win something first.”

Dennis tilted his helmet.

“I won yours at Gatehold.”

“Joel's bottle. He paid. You want one of mine, try beating me to something.”

Mikael's laugh rumbled through the Warden speakers. Dennis checked the elders' breach assignments; the reserve itself was suddenly less interesting than that challenge.

Lars entered the bridge channel before the argument could worsen.

He looked at the three elders in their assault-frames and said, very flatly:

“I did not authorize this.”

Mikael nodded.

“Then nobody can blame you.”

For one rare moment, Lars seemed to consider simply venting all three into space.

Instead he said, “Return to the embarkation deck.”

Bjorn answered, “No.”

Rasmus added, “The Wardens are already sealed.”

Mikael finished, “And if Ghar-Kul contains civilians, prisoners, or wounded, you will want us.”

That stopped the room.

Because he was right.

A Crimson Warden was not merely a killing machine. Under the Charter of Steel, its purpose was broader and more frightening. Break the door. Hold the corridor. Carry the wounded back through the breach. Recover the living. Then keep advancing.

Joel looked away first.

“Stay behind the first wave.”

All three elders clearly interpreted that as permission.

Anton was still grinning.

“Twenty seconds to torpedo range.”

The outer battle intensified. Garooth defense batteries hammered at the advancing fleet. Talyrn destroyers broke apart attack craft before they reached the Spearhound. House frigates died keeping the boarding lane open. Above the fortress, one dreadnought salvo cored an escort hulk from bow to engine.

“Launch.”

The boarding torpedoes fired.

Dozens first.

Then more.

Black armored shells screamed toward Ghar-Kul through fields of fire and debris. Some died. Most did not. They struck hangars, cargo scars, wounded armor belts, and breached three decks deep where the earlier bombardment had cracked the superstructure.

Then the Spearhound hit.

Not a ram.

A controlled, brutal docking impact.

Its armored wedge punched into a damaged outer spine and locked there with clamps the size of shuttle hulls.

The assault doors opened.

Dennis led Duskbringer into the breach.

Talyrn Thyrak followed in hard formation.

Joel moved with his brothers at the center wave.

Then the heavy footsteps came.

The Wardens.

Mikael.

Rasmus.

Bjorn.

Horned silhouettes of crimson-marked Jarn-steel advancing like walking fortresses through smoke and sparks.

The first Garooth countercharge hit them head-on.

Bjorn met it first.

His hammer took a brute off its feet and broke the deck beneath it. Mikael planted the great shield in the breach and held the entire corridor while Talyrn wounded were dragged behind him. Rasmus fired once, marking the command-gunner hidden above the loading gantry, then physically tore a jammed side hatch apart so trapped civilians could be pulled through by the second wave.

The Talyrn watching the feed understood at once.

These were not ceremonial elders.

These were Wardens.

And Wardens did not ask permission once the breach was open.

Joel raised his glaive.

“One bottle for the first command deck.”

Anton answered immediately:

“Two for first ravager.”

Dennis said:

“Bjorn’s reserve for the fortress core.”

Bjorn’s voice thundered through his Warden speakers.

“EARN IT.”

Then House, Hunter, Thyrak, and Wardens charged into Ghar-Kul.

## 10. The Old Machines Still Walk

The first breach became a wall of bodies.

Not House bodies.

Garooth.

Mikael Winge planted his Crimson Warden in the center of the corridor and simply stopped moving.

The siege shield locked against the deck.

Servos braced.

Jarn-steel plates took the first impacts.

Then the second.

Then sustained automatic fire powerful enough to tear ordinary powered armor apart.

The Warden did not move.

Behind Mikael, Talyrn medics dragged wounded through the breach while House marines expanded the secured zone.

A Thyrak officer stared at the huge horned machine.

“I thought your Seraph Hunters were your heavy assault units.”

Dennis answered over the combat channel.

“They are.”

The officer looked at Mikael.

“Then what is that?”

Dennis glanced toward the ancient frame.

“History.”

The Crimson Wardens had once been common wherever House von Hjertstedt expected the battlefield itself to become the enemy.

Collapsed stations.

Fortress breaches.

Urban fighting.

Hull interiors too confined for vehicles and too lethal for ordinary infantry.

For generations they had been the House answer to a simple problem:

If infantry could not survive the corridor—

send something that could.

But the House had moved on.

Modern Seraph Hunter armor was better suited to the wars Vintergard increasingly fought.

Smaller.

Faster.

Better isolated.

Far more advanced cognition protection.

Integrated anti-Seraph containment systems.

Superior neural authentication.

Null-projector architecture.

A Proctor-class Hunter could move through contaminated compartments, identify compromised personnel, fight Husk Guard, and maintain command integrity under threats the original Warden designers had never imagined.

So the Crimson Wardens had gradually disappeared from normal frontline service.

They were not obsolete.

They were worse than obsolete.

They were unnecessary.

Usually.

The frames remained in fortress arsenals on Skold.

Training halls.

Reserve depots.

Ceremonial readiness chambers.

Some were maintained because their owners refused to let them die.

And old Hjertstedt elders were exceptionally difficult people to convince that a perfectly functional siege engine should be retired merely because somebody had invented something newer.

Bjorn demonstrated the argument personally.

A Garooth breacher twice the mass of a normal human slammed into him with an industrial ram.

The impact shook the corridor.

Bjorn’s Warden moved backward perhaps twenty centimeters.

The Garooth looked confused.

Bjorn hit it with the hammer.

Once.

The creature disappeared into a cargo partition.

The wall behind it collapsed.

Bjorn looked through the new opening.

“Useful.”

Daniel heard him.

“You were not supposed to open that wall.”

“It is open now.”

“That is not the same thing.”

Rasmus laughed over comms.

Then his heavy rifle fired.

A Garooth gunner hidden three levels above Mikael’s position vanished.

“High nest clear.”

Christer immediately updated the tactical network.

“Good shot.”

Rasmus answered:

“I have been doing this since before you discovered women.”

Christer went silent.

Anton nearly choked laughing.

The real value of the Wardens appeared six minutes later.

A section of Ghar-Kul shifted under external bombardment.

The deck folded.

A Talyrn Thyrak and three conventional infantry disappeared beneath a collapsing structural beam.

Their squad stopped.

Mikael did not.

He turned from the firing line, handed the shield position to a Hunter, and walked into the collapsing section.

The Talyrn commander shouted:

“Structural failure!”

Mikael answered:

“Yes.”

The Warden stepped beneath the beam.

Both reinforced arms lifted.

The entire frame compressed under the weight.

Warnings screamed across its systems.

Hydraulics strained.

But the beam rose.

“Move them.”

Talyrn troops pulled the trapped soldiers free.

One Thyrak had a crushed leg.

Mikael released one hand from the beam, grabbed the augmented soldier by the armor harness, and carried him bodily out while supporting the structure with the other arm.

The Thyrak commander stared at the feed.

Dennis said quietly:

“That is why they still exist.”

A Seraph Hunter was a better predator.

A better anti-contamination platform.

A better command-integrity machine.

But a Crimson Warden had been designed in a simpler age around a simpler requirement:

Do not stop.

Bjorn and Rasmus joined Mikael at the next junction.

The three old frames formed a triangle around the casualty route.

House marines pushed outward.

Thyrak moved through them.

Hunters broke enemy concentrations ahead.

The Wardens stayed.

They took fire.

Held doors.

Lifted wreckage.

Dragged wounded.

Crushed anything that reached the recovery corridor.

The contrast became obvious.

The Seraph Hunters were spears.

They penetrated.

Purged.

Hunted.

The Crimson Wardens were anchors.

They made a piece of ground refuse to fall.

The Talyrn understood anchors.

Their entire military culture was built around refusing collapse.

Then a Garooth heavy mob hit the breach.

Dozens.

Powered armor.

Industrial weapons.

A massive armored brute leading them.

Mikael locked his shield again.

Rasmus braced his rifle over Mikael’s shoulder.

Bjorn rolled his hammer once in the Warden’s powered hand.

Anton arrived from the forward sector just in time to see them.

He stopped.

“Do you need help?”

Three elder voices answered simultaneously.

“No.”

Anton stared.

Then opened a private channel to Joel.

“They are being unreasonable.”

Joel was silent for several seconds.

Then:

“Do you understand the irony of that sentence?”

Anton considered.

“No.”

The Garooth charged.

Mikael absorbed the first impact.

Rasmus fired through the gaps.

Bjorn stepped around the shield and hit the leading brute so hard its armor folded inward.

The old machines advanced.

Slowly.

Not elegantly.

Not with the terrifying speed of modern Hunter armor.

But they advanced.

The Garooth line began moving backward.

One meter.

Then another.

Then another.

The Talyrn watching understood something important about House von Hjertstedt.

Its modern armor was frightening because it represented everything the House had learned.

The Crimson Wardens were frightening for another reason.

They represented what the House had already known for centuries.

Sometimes the battlefield did not need a better weapon.

Sometimes it needed something too stubborn to die.

## 11. The Door and the Axe

The prison decks were not marked as prisons.

Ghar-Kul called them storage.

Christer found the discrepancy first.

Whole sections of the fortress showed life-support demand far above what their declared industrial function required. Heat. Water. Waste processing. Emergency oxygen.

But almost no machinery load.

“Joel.”

A new layer appeared on the tactical map.

Seven compartments.

Then twelve.

Then twenty-three.

Daniel looked at the numbers.

“That is not crew.”

Joel understood immediately.

“Prisoners.”

Christer recalculated.

“Minimum estimate: eleven thousand.”

The channel went silent.

Then Mikael spoke from inside his Crimson Warden.

“Route.”

Joel transmitted it.

The three elders turned away from the command spine without another word.

Dennis saw their icons change direction.

“What are they doing?”

Joel answered:

“Winning their bet.”

The first prison deck had been welded shut.

Not locked.

Welded.

Mikael examined the door.

Bjorn raised his hammer.

Daniel immediately cut into the channel.

“Do not.”

Bjorn lowered it slightly.

“You keep saying that.”

Rasmus marked three load-bearing points.

Mikael drove the Warden’s reinforced fingers beneath the welded seam.

Servos screamed.

Jarn-steel groaned.

Talyrn.

Some military.

Most civilian.

Workers from lost stations.

Mining crews.

Families.

Prisoners taken from habitats weeks or months earlier.

A towering machine.

Crimson markings.

Armor scarred by gunfire.

A face of black steel beneath reinforced horns.

“Talyrn Continuance?”

Nobody answered.

Then a woman near the front slowly nodded.

“Your people are outside.”

The room changed.

Not cheering.

Not immediately.

Something more fragile.

Belief.

The Wardens stopped advancing.

That was what surprised the Talyrn commander.

Mikael established the evacuation corridor.

Rasmus took the adjoining junction and began killing anything that approached from the forge decks.

Bjorn moved into the prison compartments.

His giant frame was suddenly doing exactly what it had been built to do centuries earlier.

Lift collapsed bunks.

Rip restraints from walls.

Carry those who could not walk.

A small Talyrn child stared at the huge Warden.

Bjorn crouched.

The machine still towered over everyone even while kneeling.

“You injured?”

The child shook her head.

“Good.”

She pointed toward an unconscious man.

“My father.”

Bjorn turned.

The casualty claw opened.

“Then we take him too.”

He carried the man out.

The child rode in the armored crook of the other arm.

House marines watched him pass.

Nobody commented.

They liked living.

Joel updated the operation.

“Rescue priority.”

The joint assault changed instantly.

Talyrn units peeled away from secondary objectives.

Sanctuary medical craft moved toward the Spearhound.

Saervan tenders prepared mass-casualty reception.

House engineers began mapping atmosphere routes instead of demolishing them.

Eleven thousand became fourteen thousand.

Then seventeen.

Christer found additional compartments.

Ortheva’s voice tightened.

“How many?”

“Still counting.”

Mikael answered from the prison deck:

“Then stop counting and start receiving.”

Dennis and Anton had already gone the other direction.

The fortress command spine lay deeper inside Ghar-Kul, beyond three forge districts and a central war hall.

Dennis advanced with Duskbringer.

Anton moved beside him.

Neither man seemed interested in allowing the other to reach the ravager first.

“You are limping,” Anton said.

Dennis fired his incinerator into a Garooth gun position.

“No.”

“You are.”

“I am favoring a leg.”

“That is limping.”

“You were thrown through a reactor wall on our last campaign.”

“I recovered.”

“So did I.”

“You are still limping.”

Duskbringer’s squad channel filled with laughter.

Dennis killed the channel.

The first major ravager had prepared properly. His deep crimson skin showed between thick plates of stolen armour; he beat a fist against his chest and bared his teeth at the approaching shield line.

The war hall was enormous.

Captured banners hung from gantries.

Broken Talyrn armor decorated the walls.

Hundreds of Garooth warriors filled the chamber.

At the far end stood the ravager.

Massive.

Almost twice Anton’s height in armor.

Industrial plates had been bolted directly over its body. One arm ended in a powered cutting claw. The other carried a weapon somewhere between cannon and hammer.

Anton stopped.

Dennis stopped.

Both looked at it.

Anton said:

“Mine.”

Dennis answered:

“No.”

“I saw it first.”

“I breached the door.”

“I was through it first.”

“You shoved me.”

“That sounds like a personal problem.”

The ravager roared.

Then charged.

Dennis raised his cannon.

Anton moved clear of its line and drew his blade.

“Let me close.”

Dennis stared at him.

“What?”

“The bet.”

“What bet?”

“Ravager-class targets.”

“You never specified melee.”

“Then cover me.”

Dennis looked genuinely offended.

The ravager reached them.

Anton met it head-on.

The impact threw both of them across the deck.

Anton recovered first.

Barely.

The ravager swung the powered claw.

Anton ducked beneath it and drove his shoulder into the creature’s chest.

Armor cracked.

The ravager did not care.

It grabbed Anton and threw him through a tactical railing.

Dennis laughed.

Anton climbed out.

“Not helping.”

“I am enjoying this.”

The ravager turned toward Dennis.

Dennis stopped laughing.

“Oh.”

It charged him instead.

Dennis sidestepped, caught the weapon arm with both powered gauntlets, and redirected the strike into the deck.

Anton came from behind.

His blade entered beneath the ravager’s shoulder armor.

The creature roared and spun.

Dennis struck its knee.

Anton struck high.

Neither needed to speak.

The competition vanished for three seconds.

That was enough.

Dennis locked the claw arm.

Anton drove the blade through the ravager’s chest.

The creature sagged.

Dennis immediately drew his sidearm and fired into its head.

Anton stared.

“You cheated.”

Dennis holstered the weapon.

“It died.”

“I had it.”

“It was moving.”

“That still counts as mine.”

Dennis opened the command channel.

“Ravager neutralized. Joint kill.”

Anton shouted:

“NO.”

Far behind them, another prison door opened.

Then another.

Then another.

The evacuation tally passed twenty thousand.

Mikael’s Warden stood at the center of the corridor beneath continuous fire.

Rasmus covered the approach.

Bjorn carried two wounded adults at once.

Joel watched both halves of the operation.

The young breaking the enemy’s command.

The old carrying its prisoners home.

And understood why House von Hjertstedt had never truly retired the Crimson Wardens.

The Seraph Hunters had replaced them in the worst wars.

But some jobs were older than Seraphs.

Break the door.

Hold the corridor.

Recover whoever was still alive.

Then keep advancing.

## 12. The Last Warden Standing

Bjorn’s Crimson Warden had been built before most of the officers around him were born.

Some parts were older than their grandparents.

Its armor had been replaced.

Its joints rebuilt.

Its reactor core changed twice.

Its original control architecture had been upgraded so many times that Daniel once described it as “three centuries of mechanists arguing with each other.”

It had survived Skold.

Boarding wars.

Station collapses.

Seraph actions from before the modern Hunter formations existed.

And now—

inside Ghar-Kul—

it began to die.

The first warning was minor.

LEFT KNEE SERVO DEGRADATION.

Bjorn ignored it.

The second was worse.

HYDRAULIC PRESSURE LOSS — RIGHT ARM.

He shifted the unconscious Talyrn prisoner he was carrying into the other arm.

Then came the reactor warning.

THERMAL MANAGEMENT FAILURE.

Daniel saw it immediately.

“Bjorn.”

“No.”

“I have not even said anything yet.”

“You were going to tell me to leave.”

“Yes.”

“Then no.”

The prison count had passed twenty-three thousand.

And still rising.

Entire sections of Ghar-Kul had been used as holding pens.

Talyrn industrial workers.

Captured military personnel.

Station crews.

Families taken during previous Garooth attacks.

Some had been kept alive because they understood machinery.

Others because the Garooth intended to use them as labor farther inside the migrating front.

Joel had redirected nearly a quarter of the boarding force toward evacuation.

The problem was simple.

The breach corridor was becoming overcrowded.

The Garooth knew it.

They began attacking it deliberately.

Mikael and Rasmus held the primary junction.

Bjorn moved between the prison decks and the evacuation route.

Again.

Again.

Again.

Every trip his Warden became slower.

The old machine could survive terrible damage.

It could not survive forever.

Bjorn reached Prison Block Nine when his left leg finally failed.

The Warden dropped to one knee.

The impact cracked the deck.

A Talyrn woman screamed.

Bjorn forced the frame upright using the wall.

“Still functional.”

Daniel’s voice came immediately.

“No.”

“Walking.”

“Barely.”

“Walking is functional.”

“You have twenty-eight percent hydraulic pressure.”

“Enough.”

“Your reactor cooling is degrading.”

“Then I will walk faster.”

Daniel stopped for half a second.

“THAT IS NOT HOW COOLING WORKS.”

Anton cut into the channel.

“Let him try.”

Joel immediately said:

“Anton.”

“I am encouraging morale.”

Bjorn reached the next sealed compartment.

The door controls were dead.

Normally he would have used the hammer.

He looked at the damaged right arm.

Then at the civilians behind the reinforced observation slit.

He dropped the hammer.

Both Warden hands entered the damaged door seam.

The frame pulled.

Warning lights flooded his helm.

Servos screamed.

The right shoulder actuator failed completely.

The door moved six centimeters.

Not enough.

Bjorn roared and pulled again.

Something snapped inside the Warden.

The door tore open.

Talyrn prisoners poured through.

Bjorn leaned against the wall.

His external speaker crackled.

“Move.”

They moved.

Nobody needed to be told twice.

The Thyrak commander reached him with a recovery squad.

“We have them. You evacuate.”

Bjorn looked at him.

“How many compartments remain?”

“Three on this level.”

“Then no.”

“Your machine is failing.”

Bjorn looked toward the horned armor around him.

“This machine has been failing since before your grandfather was born.”

“That is not reassuring.”

“It was not intended to be.”

The Thyrak commander stepped closer.

“We can complete the recovery.”

Bjorn looked at the Talyrn soldiers around him.

Then at the wounded they were carrying.

“I know.”

That surprised the commander.

“Then why remain?”

Bjorn looked down the corridor.

“Because you should not have to hold this alone while I can still stand.”

The Thyrak commander went quiet.

Strength creates obligation.

He knew the words.

Bjorn had never been Talyrn.

He did not need to be.

Then the Garooth hit Prison Junction Nine.

Hard.

A heavy mob broke through a maintenance wall behind the evacuation column.

Too close.

Talyrn troops turned.

House marines fired.

There were civilians in the line.

No clean field of fire.

Bjorn moved.

The damaged Warden stepped into the corridor.

Its left leg dragged.

One arm hung almost dead.

Reactor temperature climbed.

He picked up the dropped hammer with his remaining functional hand.

“Get them out.”

The Thyrak commander stared.

“You cannot hold that.”

Bjorn planted himself between the Garooth and the prisoners.

“Then be quick.”

The first attacker struck.

Bjorn killed it.

The second hit his damaged shoulder.

Armor split.

The third struck the knee.

The Warden nearly fell.

Bjorn caught himself against the wall and swung the hammer sideways.

Two attackers disappeared.

Rasmus’s rifle began firing from the far junction.

Mikael arrived behind him.

His own Warden shield slammed down beside Bjorn.

“You always were dramatic.”

Bjorn’s voice crackled.

“You came.”

“Someone has to explain this to Lars.”

Then House marines appeared.

Then Thyrak.

Then Duskbringer Hunters returning from the forward assault.

The Garooth suddenly discovered it had attacked the wrong corridor.

Dennis arrived last.

He looked at Bjorn’s failing Warden.

“You look terrible.”

Bjorn answered:

“You are still limping.”

Dennis raised his cannon.

“Fair.”

The corridor disappeared beneath combined fire.

Daniel reached Bjorn after the last prisoner left Block Nine.

He physically climbed onto the Warden’s damaged torso and opened an emergency maintenance panel.

“You are done.”

Bjorn tried to move.

The left leg locked.

He tried again.

Nothing.

Bjorn looked at the controls.

Then at Daniel.

“Temporary.”

Daniel inspected the readings.

“No.”

Bjorn was silent.

Daniel softened slightly.

“You got them out.”

Twenty-seven thousand prisoners now crowded the evacuation network.

More were still being found.

But Block Nine was empty.

No living person remained behind Bjorn’s position.

The old man finally leaned back inside the frame.

“Then the Warden did its job.”

The Thyrak commander stood below him.

He looked at the battered Crimson Warden.

Cracked armor.

Dead servos.

Burned crimson markings.

Horned helm blackened by fire.

And thousands of people alive behind it.

He understood why Vintergard had never entirely discarded the old machines.

The Seraph Hunters were better armor.

More advanced.

More dangerous.

But the Crimson Warden had never been built to be the best weapon.

It had been built to remain when leaving would mean somebody else did not come home.

## 13. The Weight They Carried

Daniel disconnected Bjorn's controls before the old man could try them again.

The Warden's left leg remained locked against the deck. Its right shoulder hung below its proper position. Heat ticked through the plates around the reactor compartment.

Beyond the junction, the last prisoners from Block Nine were still moving toward the breach.

“Open the harness,” Daniel said.

Bjorn looked at the dead displays.

“You can repair it.”

“I can get you out of it.”

“That was not what I said.”

Daniel pulled the emergency release. Nothing happened. He checked the mechanism, took a cutting tool from his belt, and began opening the housing by hand.

For once, Bjorn let him work.

The Thyrak commander stood below the frame with a recovery team. Two of his soldiers were watching the dark maintenance opening through which the Garooth had attacked. Another laid a reinforced stretcher beside the Warden.

“We will carry the pilot,” he said.

Bjorn's eyes moved toward him.

“And the frame?”

“After the living.”

The old man considered that.

“Good answer.”

A shot struck the wall beyond Mikael's shield. Rasmus answered from the junction, his rifle's report heavy enough to vibrate through Daniel's tools.

The battle had not ended because they had stopped to rescue someone.

Joel arrived as the torso harness finally opened. He helped the Thyrak ease Bjorn forward while Daniel disengaged the remaining connections. Outside the frame, the elder looked suddenly smaller. His leg would not take his weight.

The medic caught the movement before he could conceal it.

“On the stretcher.”

“I can—”

“You have already made that argument to the machinery.”

Bjorn looked at Daniel.

Daniel kept working.

“I like this medic.”

They laid Bjorn down. Joel checked the route on his display. The main passage to the Spearhound was secure, but every casualty team arriving there was adding to the congestion. Wounded soldiers occupied the wall recesses. Civilians waited in lines that bent around corners and disappeared into smoke.

The tally had passed twenty-seven thousand. The figure described people found, not people safely aboard transports.

“Separate the queues,” Joel ordered. “Walking evacuees to the cargo passage. Critical casualties keep the main spine. Nobody changes route until both ends confirm.”

Christer answered from farther forward.

“Cargo passage still has hostile access from above.”

Joel looked toward Dennis.

“Can you close it?”

Dennis checked the map before answering.

“Yes.”

“With the passage intact.”

“I heard the first order.”

Duskbringer moved. The Thyrak commander sent soldiers behind them with lamps, atmosphere monitors and casualty slings. Within minutes, a green route extended across Joel's display. It stopped at each junction until a team physically reached it.

Then the first civilians began moving through.

A woman halted beside the stretcher. Joel recognized her from the Block Nine feed. She held a child's wrist in one hand and a strip of cloth covered in names in the other.

“There were people working below us,” she said.

The Thyrak commander turned.

“Which level?”

“I don't know the level. They took them down through a lift with a broken yellow light. We heard them at shift change.”

She held out the cloth.

“These are the ones I remember.”

Joel sent the description to Christer. The route on his display acquired a new question mark.

“We will check,” he said.

“They will not be on your list.”

“Then we need another list.”

She studied him for a moment before allowing the recovery team to guide her away.

Daniel looked up from the Warden.

“Reactor isolated. It can wait now.”

Joel marked its position for salvage, beside the casualty route rather than over it. The frame would not obstruct another stretcher.

Bjorn caught his sleeve.

“Do not lose the hammer.”

Joel secured it to the recovery sled beside him.

“Anything else?”

The elder looked toward the passage the woman had described.

“The workers.”

“We heard her.”

Bjorn released him.

The stretcher moved toward the Spearhound. Mikael walked alongside it until the next secure junction, then returned to his shield position. Rasmus remained above the corridor, counting people past him between shots.

Joel watched the elder disappear behind a line of walking wounded.

Christer's voice returned.

“Found the lift. There is power draw beneath it. Local controls only.”

Daniel sealed his tool case.

“I'll go.”

“Take engineers.”

“I was planning to.”

“And an escort.”

Daniel looked at the bullet marks around them.

“That too.”

Joel reopened the command channel.

“Prison sweep continues. Command assault holds at the next junction until Daniel identifies what their controls can shut down.”

Anton answered immediately.

“We can see the command doors.”

“Then watch them.”

There was a pause.

“Understood.”

Joel turned toward the next lift. Behind him, the evacuation was finally moving faster than the wounded were arriving.

It was the first measure of progress he trusted.

## 14. The Forge Falls Silent

Ghar-Kul's command chamber stood above a working foundry.

Anton could hear it through the doors: hammers, furnaces, heavy carriers moving along rails. Even with boarding parties inside its walls, the fortress was still manufacturing weapons.

Dennis joined him at the last covered junction.

“You waited.”

Anton checked the charge on his sidearm.

“I am capable of it.”

“We should record the occasion.”

Neither man moved toward the door.

On the shared display, Daniel's team had reached the lift below the prison decks. Their cameras showed a low industrial compartment crowded with work benches and chained machinery. Human workers lay beneath them or stood against the walls with their hands raised.

House soldiers lowered their weapons first.

Daniel found the controls behind a cage of welded bars. A Talyrn engineer leaned over his shoulder while an older prisoner pointed out the connections.

“That one feeds the furnaces,” the prisoner said. “That one opens the cooling vents.”

Daniel followed the cable toward the ceiling.

“And the doors?”

The man pointed to a separate lever bank.

No network. No convenient central system to seize. Each section had been connected to the next by whoever had survived long enough to build it.

Daniel opened his tools.

“Joel. They can vent these workshops from command. I need time to cut that connection without losing air circulation.”

Joel asked how much.

Daniel looked at the engineer.

“I will tell you when it is done.”

At the command doors, Anton heard the answer.

“Still watching,” he said.

Outside, the joint fleet was paying for that time.

Ghar-Kul's engines had begun turning the fortress toward a gap in its surviving escorts. It moved slowly, but the Spearhound was still clamped into its outer structure. A violent course change could tear the boarding vessel from the breach with thousands of people waiting behind it.

Lars studied the thrust pattern beside Ortheva.

“Can your ships hold that side?” he asked.

She moved two Talyrn groups on the plot.

“If your heavy batteries stop firing through their approach.”

Lars issued the correction. House guns shifted targets. Talyrn ships crossed the cleared lane and struck the fortress's exposed maneuvering assemblies.

One died. Another kept firing through its own damaged screen.

The turn slowed.

“Spearhound,” Ortheva said. “You have your alignment. Use it.”

Inside the fortress, transport crews opened another casualty reception bay.

Daniel worked with both hands inside the control cage. The prisoner held a lamp for him. When the emergency ventilation stopped, all three men froze.

Then the Talyrn engineer brought the local supply online.

Air stirred against Daniel's cheek.

He checked the workshop pressure. Steady. The adjoining compartments answered one at a time.

“Command cannot vent the occupied levels,” he reported. “Local support is holding. We have people at the manual controls.”

Joel looked at Christer.

“The rest?”

“Secondary command junctions ready. No evidence of a single self-destruct system. Several demolition circuits. Teams are isolating them.”

“Then we take command.”

The order reached Anton before the foundry completed another hammer stroke.

He stood.

“Finally.”

The first charge opened the lower doors. Dennis's shield teams entered under fire. Talyrn Thyrak moved along the side galleries, closing the exits before defenders could reach the routes behind them.

The High Ravager stood on the central platform beneath a forest of stolen control panels. Dark brick-red skin bunched around his jaw as he shouted for the furnace crews to feed the guns. Its armor carried pieces of different ships. One shoulder bore the scorched markings of a Talyrn rescue craft.

It dragged a gun into position and fired across the breach.

A Hunter fell. The formation closed around him while a Thyrak pair pulled him out. Dennis struck the gun mounting with a controlled burst. The weapon slewed away and emptied itself into the foundry roof.

Anton went up the right stair.

The High Ravager met him halfway.

Its first blow took the railing from the platform. Anton avoided the edge, drove his blade under its raised arm, and felt it catch against a second layer of plate.

The creature turned on him.

Dennis hit from the other side.

No argument this time. No demand for a private duel. The High Ravager was standing between them and a control board covered in red switches.

It caught Dennis across the chest and drove him backward. Anton fired into the opening beneath its arm. Dennis recovered his footing, trapped the weapon against the broken railing and held it there.

Anton struck again.

The High Ravager went down across the stairs.

On the far platform, another defender reached for the control bank. A Thyrak shot took it away from the panel before its hand closed.

Christer's teams entered through the upper service access.

“Leave the switches,” he ordered. “Kill the transmission units first.”

A specialist pulled the physical command links. Outside, the fortress's escort traffic fractured into competing calls.

Some ships continued attacking.

Some turned away.

The joint fleet pursued the armed groups that still threatened the evacuation, leaving recovery ships clear passage through the wreckage.

Daniel reached the foundry controls a few minutes later. He did not try to make the machinery work for him. He stopped it in sections, waiting for each furnace to enter a stable condition before shutting the next feed.

The great hammer beneath the command chamber struck once more.

Then remained raised.

The silence revealed things the machinery had concealed.

Distant gunfire. Pressure alarms. Someone calling for a medic from the lower stairs.

Anton stood beside the dead High Ravager, breathing hard.

Dennis checked the casualty feed.

“The Hunter?”

“Alive,” the Thyrak commander answered. “Moving him now.”

Dennis lowered his weapon.

Joel entered the captured chamber. Christer gave him the control status while Daniel argued with an engineer over a cooling pump that could not yet be switched off.

Command was theirs. The engine orders had stopped. The surviving escorts no longer acted as a fleet.

But the map still contained red compartments and unanswered doors.

“Command seized,” Joel reported to Lars. “Ghar-Kul is no longer moving under hostile control. We need relief boarders, engineers and more medical transport.”

Ortheva answered before Lars.

“They are coming.”

Joel looked down into the silent foundry. Workers were emerging from a side passage, guided by the same prisoner who had held Daniel's lamp.

“Continue the sweep,” he said. “Every occupied section gets checked by people. We do not finish this by trusting their records.”

The prisoner stopped below the platform and looked at the dead machinery.

He had worked beneath it for so long that he seemed uncertain whether he was allowed to leave.

Daniel went down to meet him.

## 15. The Living First

They were still finding people three days after the command chamber fell.

Workers behind sealed cooling ducts. Families in storage sections omitted from the fortress's crude maps. Wounded Talyrn soldiers who had survived by keeping machines running for their captors.

Every discovery changed the evacuation plan.

Ghar-Kul could no longer build or direct a fleet, but parts of it remained dangerous. Armed mobs held isolated decks. Unstable magazines prevented whole sections from being entered. Engineering teams marked safe approaches, then withdrew when their measurements changed.

Joel signed the casualty transfer orders from a bench beside the Spearhound's medical bay.

His armor had been removed. A dressing covered a cut along his forearm. He had slept, though not enough for the medic checking his eyes.

“You can finish that later,” she said.

“The transport leaves in twenty minutes.”

She read the order over his shoulder.

Then moved the water cup into his free hand.

“Drink while you do it.”

Across the bay, Bjorn watched a recovery feed.

His Warden was coming aboard on an industrial cradle.

It had taken two tugs, a cut through an empty cargo wall and most of a Talyrn engineering shift to get it out without blocking the living. Mikael's frame walked beside it. Rasmus's rifle was secured for the first time since the breach.

Daniel stood behind the cradle with a damage slate.

“The leg is finished,” he told Bjorn. “So is most of the shoulder. I will not know about the main structure until we open it in a proper yard.”

“But you brought it.”

“The Thyrak brought it. I complained about the route.”

The commander arrived in time to hear that.

“He complained about several routes.”

Bjorn reached toward the feed, then let his hand rest on the blanket.

“Thank you.”

The commander nodded. No one made a joke to cover it.

Later, in the joint operations room, Ortheva laid a list of missing Talyrn habitats beside the map of Ghar-Kul.

Some of the fortress's modules still carried their original numbers beneath the welded armor.

“These belong to people,” she said.

Joel studied the list.

“Then the recovery records need their claims attached.”

“Your ships captured them.”

“Your people lived in them.”

Annette joined the conference from Ardent. She asked for copies of the hull identifiers, survivor testimony and engineering surveys. Until ownership could be established, salvage crews would be paid for recovery work rather than rewarded by taking possession of whatever they cut free.

Some officers disliked the additional work.

The former prisoners did not.

The foundry core remained secured under joint guard. It could not be made safe by declaring it valuable. Fuel feeds had been improvised, pressure systems crossed and safety controls removed. Sections that could be recovered would go to Talyrn yards. Sections that could not would be cleared of people and evidence before controlled demolition.

There would be no single triumphant tow home.

Ghar-Kul had been built from many stolen things. Dismantling it would take longer than defeating it.

Lars widened the war map.

Beyond their new line, other Garooth formations remained. Some were moving away from the wreck of the convergence. Others were gathering around surviving supply bases.

“They lost this offensive,” Ortheva said. “They have not lost the ability to begin another.”

“Then the defense stays,” Lars answered.

They agreed the relief in detail. House formations would remain beside Talyrn forces. Damaged hulls would rotate only after replacements reached their stations. Medical and engineering support would continue through Gate Seventeen under joint authorization.

The gate's restricted access did not change.

Neither side received a free road through the other's defenses.

Lars transferred routine command of the House contingent to the senior Banner officers already serving in the joint formation. Ortheva retained Talyrn authority. Their common operations staff stayed in place.

Joel looked at the signatures gathering beneath the order.

For the first time, leaving would not mean abandoning the line.

The last occupied prison compartment was opened on the sixth day. Its people joined the protected transfers while fresh teams checked the cleared decks against the accounts of the survivors. The final organized resistance inside the fortress had broken; the remaining work belonged to recovery teams and engineers operating behind secured boundaries.

Before the command group departed, Joel received confirmation that the recovered prisoners had reached medical ships or Talyrn reception facilities. Their names would take longer to reconcile. Their transport records no longer ended at a prison door.

The farewell took place beside a transport rather than in a ceremonial hall.

A recovered worker recognized Daniel and brought him a lamp. Its casing was dented. Someone had cleaned the blood from the handle.

“It still works,” the man said.

Daniel switched it on.

“So it does.”

He accepted it with both hands.

Bjorn left on a medical transport. His Warden followed separately, still braced on the cradle. The Spearhound remained for repairs before its crew could bring it back through the gate.

Anton watched them prepare the last passenger collar.

“We never settled the bet.”

Joel looked toward the survivors boarding across the apron.

“You can ask Bjorn.”

Anton considered the elder's stretcher, the broken frame and the Thyrak walking beside both.

“Later.”

From the stretcher, Bjorn opened one eye.

“Bring the witnesses. And something fit to eat.”

Anton looked back at him.

“You intend to argue?”

“I intend to win sitting down.”

The Thyrak commander adjusted the blanket clear of the stretcher rail. Anton helped with the other side before returning to the collar.

Ortheva shook Joel's hand before he entered the transport.

“You will come back.”

He glanced at the duty roster they had signed.

“We already have people staying.”

“I meant you.”

“Yes.”

That answer satisfied her.

Behind the departing ships, salvage lamps moved through the dark shape of Ghar-Kul. Farther out, House and Talyrn patrols turned together to meet the next watch.
